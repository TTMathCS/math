One square takes 4 toothpicks. Two squares take 7. Three squares take 10.

How many toothpicks does she need for a row of **10 squares**? Maya says "4 for each square, so 40". Is she right?

## The idea

### Count them, and look for the rule

Count the toothpicks in each shape:

- Shape 1 (one square): **4** toothpicks.
- Shape 2 (two squares): **7** toothpicks.
- Shape 3 (three squares): **10** toothpicks.
- Shape 4 (four squares): **13** toothpicks.

So the numbers are 4, 7, 10, 13. The gaps are +3, +3, +3. The rule in words is: **start at 4 and add 3 each time**.

That already shows Maya is wrong. If each square took 4, the gaps would be +4. Each new square only adds **3**. Why?

Look at the colours. The first square (green) uses 4 toothpicks. Every square after that is built onto the side of
the one before. The side it shares is **already there**. So each new square needs only a top, a bottom and one new
side: **3 toothpicks**.

So a row of 10 squares is:

- 4 toothpicks for the first square,
- and then 9 more squares (the 2nd to the 10th), each adding 3.

9 threes is 27. Then 4 + 27 = **31 toothpicks**.

Maya said 40 because she forgot that the squares **share** sides. 10 squares do have 40 sides between them, but
9 of those are shared (one between each pair of neighbours), and a shared toothpick counts once, not twice.
40 − 9 = 31. ✓

Sort the toothpicks by where they are:

- **Tops** (orange): one for each square.
- **Bottoms** (blue): one for each square.
- **Uprights** (green): look closely. For 1 square there are 2 uprights. For 2 squares there are 3. For 3 squares
  there are 4. There is always **one more upright than squares**, like posts in a fence.

For 10 squares: 10 tops, 10 bottoms and 11 uprights. 10 + 10 + 11 = **31**.

### Why two ways are better than one

The two ways look completely different, but they gave the **same answer**: 31. That is not luck. Both ways count the
**same toothpicks**, just sorted into different piles. If you count a pile of toothpicks carefully, it does not matter
how you group them; you must get the same total.

So when two different ways agree, you can be very sure you are right. When they disagree, one of them has a mistake,
and you know to look again. Mathematicians love counting things in two ways for exactly this reason.

### A table

A good way to keep track of a growing shape is a **table**. The top row is the **shape number**. The bottom row is how
many **toothpicks** it takes.

| Shape number | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Toothpicks | 4 | 7 | 10 | 13 | 16 | 19 |

Reading **across** the bottom row, each number is 3 more than the one before. That is the "start at 4 and add 3"
rule.

But the table also lets you go **down**, from a shape number straight to its toothpicks, using way two: shape 6 is
6 tops, 6 bottoms and 7 uprights, so 6 + 6 + 7 = 19. ✓ You do not have to work through shapes 1 to 5 first. That is a
big help for faraway shapes like shape 50 (50 + 50 + 51 = 151).

### Working backwards

Can a row of squares use exactly **25** toothpicks? How many squares?

Use way one. The first square takes 4, leaving 25 − 4 = 21 toothpicks. Each new square takes 3, and 21 is seven 3s.
So there are 7 new squares after the first one: **8 squares** in all.

Check with way two: 8 tops, 8 bottoms, 9 uprights. 8 + 8 + 9 = 25. ✓

What about **20** toothpicks? 20 − 4 = 16, and 16 is not a whole number of 3s (five 3s is 15, six 3s is 18). So
there is **no** shape with exactly 20 toothpicks: you would have 1 left over. The numbers in this pattern are only
4, 7, 10, 13, 16, 19, 22, 25, ...

## Worked examples

**Example 1. Shape 7 of the squares, two ways.**

1. Way one: 4 for the first square, then 6 more squares at 3 each. 6 threes is 18. 4 + 18 = **22**.
2. Way two: 7 tops, 7 bottoms, 8 uprights. 7 + 7 + 8 = **22**.
3. They agree. ✓

**Example 2. Fill in the table.** Complete the table for the triangles.

| Shape number | 1 | 2 | 3 | 6 | 8 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Toothpicks | 3 | 5 | 7 | ? | ? |

1. Each triangle has 1 orange and 1 blue, plus 1 green at the start.
2. Shape 6: 6 + 6 + 1 = **13**.
3. Shape 8: 8 + 8 + 1 = **17**.
4. Check by adding 2s from shape 3: 7, 9 (shape 4), 11 (shape 5), 13 (shape 6) ✓, 15 (shape 7), 17 (shape 8) ✓.

**Example 3. Which shape has 40 toothpicks?** (Squares in a row.)

1. Take away the first square: 40 − 4 = 36.
2. 36 is twelve 3s. So 12 more squares.
3. **13 squares.** Check: 13 + 13 + 14 = 40. ✓

**Example 4. A two-row block.** Squares stacked in **two rows**, two squares tall, growing to the right. Shape 1 is a
column of 2 squares, which takes 7 toothpicks (3 across: top, middle, bottom; and 2 on the left and 2 on the right).
Each new column adds 3 across and 2 up: 5 more. So: 7, 12, 17, 22, ... Shape 4 has **22** toothpicks.

## Try it

1. How many toothpicks for a row of 5 squares? Show it two ways.
2. How many toothpicks for a row of 12 squares?
3. Copy and complete the table for squares in a row: shape 1, 2, 3, 8, 10.
4. Can a row of squares use exactly 31 toothpicks? 33 toothpicks? Explain.
5. How many toothpicks for 7 triangles in a row?
6. Which triangle shape uses 31 toothpicks?
7. A row of **pentagons** (five-sided shapes) shares one side between neighbours. The first pentagon takes 5
   toothpicks. How many does each new one add? How many for 4 pentagons?
8. Maya now says: "Each square has 4 sides, and 10 squares share 9 sides, so it is 40 − 9." Explain why this is right.

??? note "Answers"
    1. Way one: 4 + 3 + 3 + 3 + 3 = **16**. Way two: 5 tops, 5 bottoms, 6 uprights: 5 + 5 + 6 = **16**.
    2. 12 tops, 12 bottoms, 13 uprights: 12 + 12 + 13 = **37**. (Way one: 4 + 11 threes = 4 + 33 = 37.)
    3. Shape 1: 4. Shape 2: 7. Shape 3: 10. Shape 8: 8 + 8 + 9 = **25**. Shape 10: 10 + 10 + 11 = **31**.
    4. **31: yes**, it is shape 10 (31 − 4 = 27 = nine 3s, so 1 + 9 = 10 squares). **33: no**. 33 − 4 = 29, and 29 is
       not a whole number of 3s (nine 3s is 27, ten 3s is 30).
    5. 7 orange, 7 blue, 1 green: **15**. (Or 3, 5, 7, 9, 11, 13, 15.)
    6. Take away the 1 green: 30. Half of 30 is 15 orange and 15 blue. So **shape 15**.
    7. Each new pentagon shares one side, so it adds 5 − 1 = **4**. 4 pentagons: 5 + 4 + 4 + 4 = **17**.
    8. 10 squares have 10 × 4 = 40 sides if they are all separate. But each pair of neighbours shares one toothpick,
       and 10 squares in a row have 9 pairs of neighbours. Each shared toothpick was counted twice, so take 9 away:
       31. This is a **third way** to count, and it agrees with the other two!

## Common mistake

**Counting shared sides twice.** "4 toothpicks per square" forgets that neighbours share a side. Build the shape, or
draw it, and check your rule on shape 2 before you trust it.

**Off by one in the uprights.** 10 squares need 11 uprights, not 10. There is always one more post than gaps.
