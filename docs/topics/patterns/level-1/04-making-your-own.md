# Making your own pattern

*Patterns and sequences · level 1, lesson 4 of 6*

## A question to start

Lily made a pattern for her friend Omar to guess.

"What comes next?" she asked. Omar said "orange". Lily said "No! It is green!" Omar said "That's not fair!"

Who is right? Can Omar be sure what comes next?

Think about it. Omar thought the core was *green, orange, orange*. Then the 4th bead (green) starts the core again,
and the next bead is orange. That is a sensible guess! But Lily's core was really *green, orange, orange, green*,
4 beads long, so the next bead starts a new core: green. Both rules fit the four beads Omar could see. He could not
know which one Lily meant, because she showed her core only **once**. In this lesson you will learn how to make a pattern that is fun, and also **fair**.

## The idea

### Three steps to make a pattern

Making a repeating pattern takes three steps.

1. **Choose your things.** Beads, blocks, stickers, claps, colours, shapes, letters, toys. Two or three kinds is
   plenty.
2. **Choose your core.** Decide what order the things go in, and how long the core is.
3. **Repeat the core at least three times.** Say it, build it, check it.

Here the things are blue squares and green triangles. The core is *square, square, triangle*. It is repeated three

### Why three times?

If you show your core only once, nobody can tell it is a core. It could be the start of anything. That is what went
wrong for Lily.

If you show it twice, your friend can **guess** the rule. If you show it three times, your friend can **check** the
guess: "It went square, square, triangle, and again, and again. I am sure now."

This is a good rule for all of mathematics, not only for beads. When you think you see a pattern, look at more
examples before you trust it. Seeing something once is a guess. Seeing it come back again and again is evidence.

There are **6**. (Orange, orange, orange and blue, blue, blue use only one colour, so we left them out.) The letter

orange* are both AAB. They are the same pattern with the colours swapped.

So with two colours and a core of 3, there are only **3 different pattern shapes**: AAB, ABA, ABB.

### A surprise: the same row, starting in a different place

Look at the ABA core: *orange, blue, orange*. Repeat it:

> orange, blue, orange, orange, blue, orange, orange, blue, orange

Now look closely at the middle of that row. Do you see *orange, orange, blue, orange, orange, blue*? It is the AAB
pattern hiding inside!

The bottom row is the ABA pattern, moved along one place. From the second bead on, it is exactly the same as the top
row, the AAB pattern. A long string of repeating beads does not really have a "start". If you made it into a
bracelet (a circle), you could not even tell where the core begins. The rule is the same; only the starting place
changes.

That is why mathematicians care most about the **core** and its **length**, more than where you start.

### Making a pattern fair, and making it tricky

A good puzzle is **tricky but fair**. Here is how.

**To make it fair:**

- Show the core at least three times.
- Use beads that are easy to tell apart.
- Make sure every core is exactly the same. Check it before you show anyone!

**To make it trickier (but still fair):**

- Use a longer core. *Red, red, blue, green* (4 long) is harder than *red, blue* (2 long).
- Use the same thing more than once in the core, in different places: *red, blue, red, red* (ABAA).
- Stop in the **middle** of a core, so the next bead is not the first one.
- Use two kinds of change at once: a pattern of **colours** and **sizes**, like big red, small red, big blue, small
  blue.

## Worked examples

**Example 1. Make a fair AAB pattern with stickers.** Use stars and hearts.

1. Things: stars and hearts.
2. Core: AAB, so *star, star, heart*.
3. Repeat three times: star, star, heart, star, star, heart, star, star, heart.
4. Check each core: star, star, heart ✓ ✓ ✓.

Your friend sees *star, star, heart* three times and can be sure that a star comes next.

**Example 2. Fix an unfair pattern.** Tom showed: red, red, blue, green, red. "What comes next?" Is it fair?

1. Tom has shown red, red, blue, green, then red. Maybe the core is *red, red, blue, green*, and the next bead is
   red. But we have only seen that core once, plus one bead.
2. It is **not fair** yet. To make it fair, Tom should show
   *red, red, blue, green, red, red, blue, green, red, red, blue, green*: the core three times. Then the next bead is
   surely red.

**Example 3. All the cores 2 long.** You have a red block and a yellow block (and lots more of each). Which cores of
length 2 use both colours?

1. First block red or yellow; second block red or yellow.
2. Red, yellow ✓. Yellow, red ✓. (Red, red and yellow, yellow use only one colour.)
3. So there are **2 cores**: *red, yellow* and *yellow, red*. Both are AB. Repeat either one, and you get the same
   long row, just starting in a different place.

**Example 4. A two-change pattern.** Make a pattern that changes **colour** and **size**.

1. Things: big and small circles, orange and blue.
2. Core: big orange, small orange, big blue, small blue.
3. Repeat: big orange, small orange, big blue, small blue, big orange, small orange, big blue, small blue, big orange,
   small orange, big blue, small blue.
4. Look at the sizes alone: big, small, big, small... an AB pattern. Look at the colours alone: orange, orange, blue,
   blue... an AABB pattern. Two patterns are going on at the same time!

## Try it

1. Make an ABC pattern with three kinds of fruit. Show the core three times.
2. Make an ABB pattern with claps and stamps. Say it out loud.
3. Sara showed: cat, dog, cat. "What comes next?" Explain why her friend cannot be sure.
4. Lists of beads 3 long with two colours: we found 6 that use both colours. Using **green** and **orange**, write all
   6.
5. Make a pattern that is 4 long and uses three colours.
6. Look at the chessboard quilt. What colour is the square in the 4th row and the 6th column? (Count rows from the
   top and columns from the left.)
7. Make a pattern that stops in the middle of a core. Ask: what comes next?
8. Here is a bracelet (a circle) of beads: orange, blue, blue, orange, blue, blue, and then back to the start. Mo says
   the core is *orange, blue, blue*. Jen says the core is *blue, blue, orange*. Who is right?

## Common mistake

**Showing the core only once.** Then it is a guess, not a pattern your friend can work out. Repeat it at least three
times.

**Making a mistake in your own pattern.** When you build a long pattern, it is easy to slip. Say the core out loud as
you build, and check every core at the end (like in [Finding the mistake](03-finding-mistake.md)).
