Can you find the bead in the wrong place? How did you know?

Most people find it very fast. That is because your brain **expects** the pattern to go on. When it does not, the
wrong bead jumps out at you, like a wrong note in a song you know well. In this lesson you will learn to find mistakes
carefully, every time, even in long and tricky patterns, and to **explain** how you fixed them.

## The idea

### A mistake is a place where the rule breaks

A repeating pattern has a rule: *say the core again and again*. A **mistake** is a place where the beads do not follow
the rule.

In Leo's bracelet, the core is *orange, blue*. Let us number the beads and say the core along with them: orange (1),
blue (2), orange (3), blue (4), orange (5), **blue** (6)... but bead 6 is orange!

So bead 6 is the mistake. To fix it, change it to **blue**. Then every bead follows the rule.

### Explain the fix with "the core is ..."

When you fix a pattern, it is not enough to say "this one is wrong". Say **why**. A good explanation has two parts:

1. **The core is** *orange, blue*.
2. So bead 6 should be **blue**, but it is orange. I changed it to blue.

Here is why the first part matters. Someone else might think the core is something different. If you say what you
think the core is, they can check your thinking. Mathematicians always explain their reasons, so other people can
check them. That is a big part of what makes mathematics trustworthy.

### How to check a long pattern: say the core out loud

A short pattern you can check by looking. A long pattern needs a careful way. Here is one that always works.

1. **Find the core** from the start of the pattern. Look at the first few beads and find the part that repeats.
2. **Say the core out loud, again and again**, and touch each bead as you say it.
3. **Stop** when what you say does not match the bead you touch. That is the mistake.

### Three kinds of mistake

Not every mistake is a wrong colour. There are three kinds.

**1. A wrong bead.** One bead is the wrong colour or shape, like Leo's bracelet. Fix it by **changing** that one bead.

**2. A missing bead.** One bead was left out. Look at this square, triangle, circle pattern:

### Why "the smallest fix" is the best fix

Look again at the bracelet with the extra blue bead. Someone might try to fix it by **changing** colours instead of
taking a bead out. They would have to change bead 5 to orange, bead 6 to blue, bead 7 to orange, bead 8 to blue and
bead 9 to orange. That is **5 changes**! Taking out the one extra bead is **1 change**.

Both ways end with a correct pattern. But one change is much more likely to be what really went wrong. When someone
makes a bracelet, it is more likely they slipped once than that they made five mistakes in a row. So we look for the
**smallest fix**: the fewest changes that make the whole pattern follow the rule.

This is a real idea used by grown-ups. When a computer sends a message and a bit of it gets spoiled on the way,
the computer at the other end looks for the smallest change that makes the message make sense again.

### Two beads swapped

Sometimes two beads next to each other have **swapped places**. In a *square, triangle, circle* pattern you might see
*square, circle, triangle*. Two beads are wrong, but it was one slip: the two were put on in the wrong order. Fix it
by swapping them back.

### Can a pattern have no mistake at all, but still look odd?

Yes! Sometimes a pattern looks wrong but is fine, because the core is longer than you thought. Look:

> orange, orange, blue, orange, blue, orange, orange, blue, orange, blue

At first it looks like *orange, orange, blue* with a mistake. But read on: *orange, orange, blue, orange, blue* comes
twice. The core is **5 beads long**: orange, orange, blue, orange, blue. No mistake at all!

So before you call something a mistake, look at **all** the beads, not only the first few. A mistake happens once. A
core comes again and again.

## Common mistake

**Calling the first odd-looking bead a mistake, without looking at the rest.** The core may be longer than you think,
or the pattern may just end in the middle of a core. Always check the **whole** row before you decide.

**Changing many beads when one change would do.** If a whole lot of beads after one spot look wrong, the real mistake
is probably a **missing or extra** bead at that spot. Look for the smallest fix.
