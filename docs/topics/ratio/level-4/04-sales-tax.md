# Sales tax

*Ratio, rates and money · level 4, lesson 4 of 6*

## A question to start

Kim has saved exactly **60 dollars** for a video game. The price tag in the shop in Toronto says **60.00**. She takes
the game to the till, hands over her money, and the cashier says: "That's **67.80**, please."

Kim is 7.80 dollars short. Nobody made a mistake. The extra money is **sales tax**, and in Canada it is usually added
at the till, not shown on the price tag.

Where does the 7.80 come from? And how can Kim work out, before she gets to the till, what she will really pay? By the
end of this lesson you will be able to do it in your head.

## The idea

### What sales tax is

A **tax** is money that people and businesses must pay to the government. Governments use taxes to pay for schools,
hospitals, roads, parks and much more. A **sales tax** is a tax added on top of the price when you buy something. The
shop collects it from you and passes it on to the government.

Sales tax is a **percent of the price**. That is why it is in this level: every sales tax question is a "percent of
an amount" question from [lesson 2](02-percent-of-amount.md).

### Sales tax in Canada

In Canada there is a federal tax called the **GST** (Goods and Services Tax) of **5%**. Most provinces add a tax of
their own.

- In **Ontario**, the federal and provincial taxes are combined into one **HST** (Harmonized Sales Tax) of **13%**:
  5% federal plus 8% provincial. Ontario has used 13% since July 2010.
- In **Alberta** there is no provincial sales tax, so shoppers pay only the 5% GST.
- Other provinces have their own rates and rules.

Tax rates are set by governments and **can change**, so always check the current rate where you live. In this lesson
we use Ontario's 13% for most examples.

Not everything is taxed. In Canada, basic groceries such as milk, bread, fruit and vegetables have no GST or HST,
while snacks, soft drinks and restaurant meals do. In Ontario, some items (for example children's clothing and printed
books) pay only the 5% federal part. The rules have many details; a receipt shows which items were taxed.

### Working out the tax

The tax is 13% of the price. Build it from easy pieces:

$$13\% = 10\% + 3\%.$$

For the 60 dollar game:

- 10% of 60 is 6.00 dollars.
- 1% of 60 is 0.60 dollars, so 3% is 1.80 dollars.
- Tax: $6.00 + 1.80 = 7.80$ dollars.
- Total: $60.00 + 7.80 = 67.80$ dollars.

That is exactly what the cashier said.

### One step: multiply by 1.13

There is a quicker way to get the total. You pay the whole price (100%) **plus** the tax (13%), which is 113% of the
price:

And 113% as a decimal is 1.13. So

$$\text{total} = \text{price} \times 1.13.$$

For the game: $60 \times 1.13 = 67.80$ dollars. Same answer, one step. Where the tax is 5%, multiply by 1.05.

Why is it allowed to combine the two steps? Because price + 13% of price is "1 lot of the price plus 0.13 lots of the
price", which is 1.13 lots of the price. This number, 1.13, is called a **multiplier**. You will use multipliers for
every kind of percent change in later levels.

### A bill with several items

When you buy several taxable things, the tax is worked out on the **subtotal** (the total before tax), then rounded to
the nearest cent:

| Item | Price |
| --- | --- |
| Notebook | 3.49 |
| Pens | 5.99 |
| Backpack | 32.99 |
| **Subtotal** | **42.47** |
| HST 13% | 5.52 |
| **Total** | **47.99** |

The tax is $42.47 \times 0.13 = 5.5211$ dollars, which rounds to **5.52** dollars.

### Paying cash with no pennies

Canada stopped making pennies in 2012 and stopped handing them out to shops in 2013. When you pay **cash**, the total
is rounded to the nearest 5 cents. When you pay by **card**, you pay the exact amount.

| Total ends in | Cash rounds to |
| --- | --- |
| 1 or 2 cents | down to 0 |
| 3 or 4 cents | up to 5 |
| 6 or 7 cents | down to 5 |
| 8 or 9 cents | up to the next 0 |

So the 47.99 bill above costs **48.00** dollars in cash, and 47.99 dollars by card.

### Estimating tax quickly

13% is very close to $\tfrac18$, which is 12.5%. So a quick estimate of the tax is "divide by 8", and of the total, "add
an eighth". For a 40 dollar item: $40 \div 8 = 5$, so the total is about 45 dollars (exactly $40 \times 1.13 = 45.20$).
Since 13% is a little **more** than 12.5%, the real tax is a little more than this estimate.

### Tax-included prices

Some prices already **include** the tax, such as the price shown on a gas pump. If a price includes 13% tax, it is 113%
of the price before tax. To find the price before tax, **divide by 1.13**. A tax-included price of 113 dollars was 100
dollars before tax, with 13 dollars of tax.

Notice that taking 13% **off** 113 dollars does **not** get you back to 100: 13% of 113 is about 14.69, and
$113 - 14.69 = 98.31$. The 13% was a percent of the price **before** tax, not of the total. You will meet this kind of
"reverse percent" again in [level 6](../level-6/index.md).

## Worked examples

**1. The video game.** 60 dollars with 13% HST.

- Tax: $10\% + 3\% = 6.00 + 1.80 = 7.80$ dollars.
- Total: $60 \times 1.13 = 67.80$ dollars.

**2. Some taxed, some not.** At an Ontario grocery store you buy milk (5.49), bread (3.29) and a bag of chips (4.99).
Only the chips are taxed.

- Tax on the chips: $4.99 \times 0.13 = 0.6487$, which rounds to 0.65 dollars.
- Total: $5.49 + 3.29 + 4.99 + 0.65 = 14.42$ dollars.
- In cash: 14.42 ends in 2, so it rounds down to **14.40** dollars.

**3. Ontario and Alberta.** A bike costs 250 dollars before tax.

- In Ontario (13%): $250 \times 1.13 = 282.50$ dollars.
- In Alberta (5%): $250 \times 1.05 = 262.50$ dollars.
- Difference: 20 dollars, which is the 8% provincial part: 8% of 250 is 20. ✓

**4. Can I afford it?** Two shirts cost 22 dollars each in Ontario. Is 50 dollars enough?

- Subtotal: $2 \times 22 = 44$ dollars.
- Total: $44 \times 1.13 = 49.72$ dollars. Yes, just.
- Change from 50 dollars: by card, 0.28 dollars; in cash, the total rounds to 49.70, so the change is 0.30 dollars.

**5. A tax-included price.** A tax-included price is 56.50 dollars with 13% HST. What was the price before tax?

- $56.50 \div 1.13 = 50$ dollars before tax.
- The tax was $56.50 - 50 = 6.50$ dollars. Check: 13% of 50 is 6.50. ✓

2. Find the total for a 45 dollar item with 13% HST.

4. A bill has items at 12.99, 7.50 and 3.25 dollars, all taxed at 13%. Find the subtotal, the tax and the total. What
   would you pay in cash?
5. Use the multiplier to find the total for a 149.99 dollar item with 13% HST.
6. Estimate the 13% tax on a 72 dollar item by dividing by 8. Then find the exact tax. Is the estimate a bit high or a
   bit low? Why?
7. A price including 13% HST is 226 dollars. What was the price before tax?
8. Explain in your own words why "price times 1.13" gives the total with 13% tax.

## Common mistake

**Forgetting the tax.** In Canada most price tags do not include sales tax. When you plan what you can afford, add
the tax first. A quick "add an eighth" estimate is better than nothing.

**Removing tax by subtracting 13%.** To go back from a tax-included price, divide by 1.13. Subtracting 13% of the total
takes off too much, because the total is bigger than the price the tax was based on.
