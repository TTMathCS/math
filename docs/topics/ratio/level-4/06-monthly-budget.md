# Budgets

*Ratio, rates and money · level 4, lesson 6 of 6*

## A question to start

Maya is 16 and works weekends at a garden centre. She earns about **480 dollars a month**. That sounds like plenty.
Yet by the end of every month her bank account is almost empty, and she cannot say where the money went. A bus pass
here, lunch with friends there, a new phone case, a birthday present...

Here is a small fact that surprises many people: a 4.50 dollar snack every school day adds up to about **90 dollars a
month**, and about **900 dollars over a school year**. Little amounts, repeated, become big amounts. That is rate
thinking again.

How can Maya take charge of her money, so that she knows where it goes and can save for something she really wants?
The tool is a **monthly budget**. Families, students, businesses and whole countries all use the same idea.

## The idea

### Income and expenses

- **Income** is money coming in: pay from a job, an allowance, gifts.
- **Expenses** are money going out: everything you spend.

For a month, the key number is

$$\text{income} - \text{expenses}.$$

If it is positive, you have a **surplus** (money left over). If it is negative, you have a **deficit**: you spent more
than came in, so the difference came from savings, or from borrowing. A budget **balances** when expenses are no more
than income.

For people with jobs, the income to plan with is **take-home pay**: what is actually paid into your account after
income tax and other amounts are taken off your pay. That is the money you can spend.

### Fixed and variable costs

Expenses come in two kinds.

- **Fixed costs** are the same every month: rent, a phone plan, a bus pass, an insurance payment. You know them in
  advance, and they are hard to change quickly.
- **Variable costs** change from month to month: groceries, eating out, clothes, entertainment, gas for the car. You
  have more control over them, day by day.

When a budget does not balance, the quickest place to look is usually the variable costs. Fixed costs can be cut too,
but it takes a decision (a cheaper phone plan, a smaller apartment).

### Costs that are not monthly

Some costs come once a year or a few times a year: birthday presents, a school trip, car insurance paid yearly. A good
budget turns them into a **monthly rate** so they do not come as a shock. If you spend about 240 dollars a year on
presents, that is $240 \div 12 = 20$ dollars a month to put aside.

Here is the budget Maya made after tracking her spending for a month.

Seeing the numbers, Maya notices that lunches and snacks take a quarter of her income. That is the 4.50 dollar snack
habit at work.

### Saving first

Notice that **savings** is a line in the budget, not "whatever is left at the end". Many people find that if they
wait to see what is left, nothing is. Putting the savings aside **first**, on payday, is sometimes called "paying
yourself first". It turns saving into a fixed cost.

A savings line also answers "how long until I can afford it?" If Maya saves 100 dollars a month for a 750 dollar
laptop, plus 13% tax, she needs $750 \times 1.13 = 847.50$ dollars. That takes $847.50 \div 100 = 8.475$ months, so
**9 months** (rounding up, since 8 months is not quite enough).

### A rule of thumb

One popular guideline splits take-home income into three parts:

- about **50%** for needs (housing, food, transport, bills),
- about **30%** for wants (fun, eating out, extras),
- about **20%** for savings.

It is a rule of thumb, not a law. A student living at home may save far more than 20%; a family in an expensive city
may need much more than 50% for needs. But it is a useful first check.

### When the budget does not balance

There are only three ways to fix a deficit:

1. **Spend less on variable costs**: cook instead of eating out, borrow instead of buy.
2. **Cut a fixed cost**: a cheaper plan, cancelling a membership you do not use.
3. **Earn more**: more hours, a second source of income.

Borrowing to cover a deficit only moves the problem to next month, with interest added. Credit and interest come
later, in [level 6](../level-6/index.md).

## Worked examples

**1. Is Maya's budget balanced?** Income 480, total out $35 + 60 + 120 + 80 + 50 + 20 + 100 = 465$ dollars. Left over:
$480 - 465 = 15$ dollars. Yes, it balances with a small surplus, **and** she saves 100 dollars, which is
$100 \div 480 \approx 20.8\%$ of her income.

**2. A family budget that does not balance.** A family's monthly take-home pay is 5200 dollars. Their plan:

| Item | Kind | Dollars |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rent | fixed | 1900 |
| Electricity, heat, water | mostly fixed | 250 |
| Groceries | variable | 900 |
| Car payment, gas, transit | mixed | 650 |
| Phone and internet | fixed | 180 |
| Child care | fixed | 600 |
| Insurance | fixed | 150 |
| Eating out and fun | variable | 400 |
| Clothing | variable | 150 |
| Savings | | 300 |
| **Total** | | **5480** |

That is $5480 - 5200 = 280$ dollars over. One way to fix it:

- eating out and fun from 400 down to 250 (saves 150);
- groceries from 900 to 820 by planning meals (saves 80);
- a cheaper phone and internet plan, from 180 to 130 (saves 50).

Savings: $150 + 80 + 50 = 280$ dollars. Now the budget balances exactly, and they still save 300 dollars a month.
Other fixes are possible; the math tells you whether a fix is **big enough**.

**3. Turning yearly costs into monthly.** A family pays 1200 dollars a year for car insurance and expects a 180 dollar
school trip in 6 months.

- Insurance: $1200 \div 12 = 100$ dollars a month.
- School trip: $180 \div 6 = 30$ dollars a month for the next 6 months.

**4. Using the 50/30/20 guide.** Maya's take-home pay is 480 dollars.

- Needs: 50% of 480 is 240 dollars.
- Wants: 30% of 480 is 144 dollars.
- Savings: 20% of 480 is 96 dollars.

Her actual savings, 100 dollars, are a bit more than the 20% guide.

**5. A small habit.** A 4.50 dollar snack every school day, 5 days a week, about 4 weeks a month:

- Per month: $4.50 \times 5 \times 4 = 90$ dollars.
- Per school year of about 10 months: $90 \times 10 = 900$ dollars.

## Try it

1. A student's income is 300 dollars a month and her expenses are 275 dollars. Is there a surplus or a deficit, and
   how much?
2. Sort these into fixed or variable: rent, groceries, phone plan, gas for the car, gym membership, eating out.
3. Home insurance costs 600 dollars a year. How much should be put aside each month?
4. Ben earns 360 dollars a month and wants to save 15% of it. How much is that?
5. Using the 50/30/20 guide, how would you split a take-home income of 2400 dollars a month?
6. How many months does it take to save 500 dollars at 60 dollars a month?
7. A coffee costs 3.75 dollars and someone buys one on each of 22 work days a month. What does that cost per month and
   per year?
8. A budget is 120 dollars over. The person can cut eating out by 70 dollars, switch phone plans to save 25 dollars,
   or cancel a 40 dollar gym membership. Which two of these together are enough? Is any one enough by itself?

??? note "Answers"
    1. $300 - 275 = 25$, a **surplus of 25 dollars**.
    2. Fixed: rent, phone plan, gym membership. Variable: groceries, gas for the car, eating out.
    3. $600 \div 12 = 50$ dollars a month.
    4. 10% of 360 is 36 and 5% is 18, so 15% is $36 + 18 = 54$ dollars.
    5. Needs: 50% is **1200** dollars. Wants: 30% is **720** dollars. Savings: 20% is **480** dollars. (Check:
       $1200 + 720 + 480 = 2400$.)
    6. $500 \div 60 \approx 8.33$, so **9 months** (after 8 months there are only 480 dollars).
    7. Per month: $3.75 \times 22 = 82.50$ dollars. Per year: $82.50 \times 12 = 990$ dollars.
    8. No single change is enough (the biggest is 70). Pairs: eating out and phone save $70 + 25 = 95$ (not enough);
       eating out and gym save $70 + 40 = 110$ (not enough); phone and gym save $25 + 40 = 65$ (not enough). **No pair
       is enough!** All three together save $70 + 25 + 40 = 135$ dollars, which fixes it with 15 dollars to spare.
       (Sometimes the honest answer is "you need all of them, or a bigger change".)

## Common mistake

**Budgeting with pay before deductions.** If a job pays 600 dollars but 60 dollars are taken off for tax and other
deductions, the budget must use 540 dollars. Plan with what actually arrives.

**Leaving out the irregular costs.** A budget that forgets yearly and once-in-a-while costs looks balanced until the
month the insurance bill or a birthday arrives. Turn them into monthly amounts and include them.
