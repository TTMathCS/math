# Making change

*Ratio, rates and money · level 2, lesson 2 of 6*

## A question to start

A notebook costs **3 dollars and 60 cents**. You pay with a **5-dollar bill**. The cashier gives you some coins back.
How much should you get?

Most people think "5 take away 3 dollars 60 cents" and get stuck, because taking away with dollars and cents feels
hard. But shopkeepers have used a trick for hundreds of years that needs no taking away at all. They **count up**.
Watch: "3 dollars 60 ... 70, 75, 4 dollars, 5 dollars." By the end of this lesson you will know exactly what that
means, and you will be able to check any cashier.

## The idea

### What change is

When you pay **more** than the price, the shop gives you the extra money back. The money you get back is called
**change**.

> The price and the change together make what you paid.

You paid 5 dollars. The notebook kept 3 dollars and 60 cents of it. The rest comes back to you as change.

### Counting up

Here is the trick. Start at the **price**. Add coins, one at a time, until you reach the amount **paid**. The coins
you added are the change.

The best way is to add small coins first to reach a "round" number, then bigger coins and bills.

Start at **3 dollars 60**.

1. Add a **dime**: 3 dollars 70.
2. Add a **nickel**: 3 dollars 75.
3. Add a **quarter**: **4 dollars**. Now we are at a round number of dollars.
4. Add a **loonie**: **5 dollars**. We reached what you paid. Stop.

The coins you added are 1 dime, 1 nickel, 1 quarter and 1 loonie. Count them (biggest first, as in
[Counting coins](01-counting-coins.md)): "1 dollar, 1 dollar 25, 1 dollar 35, 1 dollar 40".

So your change is **1 dollar and 40 cents**.

### Why counting up works

Think of the number line in the picture. The price, 3 dollars 60, is one place on the line. What you paid, 5 dollars,
is another place. The change is the **gap** between them: how far you must go to get from the price to what you
paid.

You can measure a gap two ways:

- Start at the big end and take away (5 dollars take away 3 dollars 60).
- Start at the small end and **count up** to the big end.

Both measure the same gap, so both give the same answer. Counting up is just easier, because adding a nickel or a
dime is easy, and reaching a round number like 4 dollars is easy too. The hard part of taking away (borrowing across
the dot) never happens.

That is also why a good cashier counts the change **into your hand**, saying the numbers out loud: "3.60 ... 3.70,
3.75, 4, and 5." You hear the count reach the amount you paid, so you know the change is right.

### Checking your change

Always check with the rule from the start:

> The price and the change together make what you paid.

3 dollars 60 and 1 dollar 40: the dollars make 4, and 60 cents and 40 cents make 100 cents, which is 1 more dollar.
So 5 dollars. It checks!

### Rounding to 5 cents when you pay cash

Canada has had no pennies since 2013 (see [Canadian coins](../level-1/04-canadian-coins.md)). But some prices
still end in 1, 2, 3 and so on, like 3 dollars 62. When you pay with **coins and bills**, the total is rounded to the
nearest 5 cents:

| The total ends in | It becomes | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 or 2 | down to 0 | 3.62 becomes 3.60 |
| 3 or 4 | up to 5 | 3.63 becomes 3.65 |
| 6 or 7 | down to 5 | 3.67 becomes 3.65 |
| 8 or 9 | up to 10 | 3.68 becomes 3.70 |

The rounding always goes to whichever of the nearby 0 or 5 is **closest**. If you pay by card, nothing is rounded:
the shop takes the exact amount.

## Worked examples

**Example 1. A 10-dollar bill.** A book costs 6 dollars and 25 cents. You pay with a 10-dollar bill. What is your
change?

1. Start at 6.25.
2. Quarter: 6.50. Quarter: 6.75. Quarter: **7.00**. (Three quarters to reach a round number.)
3. Toonie: 9.00. Loonie: **10.00**. We reached what you paid.
4. The change is 3 quarters, 1 toonie and 1 loonie. Count biggest first: 2 dollars, 3 dollars, 3.25, 3.50, 3.75.
5. Change: **3 dollars and 75 cents**.
6. Check: 6.25 and 3.75. Cents: 25 and 75 make 100, which is 1 dollar. Dollars: 6 and 3 and that 1 make 10. Yes.

**Example 2. A toonie.** A juice costs 1 dollar 35. You pay with a toonie. What is your change?

1. Start at 1.35.
2. Nickel: 1.40. Dime: 1.50. Quarter: 1.75. Quarter: **2.00**.
3. Change: 1 nickel, 1 dime and 2 quarters. Count biggest first: 25, 50, 60, 65.
4. Change: **65 cents**.

**Example 3. Almost exact.** A snack costs 4 dollars 85. You pay with a 5-dollar bill.

1. Start at 4.85.
2. Nickel: 4.90. Dime: **5.00**.
3. Change: **15 cents**. Only two small coins. When the price is close to what you paid, the change is small.

**Example 4. Rounding first.** Your total is 2 dollars 63. You pay cash with a toonie and a loonie (3 dollars).

1. Round first: 63 ends in 3, so it rounds **up** to 2.65.
2. Count up from 2.65: dime 2.75, quarter **3.00**.
3. Change: a dime and a quarter, **35 cents**.

## Try it

1. You pay with a loonie for a pencil that costs 80 cents. What is your change?
2. You pay with a toonie for a muffin that costs 1 dollar 50. What is your change?
3. You pay with a 5-dollar bill for a toy that costs 2 dollars 75. What is your change? Which coins could it be?
4. You pay with a 10-dollar bill for a lunch that costs 7 dollars 40. What is your change?
5. A cashier gives you 1 dollar 50 change when you pay 5 dollars for something costing 3 dollars 60. Is that right?
   Who gets the mistake, you or the shop?
6. Paying cash, what does each total become: 2.98, 4.12, 6.06?
7. Your total is 1 dollar 33. You pay cash with a toonie. What is your change?
8. You bought a comic and paid with a 5-dollar bill. Your change was 1 dollar 25. How much did the comic cost?

??? note "Answers"
    1. **20 cents.** Count up from 80: dime, 90; dime, 100. That is 2 dimes. (Check: 80 and 20 make 100 cents, a
       dollar.)
    2. **50 cents.** Count up from 1.50: quarter, 1.75; quarter, 2.00. Two quarters make 50 cents.
    3. **2 dollars and 25 cents.** Count up from 2.75: quarter, 3.00; toonie, 5.00. So the change is a quarter and a
       toonie. (Or a quarter and 2 loonies. Any coins worth 2 dollars 25 are right.)
    4. **2 dollars and 60 cents.** Count up from 7.40: dime 7.50, quarter 7.75, quarter 8.00, toonie 10.00. Change:
       2 dollars, then 25, 50, 60 cents. Check: 7.40 and 2.60 make 10.00.
    5. **No.** The right change is 1 dollar 40. The cashier gave 1 dollar 50, which is 10 cents too much. The **shop**
       loses 10 cents. (An honest customer gives the extra dime back.)
    6. 2.98 ends in 8, so it rounds up to **3.00**. 4.12 ends in 2, so it rounds down to **4.10**. 6.06 ends in 6,
       so it rounds down to **6.05**.
    7. **65 cents.** First round 1.33 up to 1.35 (it ends in 3). Count up from 1.35: nickel 1.40, dime 1.50, quarter
       1.75, quarter 2.00. Change: 25, 50, 60, 65.
    8. **3 dollars and 75 cents.** This time, start at what you paid and go **back** by the change. 5 dollars take
       away 1 dollar is 4 dollars. 4 dollars take away 25 cents is 3 dollars 75. Check by counting up from 3.75:
       quarter 4.00, loonie 5.00. The change is a loonie and a quarter, 1 dollar 25. Yes.

## Common mistake

**Stopping at the round number.** Counting up from 3.60 to 4.00 and saying "40 cents change" forgets the last jump
from 4 dollars up to the 5 dollars you paid. Keep going until you reach **what you paid**.

**Counting the coins, not their worth.** "I got 4 coins back" says nothing. Count the change itself, biggest coin
first, and check that price and change add up to what you paid.
