Now look closely at the framed picture. What is in it? A sun, a hill, and ... a framed picture. And in **that** framed
picture? A sun, a hill, and a framed picture again.

How many pictures are hiding in there? If the picture were perfect, would they ever stop?

## The idea

### A picture that contains itself

The picture above has a copy of **itself** inside it. The copy is smaller, but it shows exactly the same thing: the
sun, the hill and the frame. And because the copy shows the frame, the copy has a copy inside it too. And that copy
has a copy, and so on.

This is called a **picture in a picture**. In level 1 you saw the same thing in
[mirrors facing each other](../level-1/04-mirrors-facing.md), and heard it in a
[story that tells itself](../level-1/05-never-ending-stories.md). Now you will learn how it works, and how to draw one
yourself.

### The cocoa tin

More than a hundred years ago, in the early 1900s, a Dutch company called Droste sold cocoa in tins. The picture on
the tin showed a nurse carrying a tray. On the tray was a cup of cocoa and a tin of Droste cocoa. And on **that** tin
was the same picture: the nurse, the tray, the tin ... In the Netherlands, a picture that contains a smaller copy of
itself came to be called the **Droste effect**, and the name has spread around the world.

You can find the Droste effect in lots of places once you look:

- A cereal box with a picture of a child holding the same cereal box.
- A video call where someone points their camera at their own screen: a screen in a screen in a screen.
- A **"You are here" map** in a park. The map shows the whole park, and the park has the map stand in it. So a perfect
  map would show a tiny map stand, with a tiny map on it, showing the park, with a tinier map stand ...
- A famous print by the Dutch artist **M. C. Escher**, called *Print Gallery* (1956): a young man in a gallery looks
  at a picture of a town, and the town has the gallery in it, with the young man inside.

### Why the copies get smaller

Each copy has to **fit inside** the spot where it hangs. The spot is smaller than the whole picture, so the copy is
smaller than the whole picture.

In our drawing, each copy is **half as wide** and **half as tall** as the picture around it. Look at the widths:

| Picture | How wide (in our drawing) |
| --- | --- |
| the big picture | 300 |
| copy 1 | 150 |
| copy 2 | 75 |
| copy 3 | 37 and a half |
| copy 4 | about 19 |
| copy 5 | about 9 |

Each copy is half the size of the one before. That is the halving from
[Halving a cookie](../level-1/02-halving-cookie.md) again! And just like the cookie, halving never reaches zero. So in
a perfect picture, **there would always be one more copy**, smaller and smaller, forever.

### Where do the copies go?

Look at where the copies sit. Each one is in the bottom right of the one before. So the copies march off towards one
special spot, a little up and to the left of the bottom right corner, getting tinier and tinier. They squeeze closer and closer to
that spot but never fill it up: there is always room for one more.

In the mirror tunnel, the copies all sit in the **middle**, one inside the next. In the cocoa tin, the copies sit off
to one side. Both are pictures in pictures: the copies just march towards a different spot.

### Real pictures stop

On paper, the copies stop. Your pencil line has a thickness, and after five or six copies, a copy is smaller than the
tip of your pencil. A printer stops at the tiniest dot it can print. A screen stops at its tiniest dot of light.

But the **idea**, the rule "inside the picture, draw the whole picture again, smaller", never stops. The rule can be
followed again and again forever. Real pictures stop; the rule does not.

### Drawing your own

Here is how to draw your own picture in a picture.

1. **Choose a picture with a frame in it.** For example: a TV in a living room, a poster on a bedroom wall, a book
   cover with a child reading the book, a window, a phone screen.
2. **Draw the big picture**, with the frame empty. Use a ruler for the frame, and make the frame the **same shape** as
   the whole page, just smaller. (If your page is wide and short, the frame should be wide and short.)
3. **Inside the frame, draw everything again**, smaller: the furniture, the people, and the empty frame in the same
   place.
4. **Repeat.** Inside the new frame, draw everything again, even smaller.
5. **Stop** when the frame is too tiny to draw inside. Colour the last tiny frame in, or put a dot in it, to show "and
   so on, forever".

A tip: keep the drawing **simple**. A sun, a tree and a frame are much easier to copy five times than a busy room.

## Worked examples

**1. Half each time.** A picture is 16 centimetres wide. Each copy inside is half as wide as the one around it. How
wide are the copies, and which copy is the first one narrower than 1 centimetre?

Halve each time: copy 1 is 8 cm, copy 2 is 4 cm, copy 3 is 2 cm, copy 4 is 1 cm, copy 5 is half a centimetre. So
**copy 5** is the first one narrower than 1 centimetre. Copies 6, 7, 8, ... would be a quarter, an eighth, a sixteenth
of a centimetre: too small for a pencil, but still there in the idea.

**2. A third each time.** A poster is 27 centimetres wide. Each copy is one third as wide. The copies are 9, 3 and
1 centimetres wide: share 27 into 3 equal parts and each part is 9; share 9 into 3 and get 3; share 3 into 3 and get 1.
Shrinking by thirds is faster than shrinking by halves, so the copies get tiny sooner.

**3. Counting the pictures.** In the sun-and-hill drawing at the top of this page, count the frames, including the
big one. There are **7** frames. The seventh is so small that you have to squint to see it. In a perfect picture there
would be infinitely many.

**4. The map in the park.** A park map shows the whole park, 400 metres wide, on a board 1 metre wide. So on the map,
everything is 400 times smaller. The map stand on the map is 400 times smaller than the real map: 1 metre shared into
400 parts, which is just two and a half millimetres. And on that tiny map is an even tinier one, 400 times smaller again. These copies shrink so fast
that the second one is already far too small to see.

## Try it

1. What is the Droste effect?
2. A picture is 32 centimetres wide. Each copy is half as wide. How wide are the first three copies?
3. In question 2, which copy is 1 centimetre wide?
4. Why do the copies in a picture in a picture get smaller?
5. Why can you not draw infinitely many copies on real paper?
6. Name two places where you have seen (or could see) a picture in a picture.
7. Draw your own picture in a picture, with at least three copies. Where do your copies march off to?

??? note "Answers"
    1. A picture that has a smaller copy of **itself** inside it, which has a smaller copy inside it, and so on. It
       is named after the Dutch cocoa tin that showed a picture of the same tin.
    2. Half of 32 is **16**, half of 16 is **8**, half of 8 is **4**. So copies 1, 2 and 3 are 16, 8 and 4
       centimetres wide.
    3. Copy 4 is 2 cm, copy 5 is **1 cm**. So **copy 5**.
    4. Each copy has to fit inside the frame of the picture around it, and that frame is smaller than the whole
       picture.
    5. After a few copies, a copy is smaller than the tip of your pencil (or the smallest dot a printer can print). The
       **rule** could go on forever, but real paper and pencils cannot.
    6. Many answers are right: a cereal box, a camera filming its own screen, two mirrors facing each other, a "You are
       here" map, a book cover showing someone reading that book, the cocoa tin.
    7. Everyone's drawing is different. The copies march off towards the spot where your frame is: if your frame is in
       the top left, the copies go towards the top left.

## Common mistake

**Making the frame a different shape from the page.** If the page is wide and the frame is tall and thin, the copy
will not fit nicely, and the picture looks squashed. Make the frame the same shape as the whole picture, just smaller.

**Thinking the copies stop because the idea stops.** They stop on paper because of the pencil. The rule "draw the whole
picture inside the frame" can always be done once more.
