# Perimeter

*Measurement · level 2, lesson 6 of 6*

## A question to start

A farmer has **12 pieces of fence**, each piece 1 metre long. She wants to make a pen for her goats in the shape of a
rectangle, using all 12 pieces, with no pieces left over and no gaps.

How many different rectangle pens can she make? And does it matter which one she picks?

Try it with 12 toothpicks or 12 matchsticks on the table before you read on. You will find more than one answer, and
here is the surprise: all the pens use exactly the same fence, but some give the goats **much more room** than
others. By the end of this lesson you will know every answer, and why.

## The idea

### Walking around the edge

Imagine an ant walking once all the way around the edge of a shape, starting at a corner and stopping when it gets
back to the same corner. The distance the ant walks is called the **perimeter** of the shape.

The word comes from two old Greek words: "peri" means **around**, and "metre" means **measure**. So perimeter means
"the measure around".

To find the perimeter, **add up the lengths of all the sides**. Go round the shape in order so you do not miss one.

5 cm and 3 cm and 5 cm and 3 cm: 5 and 3 is 8, 8 and 5 is 13, 13 and 3 is **16 cm**.

Perimeter is a **length**, the length of the ant's walk. So it is measured in length units: centimetres or metres.

Where do we need perimeters in real life? A fence around a garden, a ribbon around the edge of a card, the trim
around a picture frame, the white line around a soccer field, a running track. Each time, the question is "how long
is the way around?"

### Rectangles and squares: a shortcut

In a rectangle, the **opposite sides are the same length**: the top is as long as the bottom, and the left side is as
long as the right side. So if you know one long side and one short side, you know all four.

For the 5 cm by 3 cm rectangle: one long side and one short side make 5 and 3, which is 8. The other two sides make
another 8. So the perimeter is 8 and 8, which is **16 cm**. That is the same answer, with less adding.

A square has **4 sides all the same length**. A square with sides of 6 cm has perimeter 6, 12, 18, 24: **24 cm**.
Counting by the side length 4 times is the same as adding it 4 times.

### Perimeter on a grid

On squared paper, each little square has sides of **1 unit**. To find the perimeter of a shape on a grid, count the
little **edges** around the outside.

Walk around and count the edges: 4 along the top, 2 down the right, 4 along the bottom, 2 up the left. That is
**12 units**.

But careful! There are only **8 squares** inside. The perimeter is not the number of squares. Squares are the
**inside** of the shape; edges are the **way around**. The corner squares, for example, each have **2** edges on the
outside, so if you counted squares instead of edges, you would count the corners only once and get the wrong answer.

### Finding a missing side

Sometimes you know the perimeter but not all the sides. Then you work backwards.

A rectangle has a perimeter of **20 cm**, and its long side is **6 cm**. How long is the short side?

1. One long side and one short side make **half** of the way around. Half of 20 is 10.
2. So the long side and the short side together make 10 cm.
3. The long side is 6 cm. 6 and what make 10? **4**. The short side is **4 cm**.
4. Check: 6, 4, 6, 4. 6 and 4 is 10, and 10 and 10 is 20. ✓

- 5 by 1: 5 and 1 is 6, twice is **12**.
- 4 by 2: 4 and 2 is 6, twice is **12**.
- 3 by 3 (a square, which is a special rectangle): 3 and 3 is 6, twice is **12**.

Why are there only three? For any rectangle, one long side and one short side make **half** the perimeter, which is
6. The pairs of whole numbers that make 6 are 5 and 1, 4 and 2, 3 and 3. (6 and 0 would be no pen at all: just a
flat fence with no room inside!) So there are exactly three pens.

But do they give the goats the same room? Count the squares inside. The 5 by 1 pen has **5** squares. The 4 by 2 pen
has **8**. The 3 by 3 pen has **9**. Same fence, very different room! The farmer should choose the **square** pen,
3 m by 3 m. The amount of room inside is called **area**, and you will learn all about it in level 3.

takes a little detour step inward instead of going round the corner.

1. Go round and list every side: 10 m, 5 m, 4 m, 3 m, 6 m, 8 m. That is 6 sides; an L shape always has 6.
2. Check the lengths fit. The left side (8 m) should be the right side and the step up together: 5 and 3 is 8. ✓ The
   bottom (10 m) should be the top and the step across together: 6 and 4 is 10. ✓
3. Add, making easy tens where you can: 10, then 5 and 4 and 3 is 12, so 22; then 6 and 8 is 14, so 22 and 14 is
   **36 m**.
4. Fun check: a plain 10 m by 8 m rectangle also has perimeter 10 and 8 and 10 and 8, which is 36 m. Cutting the corner
   inward did not change the perimeter, just like the grid puzzle above.

**Example 3. A missing side.** A rectangle has a perimeter of 18 m and a short side of 2 m. How long is the long side?

1. Half of 18 is 9. So one short side and one long side make 9 m.
2. 2 and what make 9? **7**. The long side is **7 m**.
3. Check: 7, 2, 7, 2 makes 18. ✓

**Example 4. A fence and units.** A square sandbox has sides of 2 metres. Fence comes in 50 cm pieces. How many pieces?

1. The perimeter is 2, 4, 6, 8: **8 metres**.
2. Each metre is 100 cm, which is 2 pieces of 50 cm.
3. So 8 metres needs 8 lots of 2, which is **16 pieces**.

## Common mistake

**Counting squares instead of edges.** On a grid, the perimeter is the number of little **edges** around the outside,
not the number of squares. A corner square has 2 outside edges, and both count.

**Missing a side.** In a rectangle, it is easy to add only two sides (5 and 3 is 8) and forget the other two. Go all
the way round, or remember that a rectangle's perimeter is **twice** "one long side and one short side".
