# Measuring with hands and feet

*Measurement · level 1, lesson 4 of 6*

## A question to start

How long is your table? You do not need a ruler. Stretch your hand out wide, put your thumb at one end of the table,
and "walk" your hand along it, span after span, counting as you go.

Now ask a grown-up to do the same with their hand. Did you get the same number? Almost surely not! Maybe you counted
8 and the grown-up counted 5. The table did not change. So who is right?

Here is the surprise: **you are both right**. In this lesson you will find out why two careful people can measure the
same table and get two different numbers, and why that is exactly the reason people later invented rulers.

## The idea

### From "longer" to "how long"

In [Longer and shorter](01-longer-shorter.md) you found out **which** thing is longer. Now we want to know **how
long** a thing is, with a number. To do that, we pick one small thing to measure with and count how many fit.

The small thing we count is called a **unit**. Your hand span can be a unit. So can your foot, a paper clip, a
block or a crayon.

- A **hand span** is from the tip of your thumb to the tip of your little finger, with your hand stretched wide.
- A **foot** is the length of your foot, from heel to toe.
- A **pace** is one normal step.

The pencil is **5 paper clips long**. To make the count honest, follow four rules.

1. **Start exactly at the end.** Line up the first clip with the end of the pencil.
2. **No gaps.** Each clip starts right where the last one finished.
3. **No overlaps.** The clips must not lie on top of each other.
4. **Same unit every time.** All the clips must be the same size.

The strip is really 5 clips long, just like the pencil. With **gaps**, we only counted 4, because the gaps were part
of the length but nobody counted them. That number is **too small**. With **overlaps**, we counted 7, because some
bits of the strip were counted twice. That number is **too big**.

### "About" and "a bit more than"

Things are not always an exact number of units long. A book might be more than 3 hand spans but less than 4. Then
we say:

- "The book is **about** 3 hand spans long", if it is just a little more than 3, or
- "The book is **between 3 and 4** hand spans long."

That is honest. Real measuring nearly always has a little bit left over at the end.

The table is the same length both times. The big spans are bigger, so **fewer** of them are needed to cover it. The
small spans are smaller, so **more** of them are needed.

**A smaller unit gives a bigger number. A bigger unit gives a smaller number.**

Think of filling a jar with sweets. If the sweets are big, only a few fit. If they are tiny, lots fit. The jar did
not change, only the size of the sweets.

So you and the grown-up are both right: "8 of my spans" and "5 of your spans" are the same length, said in two
different units. The trouble is that the number alone, "8", does not tell anyone how long the table is, unless they
know whose hand you used. That is why, later, everyone agreed to use the **same** units, like the centimetre and the
metre. You will meet them in [level 2](../level-2/01-same-units.md).

### Body units long ago

For thousands of years, people really did measure with their bodies. The ancient Egyptians used the **cubit**,
the length from the elbow to the tip of the middle finger, to plan buildings. A **foot** was once the length of a
foot. And even today, the height of a horse is measured in **hands**. Body units are handy, because you always carry
them with you. Their problem is the one you just found: not everyone's hand is the same size.

## Worked examples

**Example 1. Who has the longer step?** Jo walks across a rug and takes 6 steps. Her big brother walks across the
same rug and takes 4 steps. Who takes longer steps?

1. The rug is the same length for both.
2. The brother needed **fewer** steps to cover it.
3. Fewer steps to cover the same length means each step is **bigger**.
4. So the **brother** takes longer steps.

**Example 2. Measuring with blocks.** Kai lays blocks along a book, end to end, starting at the end of the book. The
book is covered by 7 blocks, with a little bit of the last block sticking out past the book.

1. 7 blocks cover a bit more than the book.
2. 6 blocks would not quite reach the end.
3. So the book is **between 6 and 7 blocks** long, a bit less than 7. We can say "about 7 blocks".

**Example 3. Spot the mistake.** Lily measures a shelf with crayons and gets 10. She left a gap between each crayon.
Is the shelf more than 10 crayons long, or less?

1. The gaps are part of the shelf, but she did not count them.
2. So she counted **too few** crayons.
3. The shelf is **more** than 10 crayons long. To fix it, she should push the crayons together with no gaps and count
   again.

## Try it

1. A desk is 6 of your hand spans long. Your teacher measures it and gets 4 of her hand spans. Whose span is bigger?
2. Would you measure the length of your classroom in hand spans or in steps? Why?
3. You lay 5 pencils end to end along a table, but you leave gaps between them. Is "5 pencils long" too big or too
   small?
4. You measure a rug with paper clips, but some clips lie on top of each other. Is your number too big or too small?
5. A bench is 8 blocks long. A table is 11 blocks long (the same blocks). How many blocks longer is the table?
6. Ben measures his bed with his feet and says "12 feet". His dad measures it with his feet and says "8 feet". Ben
   says, "My bed must be longer when I measure it." Is he right?
7. Sam measures a ribbon with 3 big blocks and 2 small blocks and says "the ribbon is 5 blocks long". What is wrong?

??? note "Answers"
    1. Your teacher's. She needed fewer spans to cover the same desk, so each of her spans must be bigger.
    2. Steps (paces). Hand spans would take a very long time and you might lose count. A step is a bigger unit, so
       you need fewer of them.
    3. Too small. The gaps are part of the table's length, but you did not count them. The table is really more than
       5 pencils long.
    4. Too big. Where clips overlap, some parts of the rug are counted twice. The rug is really fewer clips long than
       you counted.
    5. Count on from 8 to 11: 9, 10, 11. That is **3 blocks** longer.
    6. No. The bed is the same bed. Ben's feet are smaller, so he needs more of them. A smaller unit gives a bigger
       number.
    7. The blocks are not all the same size. "5 blocks" does not mean anything when the blocks are different. He
       should measure again with blocks that are all the same.

## Common mistake

**Leaving gaps or letting the units overlap.** Gaps make your number too small; overlaps make it too big. Line each
unit up right where the last one ended.

**Thinking a bigger number always means a longer thing.** "My desk is 10 spans and yours is only 6 steps, so mine is
longer" is not fair: the units are different. Compare numbers only when the units are the same.
