# Parallel and perpendicular lines

*Geometry · level 3, lesson 3 of 6*

## A question to start

Look at a train track. The two rails run side by side for hundreds of kilometres, over hills and round long bends,
and they never touch. If they came even a little closer together, the train would fall off.

Now stand between the rails and look far down the track. The rails seem to meet in a point in the distance! Do they
really meet? And if not, why do they look as if they do?

## The idea

### Lines that go on forever

In geometry, a **line** is perfectly straight, has no thickness, and goes on forever in both directions. We can only
ever draw a piece of it, so imagine your drawing carrying on past the edge of the paper, past the walls, past the
Moon.

Take any two lines lying flat on the same sheet of paper (mathematicians say "in the same plane": a **plane** is a
perfectly flat surface that goes on forever). There are only two things that can happen:

- they **cross** at exactly one point, or
- they **never** cross, however far you carry them on.

They cannot cross twice. Two straight lines that met at two points would have to be the same line.

### Parallel lines

Two lines in the same plane that **never meet** are called **parallel**. Railway tracks, the lines on writing paper,
the opposite edges of a door, the top and bottom of this page: all parallel.

What makes them never meet? They point in **exactly the same direction**. If one line tipped even a tiny bit more
than the other, the gap between them would slowly shrink on one side, and far enough along they would cross.

That gives us a test you can do with a ruler: **parallel lines stay the same distance apart everywhere**. Measure the
gap (straight across, at a square corner to the lines) in two different places. If the gap is the same, the lines are
parallel.

In drawings, we show parallel lines by putting the same small arrow mark on each of them. If a drawing has two
different pairs of parallel lines, the second pair gets double arrows.

There is a short way to write "is parallel to": two upright strokes, **∥**. If line $AB$ is parallel to line $CD$, we
write $AB \parallel CD$. (Lines are often named by two points on them, written with capital letters.)

### Perpendicular lines

Two lines that cross at a **right angle** (90°) are called **perpendicular** (say "per-pen-DIK-you-lar"). The
corners of a window, the legs of a table and the floor, the lines of a noughts-and-crosses grid: all perpendicular.

When two lines are perpendicular, all **four** angles where they cross are right angles. Why? The four angles make a
full turn, 360°. If one of them is 90°, the one next to it along the same straight line is $180 - 90 = 90$ degrees,
and so on all the way round.

We show perpendicular lines with the small square at the crossing. The short way to write "is perpendicular to" is an
upside-down T: **⊥**. So $AB \perp CD$ means line $AB$ is perpendicular to line $CD$.

Lines that cross at any other angle are just called **intersecting** lines. ("Intersect" means "cut across each
other".)

### Parallel and perpendicular together

Look at a ladder. The two long sides, the **rails**, are parallel. Each **rung** is perpendicular to both rails.

This shows a fact you can trust: **if two lines are both perpendicular to the same line, they are parallel to each
other**. Picture standing on a rung and looking along it. Both rails go straight "up" from the rung, at a right
angle. They both start out in exactly the same direction, so they will never meet.

And the other way round: **if a line is perpendicular to one of two parallel lines, it is perpendicular to the other
too**. That is why one rung can meet both rails at a right angle.

This pair of facts is why streets in many cities are laid out on a **grid**: all the north-south streets are parallel
to each other, all the east-west streets are parallel to each other, and every north-south street is perpendicular to
every east-west street.

### Why the rails look as if they meet

Back to the question at the start. The rails never meet: if they did, trains would derail. They only **look** as if
they meet because things far away look smaller. The gap between the rails is the same size everywhere, but a gap far
away looks tiny, so the rails seem to come together. Artists use this on purpose: drawing parallel lines that meet at
a **vanishing point** makes a flat picture look deep. It is a trick of the eye, not a fact about the lines.

### Drawing parallel and perpendicular lines

**A perpendicular line**, with a set square (a triangle-shaped ruler with a right-angle corner) or your folded-paper
right-angle tester from lesson 1:

1. Lay one edge of the right-angle corner along your line.
2. Slide it to the point where you want the new line.
3. Draw along the other edge of the right angle.

**A parallel line**, with a ruler and a set square:

1. Lay one edge of the set square along your line.
2. Put a ruler firmly against another edge of the set square.
3. Hold the ruler still and slide the set square along it.
4. Draw along the same edge you started with. It has moved, but it has not turned, so it points the same way.

### A line, a ray and a segment

While we are naming things: a **line** goes on forever both ways. A **ray** starts at a point and goes on forever one
way, like a beam of light from a torch. A **line segment** is the piece between two end points, and it has a length
you can measure. The arms of an angle are really rays. The sides of a shape are segments. When we say two segments
are parallel, we mean the lines they lie on are parallel.

## Worked examples

**1. Lines in capital letters.** Find parallel and perpendicular pairs in the letters H, E, Z and N.

- **H**: the two uprights are **parallel**. The crossbar is **perpendicular** to both of them (just like a rung on a
  ladder).
- **E**: the three bars are all **parallel** to each other. Each bar is **perpendicular** to the upright.
- **Z**: the top and bottom bars are **parallel**. The slanting stroke is neither parallel nor perpendicular to them:
  it meets them at acute angles.
- **N**: the two uprights are **parallel**. The slant meets them at acute angles, so no perpendicular pair.

**2. A rectangle.** A rectangle has 4 sides: top, bottom, left, right. How many pairs of parallel sides and how many
pairs of perpendicular sides?

- Parallel: top ∥ bottom, left ∥ right. **2 pairs**.
- Perpendicular: each corner is a right angle, and each corner joins one pair of sides: top-left, top-right,
  bottom-left, bottom-right. **4 pairs**.

**3. Testing for parallel.** Two lines are 4 cm apart at the left end of the page and 4 cm apart at the right end. A
second pair is 4 cm apart at the left and 3 cm apart at the right. Which pair is parallel?

The first pair: the gap stays the same. The second pair gets closer together towards the right, by 1 cm across the
page. If you carried them on past the right edge, the gap would keep shrinking until they crossed. They are **not**
parallel.

**4. On a grid of streets.** Elm Street runs north-south. Oak Street runs east-west. Pine Street runs north-south.
Which pairs are parallel and which perpendicular?

- Elm and Pine both run north-south: **parallel**.
- Oak runs east-west, at a right angle to north-south: Oak ⊥ Elm and Oak ⊥ Pine.

## Try it

1. Name two things in your kitchen that have parallel edges, and two that have perpendicular edges.
2. Which of these capital letters have a pair of perpendicular lines: T, V, L, X, F?
3. How many right angles are there where two perpendicular lines cross?
4. Line $a$ is perpendicular to line $b$. Line $c$ is also perpendicular to line $b$. What can you say about $a$ and
   $c$?
5. Line $p$ is parallel to line $q$. Line $r$ is perpendicular to $p$. What is the angle between $r$ and $q$?
6. A square has 4 sides. How many pairs of parallel sides does it have? How many pairs of perpendicular sides?
7. Two lines are 5 cm apart at one place and 7 cm apart at another. Are they parallel? Will they ever meet?
8. In a room, think of a line along the floor by the front wall, and a line along the ceiling by the left wall. They
   never meet. Are they parallel?

??? note "Answers"
    1. Many answers are right. Parallel: opposite edges of a fridge door, the two long edges of a chopping board.
       Perpendicular: the edge of a table and its leg, the side and bottom of a cereal box.
    2. **T, L, F**. T and L each have a square corner; F has two bars that are each perpendicular to the upright. V
       meets at an acute angle. X crosses at a slant in most fonts (if the strokes crossed at a right angle it would
       count, but they usually do not).
    3. **Four**. Once one angle is 90°, its neighbour on the straight line is $180 - 90 = 90°$, and so on round.
    4. $a$ and $c$ are **parallel**: both are perpendicular to the same line $b$, like two rails on one rung.
    5. **90°**. A line perpendicular to one of two parallel lines is perpendicular to the other.
    6. 2 pairs of parallel sides (top and bottom, left and right) and 4 pairs of perpendicular sides (one pair at each
       corner).
    7. Not parallel: the gap changes. They get closer on the 5 cm side, so somewhere beyond that side they cross.
    8. **No**. They point in different directions (one runs across the room, the other runs front to back), so they
       are not parallel. They never meet because they are at different heights: they are not in the same flat plane.
       Lines like these are called **skew** lines. They can only happen in three dimensions.

## Common mistake

**Thinking "never meet on the page" means parallel.** Two short segments can stop before they meet, yet point in
different directions. Imagine carrying them on forever: parallel lines must keep **exactly** the same gap. Measure the
gap in two places to be sure.

**Thinking perpendicular lines must be upright and flat.** A line slanting up to the right can be perpendicular to
one slanting up to the left, as long as they meet at 90°. Turn the page: perpendicular lines stay perpendicular
however you hold them.
