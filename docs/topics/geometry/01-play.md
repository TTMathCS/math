# Play: Shapes, tangrams and symmetry

*Geometry · step 1 of 5*

## A question to start

Take a square of paper. Fold it in half, then fold it in half again, so it is four layers thick. With scissors,
snip off the corner where the two folds meet. Now unfold it.

Before you try it, guess: what shape is the hole?

You get a hole right in the middle, shaped like a diamond. One small snip became a shape with four equal sides,
because the folds copied your cut four times, each one a mirror image of the next. That copying has a name,
**symmetry**, and it is one of the big ideas of geometry, the mathematics of shapes.

## The idea

### Shapes and their parts

A flat shape made of straight sides is called a **polygon**. The straight edges are its **sides**, and the corners,
where two sides meet, are its **vertices** (one corner is a **vertex**). Polygons are named by how many sides they
have:

| Sides | Name | Where you see one |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 3 | triangle | a slice of sandwich cut corner to corner |
| 4 | quadrilateral (squares and rectangles are two kinds) | a door, a book |
| 5 | pentagon | the shape of a house drawn by a child, roof and all |
| 6 | hexagon | the cells of a honeycomb |
| 8 | octagon | a stop sign |

A **circle** is not a polygon: it has no straight sides and no corners. Every point on it is the same distance from
the centre.

A **square** has four equal sides and four square corners. A **rectangle** has four square corners, but its sides
can be two different lengths. So every square is a rectangle, but not every rectangle is a square.

### Tangrams

A **tangram** is an old Chinese puzzle: a square cut into seven pieces. There are two large triangles, one
medium triangle, two small triangles, one square and one slanted four-sided piece called a **parallelogram**.

The rule of the game: use **all seven pieces**, with no gaps and no overlaps, to make a picture: a cat, a boat, a
running person, a house. Thousands of pictures are possible.

Playing with tangrams teaches something important. The pieces fit because of their sizes. The two small triangles
together can make the square, or the medium triangle, or the parallelogram, so each of those three pieces covers
exactly as much space as two small triangles. Shapes can be cut up and rearranged, and **they still cover the same amount of space**.
That idea comes back much later, when we work out the areas of shapes.

### Lines of symmetry

Fold a picture along a line. If the two halves land exactly on top of each other, the line is a **line of
symmetry** (also called a mirror line). Each half is the mirror image of the other.

- A **square** has 4 lines of symmetry: up the middle, across the middle, and both corner-to-corner lines.
- A **rectangle** that is not a square has only 2. Try folding a sheet of paper corner to corner: the halves do not
  match.
- A triangle with three equal sides has 3.
- A **circle** has endless lines of symmetry: any line through its centre works.

Many letters have a line of symmetry too. A, M, T and W have an up-and-down one. B, C, D and E have a
side-to-side one. H, I, O and X have both.

### Turning symmetry

Some shapes look the same after you **turn** them, without folding. Put a square on the table and turn it a quarter
of the way round: it looks exactly as before. That is **turning symmetry** (also called rotational symmetry). A
square fits back onto itself 4 times in one full turn. A plus sign does too. An S or a Z fits back onto itself
after half a turn, even though neither has a line of symmetry.

Nature is full of symmetry. A butterfly has a mirror line down its body. A snowflake has six arms and looks the
same after one sixth of a turn. A starfish usually has five arms.

## Worked example

**How many lines of symmetry does a regular hexagon have?** (Regular means all six sides are the same length and
all six corners are the same.)

1. Try lines that go **from a corner to the opposite corner**. A hexagon has 6 corners, and each line uses 2 of them,
   so there are 3 such lines. Fold along any one and the halves match.
2. Try lines that go **from the middle of a side to the middle of the opposite side**. There are 6 sides, and each
   line uses 2 of them, so there are 3 more.
3. Any other line leaves one half bigger than the other.
4. So a regular hexagon has 3 + 3 = **6** lines of symmetry.

Notice the pattern: the square has 4 sides and 4 lines, the equal-sided triangle has 3 sides and 3 lines, the
regular hexagon has 6 sides and 6 lines. For a regular polygon, the number of lines of symmetry is the same as the
number of sides.

## Try it

1. Which of these letters have a line of symmetry: F, H, K, N, U?
2. How many lines of symmetry does a regular octagon (like a stop sign, without the writing) have?
3. Which two tangram pieces can be put together to make the square piece?
4. Does the letter N have turning symmetry? Turn the page upside down to check.
5. Fold a paper in half once and cut a half-heart shape along the fold. What do you see when you open it?
6. Is every rectangle a square?

??? note "Answers"
    1. H (two lines), K (a side-to-side line) and U (an up-and-down line). F and N have none.
    2. 8. It is a regular polygon with 8 sides, so it has 8 lines: 4 from corner to opposite corner and 4 from the
       middle of a side to the middle of the opposite side.
    3. The two small triangles. Put their long sides together and they make a square.
    4. Yes. After half a turn, N looks exactly like N again. It has turning symmetry but no line of symmetry.
    5. A whole heart. The fold is its line of symmetry, and the paper made the mirror half for you.
    6. No. A rectangle only needs square corners. Its sides can be different lengths, like a door. A square is the
       special rectangle whose sides are all equal.

## Common mistake

**Thinking the corner-to-corner line of a rectangle is a line of symmetry.** It cuts the rectangle into two equal
triangles, so it looks as if it should work. But fold along it and the halves do not land on top of each other: the
corners stick out. Cutting a shape into two equal pieces is not the same as folding it onto itself. Always check
by imagining the fold.
