# Solving quadratic equations

*Algebra · level 7, lesson 3 of 7*

## A question to start

Find a positive number that is exactly 1 more than its own reciprocal (the reciprocal of $x$ is $\dfrac1x$).

Try 2: its reciprocal is 0.5, and 0.5 + 1 = 1.5, not 2. Try 1.5: $\dfrac{1}{1.5} + 1 \approx 1.667$. Try 1.6:
$\dfrac{1}{1.6} + 1 = 1.625$. The answer is somewhere near 1.62, but guessing will never pin it down exactly. Written
as an equation it is $x = 1 + \dfrac1x$, and multiplying by $x$ gives

$$x^2 = x + 1, \quad \text{or} \quad x^2 - x - 1 = 0.$$

It is a quadratic equation, and it does not factor with whole numbers. By the end of this lesson you will solve it
exactly. The answer is a famous number, the **golden ratio**.

## The idea

### What it means to solve

A **quadratic equation** is one that can be written $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$ with $a \ne 0$. Its solutions are called its
**roots**. On a graph, they are the zeros of the parabola $y = ax^2 + bx + c$: the $x$-values where it meets the
$x$-axis. A parabola can cross the axis twice, touch it once, or miss it, so a quadratic has two, one or no real roots.

There are three methods. All three start the same way: **get 0 on one side**.

### Method 1: factoring and the zero product property

**Zero product property:** if $A \times B = 0$, then $A = 0$ or $B = 0$ (or both).

Why? If $A$ is not 0, divide both sides by $A$: $B = \dfrac{0}{A} = 0$. So one of them must be 0. This is special to
**zero**. If $A \times B = 6$, you learn nothing about either: it could be $2 \times 3$ or $12 \times 0.5$ or
anything else.

So to solve $x^2 - x - 12 = 0$, factor: $(x - 4)(x + 3) = 0$. Then $x - 4 = 0$ or $x + 3 = 0$, so $x = 4$ or
$x = -3$.

### Method 2: completing the square

When there are no nice factors, write the left side as a perfect square plus a number (lesson 2), then take square
roots. Remember that a positive number has **two** square roots: if $u^2 = 7$ then $u = \sqrt7$ or $u = -\sqrt7$,
written $u = \pm\sqrt7$.

$$x^2 + 4x - 3 = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; (x + 2)^2 - 4 - 3 = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; (x + 2)^2 = 7 \;\Rightarrow\; x = -2 \pm \sqrt7.$$

### Method 3: the quadratic formula, derived

Do completing the square once, with letters, and you never have to do it again. Start with $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$,
$a \ne 0$.

Divide every term by $a$, and move the constant across:

$$x^2 + \frac{b}{a}x = -\frac{c}{a}.$$

Half of $\dfrac{b}{a}$ is $\dfrac{b}{2a}$. Add its square to both sides to complete the square:

$$x^2 + \frac{b}{a}x + \frac{b^2}{4a^2} = \frac{b^2}{4a^2} - \frac{c}{a}.$$

The left side is a perfect square. On the right, use the common denominator $4a^2$:

$$\left(x + \frac{b}{2a}\right)^2 = \frac{b^2 - 4ac}{4a^2}.$$

Take square roots (both of them), using $\sqrt{4a^2} = 2\lvert a \rvert$; the $\pm$ already covers both signs:

$$x + \frac{b}{2a} = \pm\frac{\sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}.$$

Subtract $\dfrac{b}{2a}$:

$$x = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}.$$

This is the **quadratic formula**. It solves every quadratic equation, and now you know where it comes from.

- $\Delta > 0$: $\sqrt{\Delta}$ is a positive number, and $+\sqrt\Delta$ and $-\sqrt\Delta$ give **two different
  roots**. The parabola crosses the axis twice.
- $\Delta = 0$: both choices give $x = -\dfrac{b}{2a}$, **one repeated root**. The parabola just touches the axis at
  its vertex.
- $\Delta < 0$: no real number squares to a negative, so there are **no real roots**. The parabola misses the axis.

The formula also shows that the two roots sit equally far either side of $x = -\dfrac{b}{2a}$: that is the axis of
symmetry, and the vertex formula promised in lesson 2.

### Which method to choose

- If it factors easily, factor: it is fastest and gives exact answers.
- If $b$ is even and $a = 1$, completing the square is quick.
- Otherwise, use the formula. It always works.

## Worked examples

**1. Factoring.** Solve $x^2 = x + 12$.

- Get 0 on one side: $x^2 - x - 12 = 0$.
- Two numbers with product $-12$ and sum $-1$: $-4$ and $3$. So $(x - 4)(x + 3) = 0$.
- $x = 4$ or $x = -3$. Check $x = -3$: $9 = -3 + 12$. ✓

**2. Factoring with $a \ne 1$.** Solve $6x^2 + x - 2 = 0$.

- $ac = 6 \times (-2) = -12$. Two numbers with product $-12$ and sum $1$: $4$ and $-3$.
- Split the middle: $6x^2 + 4x - 3x - 2 = 2x(3x + 2) - 1(3x + 2) = (2x - 1)(3x + 2)$.
- $x = \dfrac12$ or $x = -\dfrac23$.

**3. Completing the square.** Solve $x^2 + 4x - 3 = 0$.

- $(x + 2)^2 = 7$, so $x = -2 \pm \sqrt7$, that is $x \approx 0.646$ or $x \approx -4.646$.

**4. The formula.** Solve $2x^2 - 5x - 4 = 0$.

- $a = 2$, $b = -5$, $c = -4$. $\Delta = (-5)^2 - 4(2)(-4) = 25 + 32 = 57$.
- $x = \dfrac{5 \pm \sqrt{57}}{4}$, so $x \approx 3.137$ or $x \approx -0.637$.

**5. The golden ratio.** Solve $x^2 - x - 1 = 0$.

- $a = 1$, $b = -1$, $c = -1$. $\Delta = 1 + 4 = 5$.
- $x = \dfrac{1 \pm \sqrt5}{2}$. The positive root is $\dfrac{1 + \sqrt5}{2} \approx 1.618$, the golden ratio,
  usually called $\varphi$ (phi). Check: $\dfrac{1}{1.618} \approx 0.618$, and $0.618 + 1 = 1.618$. ✓

**6. A rectangle.** A rectangle has area 60 cm², and its length is 7 cm more than its width. Find the width.

- Width $w$, length $w + 7$: $w(w + 7) = 60$, so $w^2 + 7w - 60 = 0$, so $(w + 12)(w - 5) = 0$.
- $w = 5$ or $w = -12$. A width cannot be negative, so the width is **5 cm** (and the length 12 cm).

**7. Using the discriminant.** For what value of $k$ does $kx^2 + 4x + 1 = 0$ have exactly one root?

- One root when $\Delta = 0$: $16 - 4k = 0$, so $k = 4$. Then $4x^2 + 4x + 1 = (2x + 1)^2$, root $x = -\tfrac12$.

## Common mistake

**Using the zero product property when the right side is not 0.** From $(x - 2)(x - 3) = 6$ you can **not** say
$x - 2 = 6$ or $x - 3 = 6$. Expand and bring everything to one side first: $x^2 - 5x + 6 = 6$, so $x^2 - 5x = 0$, so
$x(x - 5) = 0$, giving $x = 0$ or $x = 5$.

**Dividing by the variable.** From $x^2 = 5x$, dividing by $x$ gives only $x = 5$ and loses $x = 0$. You may only
divide by something you know is not zero.

**Dividing only part of the formula by $2a$.** The whole of $-b \pm \sqrt{\Delta}$ is over $2a$, not just the
square root.
