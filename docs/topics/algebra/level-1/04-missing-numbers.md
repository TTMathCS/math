# Missing numbers

*Algebra · level 1, lesson 4 of 5*

## A question to start

Here is a game to play at the kitchen table. You need 5 counters (buttons, coins or dried beans) and a cup.

Count the 5 counters together. Then the other player closes their eyes. You hide **some** of the counters under the
cup and leave the rest out. The other player opens their eyes and sees this:

"There were 5 counters. I can see 3. How many are under the cup?"

Most children can answer this in a few seconds. The more interesting question is **how** they know. In this lesson
you will find three different ways, and learn to write the game as a number sentence with a gap in it.

## The idea

### The hidden number

The counters under the cup are a **mystery number**, just like the bag on the balance scale. You cannot see them, but
they are there, and there is exactly **one** right answer. The cup cannot hold 2 counters and 4 counters at the same
time.

We know two things:

- all together there are **5** counters (that is the **whole**), and
- we can see **3** of them (that is one **part**).

The counters under the cup are the **other part**. The two parts together make the whole.

### Writing it down

We can write the game as a number sentence. The 3 we can see, **plus** the ones under the cup, are the same amount as
**5**:

**3 + ? = 5**

The **?** is the cup. It stands for the number we do not know yet. Remember from the last lesson that = means "is the
same amount as". So the sentence says: "3 and the hidden counters is the same amount as 5."

Here is the same game as a picture of parts and a whole. The long bar is the whole. The two short bars under it are
the two parts. Together the parts are exactly as long as the whole.

### Way 1: count on

Start at the part you know, **3**, and count up to the whole, **5**. Hold up a finger for each number you say:

"**three**... four, five."

You said two more numbers, and two fingers are up. So **2** counters are under the cup.

**Why does this work?** Counting on from 3 is like adding the hidden counters one at a time. You keep adding until you
reach 5. The number of counters you added is the number that was hiding.

### Way 2: know the partners

In [Numbers](../../numbers/level-1/05-making-ten.md) you met **number partners**: two numbers that make another number.
The partners of 5 are 0 and 5, 1 and 4, **2 and 3**, 3 and 2, 4 and 1, 5 and 0. Which number goes with 3 to make 5?
It is **2**. If you know your partners well, you do not even need to count.

### Way 3: take away

Imagine all 5 counters again. The cup took some of them. If you take away the 3 that you can see, what is left must be
the ones under the cup:

**5 − 3 = 2**

So taking away gives the same answer. **Why?** The whole is the two parts put together. If you remove one part from
the whole, the other part is left. Adding and taking away are opposites: one puts parts together, the other pulls them
apart.

### Always check: lift the cup

When you have an answer, check it. Put your number in the gap and see if the sentence is true. For **3 + ? = 5** with
answer 2: 3 + 2 is 5, and 5 is the same as 5 ✓. Then lift the cup and count. If there are 2 counters, you were right.

The cup does not have to be second. Here it is first: the cup is on the left, and 2 counters are out, and all

**? + 2 = 6.** Adding in either order gives the same total, so this is the same puzzle as 2 + ? = 6. Count on from 2:
three, four, five, six. That is **4**. Check: 4 + 2 = 6 ✓.

### Taking away with a cup

Here is a different game. Start with 7 counters in a row. The other player closes their eyes. You put **some** of
them away under the cup. Now only 4 are left on the table.

**7 − ? = 4**

"7, take away the hidden ones, leaves 4." How many did the cup take? The cup took the ones that are not on the table.
Count up from 4 to 7: five, six, seven. That is **3**. Check: 7 − 3 = 4 ✓.

### Could the cup be empty?

Yes! If there were 5 counters and all 5 are still out, then the cup is hiding **0** counters. **5 + 0 = 5**, which is
true. Zero is a perfectly good answer. And if no counters are out at all, the cup is hiding all 5: **0 + 5 = 5**.

### Two cups, the same

Here is a harder game. There are 7 counters. Two cups each hide **the same number** of counters, and 1 counter is
left out. How many are under each cup?

First take away the 1 that is out: 7 − 1 = 6, so the two cups hide 6 counters between them. The two cups hide the
same number, so share 6 fairly between the two cups: 3 and 3. Each cup hides **3**. Check: 3 + 3 + 1 = 7 ✓. This is
the balance-scale idea again: take the same away from both sides, then share.

## Worked examples

**1. The starting game.** 5 counters, 3 out. **3 + ? = 5**. Count on from 3: four, five. That is 2. The cup hides
**2**. Check: 3 + 2 = 5 ✓.

**2. A bigger whole.** 9 counters, 6 out. **6 + ? = 9**. Count on from 6: seven, eight, nine. Three fingers are up,
so the cup hides **3**. Check: 6 + 3 = 9 ✓. Or by taking away: 9 − 6 = 3.

**3. Using a ten frame.** 10 counters, 7 out. **7 + ? = 10**. Put the 7 counters in a ten frame (a box of 10
squares in two rows of 5). Three squares are empty. The empty squares are where the hidden counters belong, so the cup
hides **3**. This is a partner of 10: 7 and 3.

**4. Cup first.** **? + 5 = 8**. The same as 5 + ? = 8. Count on from 5: six, seven, eight. That is **3**. Check: 3 + 5
= 8 ✓.

**5. Taking away.** **8 − ? = 6**. 8 counters, some hidden, 6 left. Count up from 6 to 8: seven, eight. The cup took
**2**. Check: 8 − 2 = 6 ✓.

## Try it

1. 4 counters in all, 1 out. How many under the cup? Write the number sentence.
2. **2 + ? = 6**
3. **? + 4 = 7**
4. **5 + ? = 10**
5. **6 − ? = 2**
6. **8 + ? = 8**
7. Ella says the answer to **4 + ? = 9** is 13, because 4 and 9 make 13. Check her answer. What is the right one?
8. 9 counters. Two cups hide the same number each, and 3 counters are out. How many are under each cup?

??? note "Answers"
    1. **3**. The sentence is **1 + ? = 4**. Count on from 1: two, three, four. That is 3. Check: 1 + 3 = 4 ✓.
    2. **4**. Count on from 2: three, four, five, six. That is 4. Check: 2 + 4 = 6 ✓.
    3. **3**. The same as 4 + ? = 7. Count on from 4: five, six, seven. That is 3. Check: 3 + 4 = 7 ✓.
    4. **5**. 5 and 5 are partners of 10 (a double). Check: 5 + 5 = 10 ✓.
    5. **4**. 6 counters, 2 left, so the cup took the rest. Count up from 2 to 6: three, four, five, six. That is 4.
       Check: 6 − 4 = 2 ✓.
    6. **0**. The whole is 8 and 8 are already out, so nothing is under the cup. Check: 8 + 0 = 8 ✓.
    7. Check: 4 + 13 is 17, not 9. So 13 is **wrong**. Ella added the two numbers she could see, but 9 is the
       **whole**, not a part. Count on from 4 to 9: five, six, seven, eight, nine. That is **5**. Check: 4 + 5 = 9 ✓.
    8. **3 each**. Take away the 3 that are out: 9 − 3 = 6 under the two cups together. Share 6 fairly between two
       cups: 3 and 3. Check: 3 + 3 + 3 = 9 ✓.

## Common mistake

**Adding the two numbers you can see.** In **4 + ? = 9**, the 9 is the **whole**, the number of counters all
together. It is not a part. Adding 4 and 9 gives a number bigger than the whole, which is impossible: the parts can
never be more than the whole. Before you answer, ask "which number is the whole?"

**Forgetting to check.** A check takes a few seconds: put your answer in the gap and see if both sides are the same.
It catches almost every mistake.
