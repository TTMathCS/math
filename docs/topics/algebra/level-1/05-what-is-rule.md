# What is the rule?

*Algebra · level 1, lesson 5 of 5*

## A question to start

group. The socks that do not follow the rule went into the NO group.

What is the secret rule? And here is a harder question: how could you make **sure** you have guessed it right? (It
is not as easy as it looks. The answer is in the worked examples.)

## The idea

### A secret rule

A **rule** is a way of deciding YES or NO for each thing. "Is it red?" is a rule. "Does it have 4 legs?" is a rule.
"Is the number bigger than 5?" is a rule. Each thing either follows the rule (YES) or does not (NO).

In the game **Guess my rule**, one player thinks of a rule and keeps it secret. Then they sort some things into a
YES group and a NO group. The other players look carefully and try to work out the rule.

This is a mystery game, just like "I'm thinking of a number". But now the mystery is not a number. The mystery is
the **rule**. Finding a hidden rule from examples is one of the most important things mathematicians (and
scientists) do.

### Look for what is the same, and what is different

Here is how a good detective finds a rule.

1. **Look at the YES group.** What do all the things in it have in common? Something is the same about all of them.
2. **Look at the NO group.** Which of those same things is missing in every NO thing?
3. **The rule is something every YES thing has and no NO thing has.**

In the socks, the YES socks are orange, blue and green. The NO socks are **also** orange, blue and green. So colour
cannot be the rule: there is an orange sock in YES and an orange sock in NO. What do all the YES socks have that none
of the NO socks has? **Stripes.** A good guess is: "Socks with stripes go in YES."

Look at the YES group: an orange, a blue and a green triangle. Look at the NO group: an orange circle, a blue square,
a green circle. Colour cannot be the rule (orange is in both groups). What do all the YES shapes have in common? They
are all **triangles**. And no NO shape is a triangle. So a good guess is: "Triangles go in YES."

### Number rules

Numbers can be sorted by secret rules too.

| YES | NO |
| --- | --- |
| 2, 4, 8, 10 | 1, 3, 7, 9 |

What do 2, 4, 8 and 10 have in common? Try to share each of them into two equal piles. 2 is 1 and 1. 4 is 2 and 2.
8 is 4 and 4. 10 is 5 and 5. Every one shares fairly. Now try the NO numbers: 3 cannot be shared into two equal piles
(one pile gets an extra). Nor can 1, 7 or 9. Numbers that share into two equal piles are called **even**. The rule is
"even numbers".

Other number rules children like: "bigger than 5", "has a 1 in it", "the numbers we say when we count by tens".

### Test your guess with a new thing

Once you have a guess, **test** it. Pick a new thing that is not sorted yet, and say which group your rule puts it
in. Then ask the rule-keeper. If they agree, your rule passed the test. If they disagree, your rule is wrong.

For the even numbers, you could test 6. Your rule says YES (3 and 3). If the rule-keeper says YES, your guess passed.

### Two rules can fit the same examples

Here is the surprise. Look at these.

| YES | NO |
| --- | --- |
| 10, 12, 14 | 1, 3, 5 |

Guess 1: "even numbers". 10, 12, 14 are even ✓, and 1, 3, 5 are not ✓. It fits!

Guess 2: "numbers bigger than 9". 10, 12, 14 are bigger than 9 ✓, and 1, 3, 5 are not ✓. It **also** fits!

Both guesses fit every example. So the examples cannot tell us which rule is the secret one. We need a **test** that
the two guesses disagree on. Try **2**. "Even" says YES (1 and 1). "Bigger than 9" says NO. Ask the rule-keeper about
2, and the answer tells you which guess to throw away. Testing 11 works too: "even" says NO, "bigger than 9" says YES.

But testing **7** would not help: both guesses say NO. A good test is one where your guesses disagree.

### Why one NO is stronger than many YESes

This is the deepest idea in this lesson, so read it slowly.

Suppose your guess is "even numbers", and you test 4, 6, 8, 20 and 100, and the rule-keeper says YES every time.
Does that prove your rule is right? **No!** The secret rule might be something different that happens to agree on
those numbers. (Maybe the secret rule is "even numbers, but not 50". You did not test 50.) Lots of tests that pass make
you **more sure**, but they never make you **completely** sure.

But suppose you test 6 and the rule-keeper says NO. Then your guess is **definitely wrong**, because your rule said
YES. Just **one** thing that breaks your rule is enough to throw it out. A thing like that is called a
**counterexample** ("an example against").

So: many passed tests make a guess **stronger**. One failed test makes it **wrong**. Mathematicians love this idea.
Later in math, to be completely sure a rule is always right, they write a **proof**: a reason that works for every
number at once, not only the ones they tried.

### A rule with two parts

Some rules have two parts that must **both** be true. "Orange **and** a circle." Then an orange triangle is NO (it is
orange, but not a circle), a blue circle is NO (a circle, but not orange), and only orange circles are YES. These rules
are harder to guess, so look at the NO things very carefully: each one is missing at least one part.

## Worked examples

**1. The socks.** YES: striped orange, striped blue, striped green. NO: plain orange, plain blue, plain green.

Colour is in both groups, so colour is not the rule. Every YES sock has stripes, and no NO sock does. Guess: "socks
with stripes". To make **sure**, you cannot. You can only test more socks. Show a striped red sock: the rule says YES.
Show a plain yellow sock: the rule says NO. If the rule-keeper agrees each time, your guess is looking good. (And if
they say "NO" to a striped sock with spots, maybe the rule was "stripes and no spots"!)

**2. A number rule.** YES: 6, 7, 8, 9. NO: 1, 2, 3, 4.

All the YES numbers are big, all the NO numbers are small. A guess: "bigger than 5". But "bigger than 4" also fits,
because 5 is not sorted yet. Test **5**. If 5 is YES, the rule is "bigger than 4". If 5 is NO, it could be "bigger
than 5". One test, and one of the guesses is gone.

**3. A counterexample.** Tom's rule guess is "all animals with 4 legs go in YES". The rule-keeper puts a dog and a cat
in YES. Then she puts a horse, which has 4 legs, in **NO**. The horse is a counterexample: Tom's rule said YES, but it
is NO. Tom's guess is wrong, even though the dog and the cat fitted. Maybe the rule is "pets that live in a house".

**4. A two-part rule.** YES: orange circle, small orange circle. NO: blue circle, orange square, green triangle.

"Circles"? No: the blue circle is a circle but is NO. "Orange"? No: the orange square is orange but is NO. "Orange
**and** a circle"? The YES things are both. The blue circle is not orange, the orange square is not a circle, the
green triangle is neither. ✓ Everything fits.

## Try it

1. YES: 1, 3, 5, 7. NO: 2, 4, 6, 8. What is a rule that fits?
2. YES: ball, orange, coin, wheel. NO: book, box, door. What is a rule that fits?
3. YES: 10, 20, 30, 40. NO: 15, 22, 7, 31. Guess the rule. Is 50 YES or NO with your rule?
4. YES: 11, 12, 13. NO: 2, 5, 9. Find **two** different rules that fit.
5. For your two rules in question 4, find a number they disagree on.
6. Jo's guess is "numbers with a 1 in them". The rule-keeper's YES group has 1, 10, 12, 21. Then the rule-keeper says
   that 31 is NO. What does that tell Jo?
7. Make a secret rule for the numbers 1 to 10. Sort them into YES and NO. Can someone at home guess it?

??? note "Answers"
    1. **"Odd numbers"**: the numbers that cannot be shared into two equal piles. Each YES number has one left over
       when you make pairs; each NO number makes pairs exactly.
    2. **"Round things"** (shaped like a circle or a ball). The book, box and door all have flat sides and corners.
    3. **"Count by tens"** (10, 20, 30, ...), or "numbers that end in 0". 15, 22, 7 and 31 do not. With this rule, 50
       is **YES**.
    4. Many answers. For example **"bigger than 10"** (11, 12, 13 are; 2, 5, 9 are not), and **"two-digit numbers"**
       (written with two digits). Another: "numbers that start with 1 and have two digits".
    5. "Bigger than 10" and "two-digit numbers" disagree on **10**: 10 is two digits (YES), but 10 is not bigger than
       10 (NO). Asking about 10 tells you which rule to keep. (Any good test is a number the two rules disagree on.)
    6. 31 has a 1 in it, so Jo's rule says YES, but the rule-keeper said NO. **31 is a counterexample**, so Jo's guess
       is **wrong**, even though 1, 10, 12 and 21 fitted. Maybe the rule is "numbers smaller than 30 with a 1 in
       them", or something else. Jo needs more tests.
    7. Any rule works if every number from 1 to 10 is sorted by it. For example "bigger than 7" gives YES: 8, 9, 10
       and NO: 1 to 7. A sneaky one: "you say it with one syllable" (YES: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 9, 10; NO: 7, which is
       "se-ven").

## Common mistake

**Thinking that a few examples that fit prove the rule.** If your rule fits every example so far, it is a good
guess, not a sure thing. Another rule may fit them too. Keep testing, and choose tests where your guesses disagree.

**Looking only at the YES group.** "They are all orange!" might be true of the YES group, but if there are orange
things in the NO group too, then "orange" cannot be the whole rule. Always check the NO group.
