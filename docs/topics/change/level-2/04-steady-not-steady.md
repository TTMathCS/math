# Steady and not steady

*Change and motion · level 2, lesson 4 of 6*

## A question to start

Take a sheet of paper and fold it in half. Now it is 2 layers thick. Fold it in half again: 4 layers. Again: 8 layers.

Each fold is just one more fold, the same little action every time. So does the paper get thicker by the **same
amount** with each fold?

Try it and count: 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32... After only 7 folds the paper is 128 layers thick, and it is so stiff that you
can hardly fold it again. Something is going on here that is very different from adding the same amount each time. In
this lesson you will learn to tell the two kinds of change apart: **steady** change and change that is **not
steady**.

## The idea

### Steady change: the same amount each time

A bathtub has its plug in, and the tap is on. Every minute, the tap adds **2 litres** of water. (A litre is the amount
in a big milk carton.)

| Minutes | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Water in the tub (litres) | 0 | 2 | 4 | 6 | 8 | 10 |
| Change each minute | | up 2 | up 2 | up 2 | up 2 | up 2 |

The change row says the same thing every time: **up 2**. That is what **steady** means: the **same change in every
equal step of time**. Another word for it is **constant** (it does not vary).

You can predict a steady change very easily. After 6 minutes: 12 litres. After 10 minutes: count by twos ten times,
20 litres. You just keep adding the same amount.

### Not steady: the snowball

Now think about rolling a snowball across a snowy garden to make a snowman. Each time you roll it all the way across
the garden, it picks up more snow. Here is a pretend snowball's weight after each trip across:

| Trips across | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Weight (kg) | 1 | 2 | 4 | 7 | 11 | 16 |
| Change each trip | | up 1 | up 2 | up 3 | up 4 | up 5 |

The change row is **not** the same each time. The snowball gains **more** on every trip: 1, then 2, then 3, then 4,
then 5. This change is **not steady**. It is **speeding up**, growing faster and faster.

**Why does the snowball grow faster and faster?** A snowball picks up snow with its **outside**, the part that
touches the ground. A small snowball has a small outside, so it picks up only a little. A big snowball has a big
outside, so it picks up a lot. The bigger it gets, the faster it grows. That is the secret of every change that
speeds up like this: **the more there is, the more gets added.**

- The tub's dots (blue) lie on a **straight line**. A straight line has the same steepness everywhere, because it
  goes up the same amount at every step.
- The snowball's dots (orange) make a **curve** that bends upwards. It gets steeper and steeper, because it goes up
  more at every step.

So here is a rule you can use for life: **steady change makes a straight line; change that speeds up makes a curve
that bends up.**

Look at something surprising in the chart. At the start, the snowball is **ahead** (1 kg against 0 litres). At steps
1 and 2 they are level. After that, the snowball races away. Something that grows faster and faster will always,
in the end, overtake something that grows steadily, however far behind it starts.

### Back to the paper folds

Each fold **doubles** the layers: 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 128.

| Folds | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Layers | 1 | 2 | 4 | 8 | 16 | 32 | 64 | 128 |
| Change each fold | | up 1 | up 2 | up 4 | up 8 | up 16 | up 32 | up 64 |

The change row grows even faster than the snowball's: it **doubles** too. Why? A fold puts a copy of all the layers
on top of themselves. So the number of new layers added is always **the same as the number already there**. The more
layers there are, the more get added. Just like the snowball, but even stronger.

### Not steady the other way: slowing down

Change can also be not steady because it **slows down**. A young tree grows a lot each year when it is small, and
less each year when it is old. A cup of hot cocoa cools fast at first and then more slowly (you saw this in lesson
3). The change row gets **smaller** each time, and the dots make a curve that **bends over** and flattens out.

So there are three kinds to look for:

| Change row | Name | Picture |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The same each time | **Steady** | Straight line |
| Bigger each time | **Speeding up** (growing faster) | Curve bending up |
| Smaller each time | **Slowing down** | Curve bending over, flattening out |

## Worked examples

**Example 1. Pocket money.** Zara gets 3 dollars every week and saves all of it. She starts with nothing. Is this
steady? How much after 6 weeks?

1. Each week adds 3 dollars: 3, 6, 9, 12, 15, 18.
2. The change is up 3 every week: **steady**.
3. After 6 weeks: **18 dollars**.

**Example 2. A growing stack.** A pattern of squares has 1 square, then 3, then 6, then 10, then 15. Is it steady?

1. Changes: 1 to 3 is up 2; 3 to 6 is up 3; 6 to 10 is up 4; 10 to 15 is up 5.
2. The changes get bigger: **not steady, speeding up**.
3. Next: up 6, so 15 + 6 = **21**.

**Example 3. A candle.** A candle is 20 cm long. Every hour it burns down 2 cm. Steady or not?

1. 20, 18, 16, 14, ... The change is down 2 every hour.
2. That is **steady** (steady going down). On a chart it makes a straight line going down.
3. It will be gone after 10 hours, because 10 lots of 2 cm is 20 cm.

**Example 4. Telling them apart from a table.** Which of these are steady?

- A: 5, 10, 15, 20, 25. Changes: 5, 5, 5, 5. **Steady.**
- B: 2, 4, 8, 16, 32. Changes: 2, 4, 8, 16. **Speeding up** (doubling).
- C: 50, 40, 32, 26, 22. Changes: down 10, down 8, down 6, down 4. **Not steady, slowing down.**
- D: 30, 27, 24, 21, 18. Changes: down 3 each time. **Steady** (going down).

## Try it

1. A tap fills a bucket with 3 litres every minute. Write how much water there is after 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5 minutes. Is
   it steady?
2. Find the change row for 4, 7, 10, 13, 16. Is it steady?
3. Find the change row for 1, 3, 7, 13, 21. Is it steady? What comes next?
4. A plant is 10 cm, then 15 cm, then 18 cm, then 20 cm, then 21 cm. Is its growth speeding up or slowing down?
5. A rumour: on day 1 one child knows a secret. Every day, every child who knows tells one new child. How many know on
   day 2, 3, 4, 5? Is this steady?
6. Which makes a straight line on a dot chart: 2, 5, 8, 11 or 2, 4, 8, 16?
7. Sam saves 10 dollars a week. Mia starts with 1 dollar and doubles her money every week. Who has more after 3
   weeks? After 6 weeks?
8. Why does a snowball grow faster as it gets bigger?

??? note "Answers"
    1. **3, 6, 9, 12, 15 litres**. The change is up 3 every minute: **steady**.
    2. Changes: 3, 3, 3, 3. **Steady.**
    3. Changes: 2, 4, 6, 8. **Not steady** (speeding up). The next change is 10, so **31** comes next.
    4. Changes: up 5, up 3, up 2, up 1. They get smaller: **slowing down**.
    5. Each child who knows tells one more, so the number doubles: day 1: 1, day 2: **2**, day 3: **4**, day 4: **8**,
       day 5: **16**. **Not steady**: it speeds up, like the paper folds.
    6. **2, 5, 8, 11**: the change is up 3 every time, so it is steady and makes a straight line.

    8. A snowball picks up snow with its outside. A bigger snowball has a bigger outside touching the snow, so it
       picks up more on each roll.

## Common mistake

**Looking at the numbers instead of the changes.** The numbers 1, 2, 4, 7, 11 all go up, and so do 2, 4, 6, 8, 10.
To tell steady from not steady, you must look at the **change row**, not the numbers themselves.

**Thinking that "going down" means "not steady".** A candle burning down 2 cm every hour is steady. Steady means the
change is the same each time, whether it is up or down.
