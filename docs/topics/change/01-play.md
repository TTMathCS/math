# Play: Fast and slow

*Change and motion · step 1 of 5*

## A question to start

Mia and Leo have a race, but they cannot agree on the track.

- Mia runs **100 metres** and takes **20 seconds**.
- Leo runs **50 metres** and takes **9 seconds**.

Who is faster? Mia ran further, and Leo took less time, so it is not obvious. You cannot just look at one number.
On this page you will find a fair way to compare, and then use it on things as slow as a robot on Mars and as fast
as a rocket.

## The idea

### Fast means a lot of distance in a little time

Being **fast** means covering a lot of ground without taking much time. So speed always needs **two** things: how
far you went, and how long it took. One number on its own can trick you.

### To compare fairly, make one thing the same

The easiest way to compare two movers is to make either the **distance** or the **time** the same for both.

- **Same distance.** Who would finish 100 metres first? Leo ran 50 metres in 9 seconds. If he kept going at the
  same pace, the next 50 metres would take another 9 seconds, so 100 metres would take him 18 seconds. Mia took 20.
  **Leo is faster.**
- **Same time.** How far does each go in one second? This is what the word **speed** usually means: how far
  you go in one unit of time, like one second or one hour. A car going "100 kilometres an hour" would go 100
  kilometres if it kept that speed for one whole hour.

### Speed can change

Almost nothing moves at the same speed all the time. A car starts at zero, speeds up, slows down at a red light,
stops, and starts again. A ball you throw up slows down, stops for a moment at the top, and speeds up on the way
down. When we say "the car went 60 kilometres in an hour", we are really talking about its speed on **average**;
at some moments it was faster and at some moments slower.

You can tell if something is **speeding up**: in each second it goes further than in the second before. If it goes
less far each second, it is **slowing down**.

### Steepness is a kind of speed too

Hills and ramps change height as you move along them. A **steep** ramp goes up a lot while you move only a little
bit forward. A **gentle** ramp goes up only a little.

Ramp A climbs 2 squares for every 1 square along. Ramp B climbs only 1 square for every 2 squares along. Ramp A is
much steeper. Later in this topic, steepness and speed turn out to be the same idea: speed is how quickly
*distance* changes as time goes on, and steepness is how quickly *height* changes as you go along.

### How fast are things?

Some real speeds, to get a feel for them:

- A person walking slowly covers about **1 metre** each second.
- NASA's Curiosity rover on Mars drives at about **4 centimetres** each second at most. A slow walk is about 25
  times faster. The rover is slow on purpose: it is far from any mechanic, and it must not hit a rock it has not
  seen.
- To leave Earth and never fall back, a rocket must reach about **11 kilometres every second**. That is fast
  enough to cross a big city in a few seconds. To leave Mars, which is smaller and pulls less strongly, about
  **5 kilometres every second** is enough.

## Worked example

**A bike ride.** Ria writes down how far she has ridden at the end of each minute.

| Minute | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Total distance (metres) | 200 | 450 | 750 | 900 | 900 |

What happened in each minute?

1. **Minute 1:** from 0 to 200 metres. She rode **200 metres**.
2. **Minute 2:** from 200 to 450. She rode **250 metres**, more than the first minute, so she **sped up**.
3. **Minute 3:** from 450 to 750. She rode **300 metres**. Faster again.
4. **Minute 4:** from 750 to 900. Only **150 metres**. She **slowed down**, maybe going up a hill.
5. **Minute 5:** from 900 to 900. **No distance at all.** She stopped.

The trick is to look at the **changes** between the numbers, not the numbers themselves. The total distance only
ever goes up, but the speed went up and then down. Her fastest minute was minute 3.

## Try it

1. Ana walks 6 kilometres in 2 hours. Ben walks 9 kilometres in 3 hours. Who walks faster?
2. A toy car goes 30 centimetres every second. How far does it go in 5 seconds?
3. Sam rides his bike 2 kilometres in the first 10 minutes, and 1 kilometre in the next 10 minutes. Did he speed
   up or slow down?
4. Ramp C goes up 3 for every 6 along. Ramp D goes up 2 for every 3 along. Which is steeper?
5. The Curiosity rover drives 4 centimetres every second. How far can it go in one minute without stopping?

??? note "Answers"
    1. **The same.** Ana walks 3 kilometres each hour (6 split into 2 hours). Ben also walks 3 kilometres each
       hour (9 split into 3 hours).
    2. 30 + 30 + 30 + 30 + 30 = **150 centimetres**, which is 1.5 metres.
    3. He **slowed down**: he went less far in the second 10 minutes than in the first.
    4. Make the "along" the same. Ramp C goes up 3 in 6 along. Ramp D goes up 2 in 3 along, so in 6 along it goes
       up 4. Going up 4 beats going up 3, so **ramp D is steeper**.
    5. A minute is 60 seconds, so 60 lots of 4 centimetres: **240 centimetres**, which is 2.4 metres. About the
       length of a sofa and a half.

## Common mistake

**Thinking the one who went further was faster.** Going further only means faster if the time was the same. A
snail that crawls all day can go further than a cheetah that runs for two seconds. Always ask both questions:
*how far?* and *how long?*
