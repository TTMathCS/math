# Changes that come back

*Change and motion · level 1, lesson 5 of 6*

## A question to start

Every evening the sky gets dark. Every morning it gets light again. You have seen this happen every single day of
your life. So here is a strange question: how do you **know** that the Sun will come up tomorrow?

Nobody has seen tomorrow yet. But you are completely sure. In this lesson you will find out what kind of change
makes you so sure, and why it lets you **predict** the future, which means to say what will happen before it happens.

## The idea

### Changes that go one way, and changes that come back

In lesson 1 you saw changes that go **one way**: a puppy grows into a dog and never becomes a puppy again. A candle
burns down and never grows back.

Some changes are different. They change, and change, and then **come back** to where they started, and then do it
all again. Grown-ups call this a **cycle** (say "SY-kul"), like the wheels of a bicycle that go round and round.

Day, night, day, night... It is like a **repeating pattern** of beads on a string: the same thing over and over.

**Why does it happen?** Our Earth is a giant ball that **spins**, like a top, all the time. It turns around once
every day. When your side of the Earth faces the Sun, it is day. As the Earth keeps turning, your side turns away from
the Sun, and it is night. Then it turns back to face the Sun, and it is morning again.

You can try this with a lamp and an orange. Stick a sticker on the orange to be "you". Turn the orange slowly in front
of the lamp. The sticker goes into the light (day), round to the dark side (night), and back into the light (the next
day).

So the answer to the question at the start: you know the Sun will "come up" tomorrow because the Earth keeps spinning,
the same way it has spun for a very, very long time. The pattern repeats, and a repeating pattern lets you predict.

**On Mars**, there is day and night too, because Mars spins as well. A day on Mars is called a **sol**, and it is a
little longer than our day: about 40 minutes longer.

### The seasons

Another cycle is the **seasons**: winter, spring, summer, autumn (also called fall), and then winter again.

The seasons take a whole **year** to go round once. A year is a long time: 365 days (366 in a leap year). In many
places:

- **winter** is cold, and the days are short (it gets dark early);
- **spring** gets warmer, and plants start to grow;
- **summer** is the warmest, and the days are long;
- **autumn** gets cooler, and many trees drop their leaves.

Then winter comes back, and it all happens again. (In some warm places near the middle of the Earth, the seasons are
more like a "rainy season" and a "dry season". They still come back every year.)

**Why does it happen?** The Earth travels around the Sun in a big loop, and one trip around takes one year. The Earth
is also a little **tilted**, like a top leaning over. For part of the year your half of the Earth leans towards the
Sun and gets more sunshine: summer. For the other part it leans away: winter.

**On Mars** there are seasons too, because Mars is tilted like the Earth. But Mars takes much longer to go around the
Sun, almost two of our years, so each Mars season lasts almost twice as long as ours.

First you can hardly see the Moon at all (a **new moon**). Then a thin curved sliver appears, like a fingernail (a
**crescent**). Night after night the bright part grows, to a **half moon**, then nearly round, then a **full moon**,
a bright round circle. Then it shrinks again in the same way, back to new. The whole cycle takes about **a month**,
just over 29 days.

(The picture shows how it looks from the northern half of the Earth, where Canada is. From the southern half, like
Australia, the Moon looks flipped: it grows from the left.)

**Why does it happen?** The Moon does not really change shape. It is always a round ball. The Sun always lights up
half of it, the half facing the Sun. As the Moon travels around the Earth, we see more or less of that lit-up half.
When the Moon is between us and the Sun, its lit side faces away from us: new moon. When the Moon is on the far side
of us from the Sun, we see all of its lit side: full moon.

### Why cycles let you predict

All three cycles have something in common: the same changes happen **in the same order**, again and again, and each
round takes about the same time. So once you know the cycle, you can predict:

- If it is night now, then **day** comes next.
- If it is spring now, then **summer** comes next.
- If the Moon is full tonight, then in about two weeks it will be **new**, and in about four weeks it will be **full**
  again.

That is the power of a cycle. It is a pattern that goes round in a loop, and patterns can be continued.

### Small cycles all around you

Cycles are everywhere, not only in the sky:

- a **swing** goes forward, back, forward, back;
- a **traffic light** goes green, yellow (amber), red, green, ...;
- your **heart** beats: squeeze, rest, squeeze, rest;
- you breathe **in, out, in, out**;
- a **week** goes Monday, Tuesday, ..., Sunday, and then Monday again.

## Worked examples

**Example 1. What comes next?** The traffic light is green, then yellow, then red. What comes after red?

1. The traffic light is a cycle: green, yellow, red, and back to the start.
2. After red comes **green**.

**Example 2. Days of the week.** Today is Friday. What day will it be in 3 days?

1. Count forward: Saturday (1), Sunday (2), Monday (3).
2. It will be **Monday**. The week went round its loop and started again.

**Example 3. Seasons.** It is summer now. What season will it be in two seasons?

1. After summer comes autumn (1 season), then winter (2 seasons).
2. **Winter**.

**Example 4. Growing up is not a cycle.** Is "baby, child, grown-up" a cycle?

1. A cycle comes back to the start.
2. A grown-up does not turn back into a baby.
3. So **no**, growing up goes one way. It is not a cycle. (But here is a nice idea: a grown-up can have a baby of
   their own, so in a family, babies come again and again!)

## Try it

1. It is morning now. What comes after day?
2. Name the four seasons in order, starting with spring.
3. Tonight the Moon is a thin crescent and getting bigger. In about a week, will it be bigger or smaller?
4. Today is Wednesday. What day was it yesterday? What day will it be tomorrow?
5. Which of these are cycles, and which go one way? A swing going back and forth; an apple getting eaten; the
   seasons; a candle burning down; the days of the week.
6. Name one cycle in your own day (something you do again and again in the same order).
7. On Mars, a day (a sol) is about 40 minutes longer than on Earth. If you lived on Mars, would you get more or less
   time each day than on Earth?
8. Grown-up question: why do we always see the same face of the Moon, but different amounts of it lit up?

??? note "Answers"
    1. **Evening and then night**, and then day again.
    2. **Spring, summer, autumn (fall), winter**.
    3. **Bigger**. A growing crescent becomes a half moon in about a week.
    4. Yesterday was **Tuesday**. Tomorrow will be **Thursday**.
    5. **Cycles**: the swing, the seasons, the days of the week. **One way**: the apple getting eaten, the candle
       burning down.
    6. Many answers, for example: wake up, eat breakfast, go to school, come home, have dinner, sleep, and then again
       the next day.
    7. **More** time: about 40 minutes extra every sol.
    8. The Moon turns around once in the same time it takes to go around the Earth, so the same side always faces us.
       But the Sun lights it from different directions as it travels, so the lit part we see changes.

## Common mistake

**Thinking the Moon really changes shape, or that the Earth's shadow makes the phases.** The Moon is always a round
ball, half lit by the Sun. We just see different amounts of its lit half. (The Earth's shadow only falls on the Moon
in a lunar eclipse, which is rare.)

**Calling every repeat a cycle.** A cycle comes back to **the same place** and then goes round **in the same order**.
Growing up, a candle, or a melting snowman do not come back.
