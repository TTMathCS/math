Your job is to tidy them into groups. How would you do it?

Some children put all the orange ones together, all the blue ones together and all the green ones together. Some
children put the big ones in one group and the small ones in another. One child looks very closely and says, "Some
have two holes and some have four holes!"

Who is right? Here is the surprise: **all of them**. The same ten buttons can be sorted in many different ways. And
each way tells you something different about the buttons.

Sorting is where statistics begins. **Statistics** is the part of math that helps us learn from lots of facts, like
"which colour of button do we have most of?" Before you can answer a question like that, you have to sort.

## The idea

### What sorting means

To **sort** means to put things into groups, so that the things in each group are alike in some way.

The way they are alike is called the **rule**. "Put the same colours together" is a rule. "Put the big ones here and
the small ones there" is another rule.

When you sort, you choose **one thing to look at** and you pay no attention to everything else. If your rule is
colour, then size does not matter. A big orange button and a small orange button go in the same group, because they
are both orange.

### Two jobs for a good rule

A rule for sorting has two jobs to do.

1. **Every button must go in a group.** None left over on the table.
2. **Every button must go in only one group.** No button can be in two groups at once.

Here is a check you can do every time. Count each group, and add the counts together. You should get back the number
you started with.

- Colour: 5 and 3 make 8, and 2 more make **10**.
- Size: 4 and 6 make **10**.
- Holes: 5 and 5 make **10**.

There were 10 buttons in the pile. Every sorting gives back 10, so no button was lost and no button was counted
twice. This is a check that people who work with data use all the time, even with millions of things.

Every button has exactly one colour and exactly one size, so every button fits in exactly one box. Now we can see
things that the other sortings hid. For example:

- There are 2 big orange buttons and 3 small orange buttons.
- There is only 1 big blue button.
- Every colour has at least one big button and at least one small button.

A grid like this is a very powerful idea. When you are older you will call it a **table**, and grown-up
scientists use tables to sort many things at once.

### Yes or no sorting

The easiest rule of all asks a **yes or no** question. "Is it blue?" Every button is either blue (yes) or not blue
(no). So there are just two groups:

- **Yes, blue**: 3 buttons.
- **No, not blue**: 7 buttons.

3 and 7 make 10. Every button is in exactly one group, so this rule always does both of its jobs. Yes-or-no rules are
always safe.

### Why we sort

Sorting turns a jumble into something you can understand. Look back at the messy pile at the top of the page. Could
you tell, just by looking, whether there were more orange buttons or more blue ones? It was hard. After sorting, it is
easy: 5 orange, 3 blue.

Scientists sort things all the time. People who study rocks sort them by colour, by size, and by how they were made.
When a rover on Mars takes photos of rocks, scientists on Earth sort those rocks too, to learn how each one formed.
Sorting is the first step to understanding.

## Worked examples

**Example 1. Sorting toys.** Mia has these toys: a ball, a toy car, a teddy bear, a toy truck, a doll, a toy bus.
Sort them with the rule "has wheels" or "has no wheels".

1. Look at each toy, one at a time, and ask: does it have wheels?
2. Ball: no. Car: yes. Teddy bear: no. Truck: yes. Doll: no. Bus: yes.
3. **Has wheels**: car, truck, bus. That is 3 toys.
4. **No wheels**: ball, teddy bear, doll. That is 3 toys.
5. Check: 3 and 3 make 6. Mia has 6 toys. None lost.

**Example 2. A rule that does not work.** Ben sorts the buttons into two groups: "orange buttons" and "big
buttons". What goes wrong?

1. Pick up the big orange button. Is it orange? Yes. Is it big? Yes.
2. So it belongs in **both** groups! Where should Ben put it? He cannot put it in two places.
3. Now pick up a small blue button. Is it orange? No. Is it big? No.
4. So it belongs in **neither** group! It is left over on the table.

Ben's rule breaks both jobs. The fix is to choose **one** thing to look at. Sort by colour (orange, blue, green), or
sort by size (big, small), but not a mix of the two. Or use the grid, which looks at both things in a careful way.

## Try it

1. Sort these animals with the rule "can fly" or "cannot fly": a bird, a dog, a bee, a fish, a butterfly, a cat.
2. In the colour sort of the buttons, which colour had the most buttons? Which had the fewest?
3. Sort these by shape: a coin, a book, a plate, a door, a clock, a window. Use the rule "round" or "has corners".
4. Max sorted his socks into "striped" and "red". Why is that a bad rule? Think about a red striped sock.
5. A box of crayons has 4 red, 2 yellow and 3 blue crayons. You sort them by colour. How many groups do you get?
   How many crayons are there altogether?
6. Look at the sorting grid of buttons. How many small buttons are blue? How many big buttons are there altogether?
7. Sort the numbers 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8 with the yes-or-no rule "Can I share it into two equal piles?"
8. Think of three different ways to sort the children in your class or family. (Be kind: choose rules nobody minds.)

    1. **Can fly**: bird, bee, butterfly (3). **Cannot fly**: dog, fish, cat (3). 3 and 3 make 6 animals.
    2. Orange had the most (5). Green had the fewest (2).
    3. **Round**: coin, plate, clock. **Has corners**: book, door, window.
    4. A red striped sock fits in **both** groups, so Max does not know where to put it. And a plain blue sock fits in
       **neither** group. A better rule looks at one thing: "striped or not striped", or "sort by colour".
    5. 3 groups: red, yellow, blue. Altogether 4 and 2 make 6, and 3 more make **9** crayons.

       or winter". Check each rule: does every person fit in exactly one group?

## Common mistake

**Mixing two rules at once.** "Red ones here, big ones there" does not work, because a big red thing belongs in
both groups and a small blue thing belongs in neither. Choose one thing to look at. If you really want to look at two
things, use a grid with a box for each pair, like "red and big".

**Thinking there is only one right way to sort.** There are many right ways. A sorting is right when every thing goes
in exactly one group. Different rules answer different questions.
