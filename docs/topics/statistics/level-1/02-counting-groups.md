Many people say "more bananas!" The bananas are big and they are spread all across the page. The grapes are small
and squeezed into a corner.

Now count. Bananas: 1, 2, 3. Grapes: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6.

There are **more grapes**. Six grapes, and only three bananas. Our eyes were tricked, because big things and
spread-out things **look** like more. Counting does not get tricked. That is why people who work with data always
count.

## The idea

### Count each group

In the last lesson we sorted ten buttons by colour. Now we count each group. Touch each button once and say one
number for each button. The last number you say is how many are in the group.

- Orange: 1, 2, 3, 4, **5**.
- Blue: 1, 2, **3**.
- Green: 1, **2**.

Write the number under each group, so you do not have to count again. These numbers are called **counts**. A count
tells you how many things are in a group.

### Which group has the most?

Once every group has a number, you can compare them. The group with the biggest number has the **most**. The group
with the smallest number has the **fewest**.

- 5 is bigger than 3, and bigger than 2. So **orange** has the most.
- 2 is smaller than 3, and smaller than 5. So **green** has the fewest.

"Most" and "fewest" are two of the first questions anyone asks about data. Which fruit do most children like? Which
day had the fewest sunny hours? You answer them by sorting and then counting.

### Comparing by lining up

There is a second way to compare two groups, and it works even before you can count very far. **Line them up**, one
next to one.

Put the orange buttons in a row. Put the blue buttons in a row just under them, one blue under each orange. Start both
rows at the same place, and space them out the same.

Each blue button has an orange partner, shown by the dashed lines. But two orange buttons have **no partner**. They
are left over. So there are more orange buttons, and the leftovers tell you **how many more**: 2 more.

This is a very old and very clever idea. You do not need to know any numbers to see which row is longer, as long as
the rows start at the same place and the things are spaced the same. In the next lesson you will see that a **picture
graph** is exactly this: rows of things, lined up so they can be compared.

### The same

Sometimes two groups have the same count. Then neither one has more. When we sorted the buttons by holes, we got 5
with two holes and 5 with four holes. If you line them up, every button has a partner and none are left over. We say
the groups are **the same** size, or **equal**.

### A group with nothing in it

Suppose your rule was colour, and someone asks "How many purple buttons?" There are none. The count for purple is
**zero**, which we write **0**. Zero is a real answer. It tells you something: no purple buttons at all. When you sort,
a group can be empty, and its count is 0.

### Count them all: the total

Add up all the counts and you get the **total**: the number of things altogether.

- Colour: 5 and 3 make 8, and 2 more make **10**.

Now count the whole pile, all at once, without sorting: 1, 2, 3, ..., 10. Also **10**.

The two answers must be the same, because sorting does not make buttons appear or disappear. This is your **total
check**. If the two numbers are different, something went wrong: a button was counted twice, or a button was missed.
Then you count again, slowly. Checking the total is one of the most useful habits in all of statistics.

### Counting with a list

When the groups get bigger, it helps to write the counts in a little list, like this:

| Colour | How many |
| --- | --- |
| orange | 5 |
| blue | 3 |
| green | 2 |
| purple | 0 |
| **all together** | **10** |

This is a **table**. Each line is called a **row**. One row for each group, and a last row for the total. A table
keeps your counts safe, so you can look at them again tomorrow.

## Worked examples

**Example 1. A box of crayons.** A box holds these crayons: red, blue, red, yellow, blue, red, green, red, blue.
How many of each colour? Which colour is there most of, and which fewest?

1. Sort them: put each colour in its own pile.
2. Count each pile. Red: 1, 2, 3, **4**. Blue: 1, 2, **3**. Yellow: **1**. Green: **1**.
3. Most: red, with 4.
4. Fewest: yellow and green are **both** fewest, with 1 each. When two groups tie, say both.
5. Total check: 4 and 3 make 7, and 1 more is 8, and 1 more is **9**. Count the crayons in the list: there are 9.
   Good, none missed.

**Example 2. Lining up cars and trucks.** Leo has a row of toy cars and a row of toy trucks. He lines them up,
one truck under each car.

1. Each car has a truck partner, but some trucks have no car partner.
2. So there are **more trucks** than cars.
3. Count the trucks with no partner: 3 are left over. So there are **3 more** trucks than cars.
4. Check by counting: cars: 4. Trucks: 7. From 4, count up to 7: 5, 6, 7. That is 3 more. Both ways agree.

**Example 3. The total check finds a mistake.** Sara sorted 12 shells into three groups. She wrote: white 5, pink 4,
brown 4.

1. Add up her counts: 5 and 4 make 9, and 4 more make 13.
2. But there were only **12** shells! 13 is one too many.
3. So one group has a mistake. Sara counts each group again, slowly, touching each shell. She finds that pink has
   only 3. She had counted one pink shell twice.
4. New counts: white 5, pink 3, brown 4. Total: 5 and 3 make 8, and 4 more make **12**. Now it matches.

Notice that the total check does not tell you **which** group is wrong. It only tells you that **something** is
wrong. That is still very useful: it tells you to look again.

## Try it

1. Count the groups: a bag holds 3 red marbles, 6 blue marbles and 4 yellow marbles. Which colour is there most
   of? Which colour is there fewest of?
2. In the bag from question 1, how many marbles are there altogether?
3. A row of 6 apples is lined up above a row of 4 pears, one pear under each apple. How many apples have no partner?
   Are there more apples or more pears, and how many more?
4. You sort your toys into "has wheels" and "no wheels". You count 0 toys with wheels. What does that tell you?
5. Ana sorted 10 leaves: green 4, yellow 3, red 4. Do the total check. What do you find?
6. A group of children sorted themselves by the month they were born. January had 2 children, February had 2, and
   March had 2. Which month had the most?
7. Here are some animals at a farm: cow, hen, hen, pig, cow, hen, duck, hen, pig. Sort them, count each group, and
   make a table with a total row.

??? note "Answers"
    1. Blue has the most (6). Red has the fewest (3).
    2. 3 and 6 make 9, and 4 more make **13** marbles.
    3. 2 apples have no partner. There are **more apples**, **2 more**. Check: from 4 count up to 6: 5, 6. That is 2.
    4. You have **no** toys with wheels. Every toy is in the "no wheels" group. A count of 0 is still an answer.
    5. 4 and 3 make 7, and 4 more make **11**. But there were only 10 leaves. One leaf was counted twice (or put in
       two groups). Ana needs to count again.
    6. None of them. All three months have **the same** count, 2. They are equal, so no month has the most.
    7. Cow: 2. Hen: 4. Pig: 2. Duck: 1.

        | Animal | How many |
        | --- | --- |
        | cow | 2 |
        | hen | 4 |
        | pig | 2 |
        | duck | 1 |
        | **all together** | **9** |

        Check: there are 9 animals in the list, and 2 and 4 and 2 and 1 make 9. Hens are the most, ducks the fewest.

## Common mistake

**Deciding "more" by how big the pile looks.** Three big bananas spread out can look like more than six small
grapes squeezed together. Size and spreading out trick the eye. To compare fairly, **count**, or line the things up
one next to one, starting at the same place.

**Forgetting the total check.** If your counts add up to more or less than the number of things you started with,
something went wrong. Always add the counts and compare.
