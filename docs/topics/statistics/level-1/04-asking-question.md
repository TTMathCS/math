# Asking a question

*Statistics and data · level 1, lesson 4 of 5*

## A question to start

A teacher wants to make a picture graph of the class's favourite pets. She asks, "What is your favourite pet?" Here
are some of the answers she gets:

- "My grandma's dog, Rex!"
- "Fluffy ones."
- "A dinosaur!"
- "I like cats and dogs and also horses."
- "Dog."
- "Puppies!"

Try to make a picture graph from those answers. Is "Rex" the same as "dog"? Is "puppies" the same as "dog"? Where
does "fluffy ones" go? Where does the child who likes three pets put a sticker?

It is very hard. The answers are all good answers, but they **do not sort**. In this lesson you learn how to ask a
question so that the answers sort themselves, and every child's answer can be counted.

## The idea

### Data starts with a question

The answers people give you are called **data**. All the data in this level, the stickers and the counts, came from
somebody asking a question. A good question gives data that you can sort and count. A muddled question gives a
muddle.

Asking the same question to lots of people, and writing down their answers, is called a **survey**.

### Give choices

The best trick is to give **choices**. Instead of "What is your favourite pet?", ask:

> "Which of these pets do you like best: dog, cat, fish, or something else?"

Now every child picks **one** of four answers. The answers sort straight into four groups. "Rex" becomes "dog".
"Puppies" becomes "dog". The dinosaur fan picks "something else".

### Choosing good choices

Choices need care. Here is what to think about.

1. **Not too many.** Three to five choices is plenty. Ten choices would give ten tiny rows, and it would be hard to
   see anything.
2. **Easy to tell apart.** "Dog" and "puppy" should not both be choices, because a puppy is a dog. Each child should
   know exactly which one is theirs.
3. **A choice for everyone.** What if a child's favourite is a hamster, and hamster is not on the list? Add a choice
   called **"something else"**, so nobody is stuck. (Some surveys call it "other".)

### Everyone answers once

A survey is fair when **everyone answers exactly once**. Not zero times, and not two times.

- If someone is **missed**, their answer is lost, and the graph is not about the whole class.
- If someone answers **twice**, their choice counts double. That is not fair to everybody else.

How can you make sure? Use a **class list**. Write every child's name. As each child answers, put a tick next to
their name, and write down their choice.

| Name | Tick | Choice |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ava | ✓ | dog |
| Ben | ✓ | cat |
| Cleo | ✓ | dog |
| Dev | ✓ | something else |
| Emma | | |

Emma has no tick yet, so you know she still needs to answer. Nobody can get two ticks, because there is only one
line for each name.

### Ways to collect the answers

There are many ways to collect answers. Each one has a good side and a tricky side.

- **Hands up.** Quick! But children may look around and copy their friends. Some may put their hand up twice, for two
  choices, by mistake.
- **Stand in a corner.** Each corner of the room is one choice. Fun, and easy to count. But friends may follow
  friends.
- **A block in a cup.** Each child drops one block into the cup for their choice. Nobody sees what you chose. Then
  you tip out each cup and count.

- **A sticker on the graph.** Each child puts their own sticker straight on a picture graph. The graph builds itself!

Whichever way you choose, one rule stays the same: **one answer from each child, and every child answers**.

### Questions that work, and questions that do not

Some questions are not good for counting at all.

- "Is your dog nice?" Only children with dogs can answer, and "nice" means different things to different people.
- "How much do you like cats?" Everyone would answer in different words.

A good counting question has a short list of clear answers that everyone can choose from. "Do you have a pet at home:
yes or no?" is a great question. Every child can answer, and there are just two groups.

### After the survey

Once you have the answers, you know what to do from the last three lessons:

1. **Sort** the answers into groups (one group for each choice).
2. **Count** each group, and check that the total equals the number of children.
3. **Draw** a picture graph.

With the cups above, the counts are: dog 6, cat 5, fish 1, something else 3. Total: 6 and 5 make 11, and 1 more makes
12, and 3 more makes **15**. If there are 15 children in the class, everyone answered once.

## Worked examples

**Example 1. Fixing a muddled question.** A child wants to know what the class likes to eat for a snack. She asks,
"What do you like to eat?" What goes wrong, and how can she fix it?

1. Children will say all sorts of things: "pizza", "everything", "my mom's soup", "apples and cheese".
2. Many answers will not sort. "Everything" fits no group. "Apples and cheese" is two answers.
3. Fix: give choices, and say "pick one". "Which snack do you like best: fruit, crackers, yogurt, or something else?
   Pick one."
4. Now every answer goes in exactly one of four groups.

**Example 2. Too many answers.** A class of 22 children voted for a class name with hands up. The counts were:
Stars 9, Rockets 8, Comets 9. Is something wrong?

1. Find the total: 9 and 8 make 17, and 9 more make **26**.
2. But there are only 22 children. 26 is **4 too many**.
3. So some children voted twice (maybe they liked two names and put their hand up both times).
4. The vote is not fair. Fix: vote again with a class list and ticks, or with blocks in cups, so each child votes
   only once.

**Example 3. Planning a survey.** Plan a survey to find out what the class would like to play at recess.

1. **The question**: "Which game would you like to play at recess: tag, ball games, skipping, or something else?
   Pick one."
2. **Who answers**: every child in the class, once each. Use the class list with ticks.
3. **How to collect**: each child drops a block in one of four cups, so nobody copies.
4. **After**: sort, count, check the total equals the number of children, and draw a picture graph.

## Try it

1. Why is "What do you like?" a hard question to make a picture graph from?
2. Make the question "What is your favourite animal?" into a question with four choices.
3. Why is it a good idea to have a choice called "something else"?
4. A class has 18 children. In a vote about a trip, the counts were: zoo 7, farm 6, park 3. Add them up. What might
   have happened?
5. Why might "hands up" give a different answer from "blocks in cups"?
6. Which question is better for a survey, and why? (a) "Do you walk to school: yes or no?" (b) "How do you feel about
   walking?"
7. Kai asks, "Which fruit do you like best: apple, banana, fruit, or something else?" One of his choices is a
   problem. Which one, and why?
8. Plan your own survey for your family: write the question, the choices, and how you will make sure everyone
   answers once.

## Common mistake

**Asking without choices.** An open question gets answers in all sorts of words that do not sort. Give a short list
of clear choices, and include "something else".

**Letting people answer twice, or missing someone.** Then the counts are wrong, and the graph does not show the whole
group. Use a list of names and tick each person once. Always check that the total equals the number of people.
