# Pictographs with a key

*Statistics and data · level 2, lesson 4 of 6*

## A question to start

Four grades in a school counted the books they read in one month, 160 books in all. You want to make a picture
graph, with one sticker for each book read. But Grade 3 alone read 60 books. That is 60 stickers in one row! The row would run
right off the page.

What if one sticker could stand for **more than one** thing? One sticker for every 10 books. Then Grade 3 needs only
6 stickers. That small change, a picture that stands for a group, is the idea of this lesson. It is how newspapers and
posters show big numbers with a few pictures.

## The idea

### A picture for a group

A **pictograph** is a picture graph where each picture can stand for **more than one** thing. How many? That is
written in a little box called the **key** (some people call it the **legend**).

The key says: one whole circle stands for **2** children.

### Reading a pictograph

To read a row, count its pictures and use the key.

- **Cheese**: 4 whole circles. Each is 2 children. Count by 2s: 2, 4, 6, 8. **8** children.
- **Pepperoni**: 5 whole circles and a **half** circle. Count by 2s for the whole ones: 2, 4, 6, 8, 10. A half circle
  is half of 2, which is **1**. So 10 and 1 make **11** children.
- **Veggie**: 2 whole circles and a half: 2, 4, then 1 more. **5** children.
- **Mushroom**: 1 whole circle: **2** children.

Check the total: 8 and 11 make 19, and 5 make 24, and 2 make **26**. That matches the 26 children.

The **key is everything**. If you forgot to read it and counted each circle as 1 child, you would say cheese has 4,
and you would be wrong. Always read the key first.

### Why half a picture means half the key

If one circle is 2 children, then cutting the circle in half cuts the 2 children in half: 1 child. In the same way:

- If one picture is **10**, half a picture is **5**.
- If one picture is **4**, half a picture is **2**.
- If one picture is **2**, half a picture is **1**.

Half a picture always means half of the key.

### Making a pictograph

To make a pictograph from a table:

1. **Choose a key.** Look at the numbers. Choose a key that makes the rows a good length (not too long, not too
   short) and that works with the numbers.
2. **Work out each row.** How many groups of the key fit into each number? Use whole pictures for the whole groups,
   and a half picture if there is half a group left.
3. **Draw** the pictures, all the same size, lined up from the same starting line, with labels, a title and the key.

For the pizza data with a key of 2:

| Topping | Children | Groups of 2 | Pictures |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| cheese | 8 | 4 | 4 whole |
| pepperoni | 11 | 5 and 1 left | 5 whole and a half |
| veggie | 5 | 2 and 1 left | 2 whole and a half |
| mushroom | 2 | 1 | 1 whole |

### Choosing a good key

Not every key works with every set of numbers.

With a key of **2**, you can show any whole number: even numbers with whole pictures, odd numbers with one half
picture at the end.

With a key of **10**, half a picture is 5. So you can show 10, 20, 30, ... with whole pictures and 5, 15, 25, 35, ...
with a half at the end. But how would you show 23? It is 2 whole pictures and 3 more, and 3 is not half of 10. You
would need a strange little piece of a picture, about three tenths of a circle, which is very hard to draw and very
hard to read.

So choose a key that **fits your numbers**:

- Numbers that are all multiples of 5 or 10 (like 30, 45, 60, 25): a key of **10** works well.
- Small numbers, some odd and some even: a key of **2** works well.
- A key of **5**: whole pictures show 5, 10, 15, ...; but half a picture would be 2 and a half, which is a strange
  amount of children. So a key of 5 works best when every number is a multiple of 5.

Also choose a key that keeps the rows a sensible length. With the school books data, a key of 1 gives a row of 60
pictures, much too long. A key of 10 gives a row of 6. Just right.

### Pictograph or bar graph?

A pictograph is friendly and fun to look at, and a key lets it show big numbers. But reading half pictures is not very
exact, and some numbers do not fit the key. A bar graph with a scale can show any number. Use a pictograph to make a
point quickly and clearly; use a bar graph when you need exact values.

1. Read the key: one circle is **10** books. Half a circle is **5** books.
2. Grade 1: 3 circles. 10, 20, 30: **30** books.
3. Grade 2: 4 circles and a half. 10, 20, 30, 40, and 5 more: **45** books.
4. Grade 3: 6 circles: **60** books.
5. Grade 4: 2 circles and a half: 20 and 5, **25** books.
6. Altogether: 30 and 45 make 75, and 60 make 135, and 25 make **160** books.
7. How many more books did Grade 3 read than Grade 1? From 30 up to 60 is **30 more**. On the graph, that is 3 more
   circles, and 3 circles are 30 books. You can answer "how many more" by counting the extra pictures and using the
   key.

**Example 2. Making a pictograph.** Make a pictograph of this table, with a key of 2.

| Fruit | Children |
| --- | --- |
| apple | 6 |
| banana | 9 |
| grapes | 4 |

1. Apple: 6 is three groups of 2: **3** circles.
2. Banana: 9 is four groups of 2 (8) and 1 left: **4 circles and a half**.
3. Grapes: 4 is two groups of 2: **2** circles.
4. Draw them in rows, with labels, a title, and the key "one circle = 2 children".

**Example 3. Choosing the key.** A survey of 200 people asked about their favourite season: winter 40, spring 50,
summer 80, fall 30. Choose a key.

1. With a key of 1, summer would need 80 pictures. Too many.
2. With a key of 2, summer needs 40 pictures. Still too many.
3. With a key of **10**: winter 4, spring 5, summer 8, fall 3. Every number is a multiple of 10, so all whole
   pictures. The longest row has 8 pictures. That fits nicely.
4. (A key of 20 would also work: winter 2, spring 2 and a half, summer 4, fall 1 and a half. But the halves make it
   harder to read, so 10 is better.)

## Try it

1. In the pizza pictograph, how many more children chose pepperoni than mushroom?
2. A pictograph has the key "one star = 5 votes". A row has 4 stars. How many votes?
3. With the key "one circle = 4 children", what does half a circle stand for? What do 3 circles and a half stand
   for?
4. Make the rows for a pictograph with a key of 2: red 10, blue 7, green 3.
5. A pictograph about pets uses "one paw = 10 pets". The cat row has 3 paws and a half. How many cats?
6. Why would "one picture = 5 children" be a bad key for the numbers 7, 12, 9?
7. Choose a good key for 20, 35, 50, 15, and say how many pictures each row needs.
8. Jamal reads a pictograph without looking at the key. He says "the dog row has 6 pictures, so 6 people chose dog".
   The key says one picture is 3 people. What is the right answer?

??? note "Answers"
    1. Pepperoni 11, mushroom 2: from 2 up to 11 is **9 more**.
    2. 5, 10, 15, 20: **20** votes.
    3. Half a circle is half of 4: **2** children. 3 circles and a half: 4, 8, 12, and 2 more: **14** children.
    4. Red: 10 is five 2s, **5** circles. Blue: 7 is three 2s and 1 left, **3 circles and a half**. Green: 3 is one 2
       and 1 left, **1 circle and a half**.
    5. 10, 20, 30, and half of 10 is 5: **35** cats.
    6. None of them is a multiple of 5, or half of one. 7 is one 5 and 2 more; 12 is two 5s and 2 more; 9 is one 5 and
       4 more. You would need awkward bits of pictures that nobody could read. A key of 1 (or 2, with halves) would
       be better.
    7. A key of **10** works: 20 is **2**, 35 is **3 and a half**, 50 is **5**, 15 is **1 and a half**. (A key of 5
       also works, with whole pictures only: 4, 7, 10, 3.)
    8. Each picture is 3 people: 3, 6, 9, 12, 15, 18. **18** people chose dog.

## Common mistake

**Forgetting the key.** Counting the pictures is only half the job. Each picture stands for the number in the key, so
always read the key first and count by that number.

**Reading half a picture as 1.** Half a picture is half of the key. With a key of 10, half a picture is 5, not 1.
