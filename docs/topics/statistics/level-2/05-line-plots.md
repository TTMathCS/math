# Line plots

*Statistics and data · level 2, lesson 5 of 6*

## A question to start

A class of 20 children counted how many baby teeth each of them has lost so far. Here are the answers, in the order
the children said them:

2, 3, 1, 4, 2, 0, 5, 3, 2, 8, 1, 4, 3, 2, 0, 5, 2, 3, 1, 4

What is the most common number of lost teeth? Did anyone lose a lot more than everybody else? Is there a number that
nobody had?

It is hard to tell from a jumble of numbers. These answers are different from favourite pets or seasons: they are
**numbers** that sit in order on a number line, 0, 1, 2, 3, and so on. For data like this there is a graph that keeps
the numbers in their places. It is called a **line plot**, and you can draw one in a minute with nothing but X's.

## The idea

### Two kinds of data

So far the answers to our surveys have been **choices**, like dog, cat or fish. Choices have no order: dog is not
"bigger" than cat. You can put the bars of a bar graph in any order.

But some answers are **numbers that you count or measure**: teeth lost, shoe size, how many letters in your name, how
many minutes it takes to walk to school. These have a natural order. 3 teeth is more than 2 teeth and less than 4. A
good graph for numbers keeps that order, with every number in its place, like the marks on a ruler.

### Drawing a line plot

A **line plot** is a number line with an **X** above it for each answer.

1. **Draw a number line.** Find the smallest answer (0) and the largest (8). Mark **every** number from the smallest
   to the largest, equally spaced: 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8. Do not skip 6 and 7, even if nobody said them.
2. **Go through the answers one by one.** For each answer, draw an X above its number, and cross the answer off the
   list. If there is already an X there, draw the new one just above it.
3. **Keep the X's the same size and stack them evenly**, so the height of each stack shows how many.
4. **Add a title and a label** under the line that says what the numbers are.

It is a little like a tally chart and a bar graph at once: each X is one child, and the stacks act like bars. But the
number line keeps everything in order and shows the numbers nobody said.

### What a line plot shows

Now the questions from the start are easy.

**The most common value.** The tallest stack is above **2**, with 5 X's. So the most common number of lost teeth is
2. Grown-ups call the most common value the **mode**; you will meet it again in level 3.

**Gaps.** Above 6 and 7 there are no X's at all. A place with no X's is called a **gap**. Nobody in the class had lost
6 or 7 teeth.

**An unusual value.** One X sits all alone at 8, far from the rest, on the other side of the gap. A value that is far
away from the others is called an **outlier** (it lies **out** away from the rest). One child has lost many more
teeth than anyone else. Maybe they are older, or maybe they had an accident. Outliers are worth a second look: they
might be a real surprise, or a mistake in the data.

**Where most of the data sits.** Most X's are piled up between 1 and 4. That is called a **cluster**: a bunch of
values close together. Most children in this class have lost between 1 and 4 teeth.

**How spread out.** The answers go from 0 (smallest) to 8 (largest). That tells you how widely the answers are spread.

### Why every number gets a mark

Imagine leaving out 6 and 7 because nobody said them, and putting 8 straight after 5. The plot would look as if the
child with 8 teeth lost were close to everyone else. The gap, and the outlier, would disappear. A number line must be
like a ruler: every number in its place, equally spaced. That is what lets the plot tell the truth about gaps and
outliers.

### Counting from a line plot

Each X is one child, so the number of X's is the number of children. Count them: 2 and 3 and 5 and 4 and 3 and 2 and
1 make **20**. Twenty children, twenty X's. The total check works here too.

## Worked examples

**Example 1. Questions from the teeth plot.**

1. How many children have lost **3** teeth? Count the X's above 3: **4**.
2. How many have lost **no** teeth? Above 0: **2**.
3. How many have lost **4 or more**? Above 4: 3. Above 5: 2. Above 8: 1. (Above 6 and 7: none.) 3 and 2 and 1 make
   **6** children.
4. How many have lost **fewer than 2**? Above 0: 2, above 1: 3. That makes **5** children.
5. Check: 6 children have 4 or more, 5 have fewer than 2, and 5 have exactly 2, and 4 have exactly 3. 6 and 5 and 5
   and 4 make 20. Every child counted once.

## Try it

Use the teeth line plot for questions 1 to 3.

1. How many children have lost exactly 5 teeth?
2. How many children have lost 1 tooth or 2 teeth?
3. A new child joins the class and has lost 6 teeth. What changes on the plot? Is there still a gap?
4. Make a line plot of these numbers of pets per family: 1, 0, 2, 1, 1, 3, 0, 1, 2, 6. What is the most common
   number? Where is the gap? Is there an outlier?
5. Leo made a line plot of minutes to walk to school, but his number line goes 5, 6, 7, 10, 15. What is wrong?
6. Why is a line plot a good graph for shoe sizes, but not for favourite colours?
7. In the shoe size plot, how many people wear a size smaller than 8?

    1. **2** children.
    2. Above 1: 3. Above 2: 5. 3 and 5 make **8** children.
    3. A new X appears above 6, so the 6 stack has 1 X. There is still a gap at **7**. Now the child with 8 lost
       teeth is less far away from the rest.
    4. Number line 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6. X's: 0 gets 2, 1 gets 4, 2 gets 2, 3 gets 1, 6 gets 1 (2 and 4 and 2 and 1 and
       1 make 10). Most common: **1** pet. Gap: **4 and 5**. Outlier: the family with **6** pets.
    5. The numbers are **not equally spaced**: from 7 to 10 is 3 minutes, and from 10 to 15 is 5 minutes, but they
       are drawn the same distance apart. He must mark every number from 5 to 15, equally spaced, like a ruler.
    6. Shoe sizes are numbers in order, so they fit on a number line, and gaps and outliers make sense. Colours have

       colours.
    7. Above 6: 2. Above 7: 3. **5** people.

## Common mistake

**Leaving out numbers that nobody chose.** Every number from smallest to largest goes on the line, equally spaced,
even if it has no X's. Otherwise the gaps vanish and outliers look closer than they are.

**Drawing X's of different sizes, or stacking them unevenly.** Then a tall stack might hold fewer X's than a short
one. Keep every X the same size and the same distance apart.
