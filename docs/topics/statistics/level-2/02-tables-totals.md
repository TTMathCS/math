# Tables with totals

*Statistics and data · level 2, lesson 2 of 6*

## A question to start

Priya and Tom both counted the same tally chart of favourite seasons, from a class of 26 children. Priya says the
counts add up to 26. Tom says they add up to 27.

They cannot both be right. Who made the mistake, and how could you find it without asking all 26 children again?

The answer is a **table with a total**. A total is like an alarm. When it does not match the number of people you
asked, it rings, and it tells you to look again. In this lesson you learn to build tables, to add their totals
quickly, and to use the totals as a check, even in a table with rows **and** columns.

## The idea

### From tallies to a table

A tally chart is perfect while the answers are coming in. But tally marks are slow to read, and you cannot easily
add them. When the survey is finished, we turn each tally into a number and write the numbers in a **table**.

A table is a grid of boxes. Each line going across is a **row**. Each line going down is a **column**. The top row
holds the **headings**, which say what each column means. And the table has a **title** that says what it is about.

Here is the season survey from the last lesson as a table.

**Our favourite season (26 children)**

| Season | Number of children |
| --- | --- |
| winter | 6 |
| spring | 5 |
| summer | 11 |
| fall | 4 |
| **Total** | **26** |

The last row is the **total**: all the counts added together.

### Adding a column quickly

To add a column of numbers, look for pairs that make **10**, or that are easy to add.

- 6 and 4 make 10 (winter and fall).
- 5 and 11 make 16 (spring and summer).
- 10 and 16 make **26**.

You may add the numbers in any order you like, and the total is always the same. That is a very useful fact. It
means you can hunt for friendly pairs instead of always adding from the top.

### Why the total is a check

Every child gave **one** answer, and every answer went in **one** row. So when you add up all the rows, you should
get exactly the number of children. If the total is:

- **too big**, something was counted twice: maybe a child answered twice, or a tally mark was read wrong, or one
  number was copied wrong.
- **too small**, something was missed: maybe a child was away, or a mark was skipped.
- **just right**, that is good news. (It is not a full proof that every number is right: two mistakes could cancel
  out. But it catches most mistakes.)

Back to Priya and Tom. Tom's table said: winter 6, spring 5, summer 12, fall 4. Total 27. Priya's said summer 11.
Look at the tally for summer again: two gates and one line, which is 5, 10, 11. Tom read **11** as **12**. Priya is
right. The total found the mistake, and the tally showed which row it was in.

### A table with two columns of counts

Sometimes we ask the **same** question in **two** groups and want to compare them. Two classes, Room 1 and Room 2,
each chose a snack.

**Snack choices in two classes**

| Snack | Room 1 | Room 2 | Total |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| apple | 8 | 5 | 13 |
| crackers | 6 | 10 | 16 |
| yogurt | 9 | 7 | 16 |
| **Total** | **23** | **22** | **45** |

Now there are totals **both ways**.

- The totals **across** each row (the last column) tell you how many children chose each snack in both rooms
  together. Apple: 8 and 5 make 13.
- The totals **down** each column (the last row) tell you how many children are in each room. Room 1: 8 and 6 and 9
  make 23. Room 2: 5 and 10 and 7 make 22.
- The number in the bottom right corner is the **grand total**: every child in both rooms.

### The corner check

Here is something beautiful. You can find the grand total in **two different ways**:

- add the last column: 13 and 16 and 16 make **45**;
- add the last row: 23 and 22 make **45**.

They **must** be the same. Why? Both ways add up every number inside the table, once each. Adding by rows first, or by
columns first, just changes the order, and the order of adding never changes the answer. It is like counting the
children in a classroom by rows of desks or by columns of desks: either way you count every child.

So if your two corner answers are different, there is a mistake somewhere in the table. This is the **corner check**,
and people who keep accounts for shops and businesses have used it for hundreds of years.

### What a table is good for

A table is good for **exact** numbers. You can look up "how many in Room 2 chose crackers?" and find 10 straight away.
A table is not so good for **seeing** the pattern at a glance; for that we draw a graph, in the next lesson. Most
good reports have both: a table for the exact numbers, and a graph for the picture.

## Worked examples

**Example 1. Tallies to a table.** A tally chart about favourite sports shows: soccer, two gates and three lines;
hockey, one gate and four lines; swimming, one gate; basketball, three lines. Twenty-seven children answered. Make
the table and check it.

1. Soccer: 5, 10, then 11, 12, 13. **13**.
2. Hockey: 5, then 6, 7, 8, 9. **9**.
3. Swimming: **5**. Basketball: **3**.
4. Total: 13 and 9 make 22, and 5 make 27, and 3 make **30**.
5. But only 27 children answered! The total is **3 too many**.
6. The tallies are read again, and they are right. So the problem is in the voting: the teacher finds out that
   three children put up their hands twice, for two sports. The class does the survey again, more carefully.

| Sport | Number of children |
| --- | --- |
| soccer | 13 |
| hockey | 9 |
| swimming | 5 |
| basketball | 3 |
| **Total** | **30** |

The table is honest about what was counted. But because 30 is not 27, it cannot be trusted as a vote.

**Example 2. Filling in a missing number.** A table of favourite colours from 24 children has red 7, blue 9,
green ?, yellow 3. Find the missing number.

1. The total must be 24.
2. The numbers we know: 7 and 9 make 16, and 3 make 19.
3. Green is what is left: from 19 count up to 24. That is **5**.
4. Check: 7 and 9 and 5 and 3 make 24. Yes.

**Example 3. Reading a table with two groups.** Use the snack table above.

1. How many children in Room 2 chose crackers? Find the crackers row and the Room 2 column: **10**.
2. Which snack was most popular in Room 1? The biggest number in the Room 1 column is 9: **yogurt**.
3. Which snack was most popular in both rooms together? Look at the Total column: crackers 16 and yogurt 16 are
   **tied**.
4. How many more children are in Room 1 than in Room 2? 23 and 22: **1 more**.

Notice that the "most popular" answer can change when you put groups together. Room 1 liked yogurt best, Room 2
liked crackers best, and together it is a tie.

## Common mistake

**Skipping the total.** The total takes a minute and catches most counting mistakes. A table without a total gives
no warning when something is wrong.

**Thinking a matching total proves everything is right.** If one count is 1 too big and another is 1 too small, the
total still matches. The total check catches most mistakes, not all of them, so read the tallies carefully too.
