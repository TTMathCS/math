# Tally marks

*Statistics and data · level 2, lesson 1 of 6*

## A question to start

You sit by the window for ten minutes and count the vehicles going past: cars, trucks and buses. A car. Another car.
A bus! Three cars in a row. A truck. They come fast, sometimes two at once.

How can you keep count of three different things at the same time, without losing your place? You cannot write "17"
and then rub it out and write "18" every time a car goes by. You would miss the next one.

People solved this problem thousands of years ago, long before anyone wrote numbers the way we do. The answer is a
mark for each thing: a **tally mark**. It is so simple that it is still used today, by scorekeepers, scientists
counting birds, and anyone running a survey.

## The idea

### One mark for each thing

A **tally mark** is a short line. You draw one line each time you see the thing you are counting. You never rub
anything out, and you never have to remember a number. You just add a line.

Car: |. Another car: ||. Another: |||. The marks **are** the count.

The word "tally" comes from an old word for a stick with notches cut in it. Bones and sticks with rows of cut notches
have been found that are tens of thousands of years old, and many people think they were used for keeping count.
Tally marks may be one of the oldest kinds of math there is.

### Bundles of five

Here is the problem with plain lines. What is this?

`||||||||||||||||`

You have to count every line, one by one, and it is easy to lose your place. So we bundle the lines into **fives**.
Draw four lines, and then draw the fifth line **across** them. That makes a little gate, and the gate means 5.

That is one bundle of five and two more lines: **7**.

Why five? Look at your hand. Four fingers and a thumb across them: five. Five is small enough to see at a glance,
without counting. You do not count the lines in a gate; you just see "a gate, that's 5".

When all the answers are in, you count each row:

- Winter: one gate and one line: 5, 6. **6**.
- Spring: one gate: **5**.
- Summer: two gates and one line: 5, 10, 11. **11**.
- Fall: four lines: **4**.

And check the total: 6 and 5 make 11, and 11 make 22, and 4 make **26**. There are 26 children in the class, so every
answer was marked once.

### Working through a list

Sometimes the answers are already written down, in a long jumbled list. To make a tally chart from a list:

1. Take the **first** answer. Make a mark in its row. **Cross it out** on the list.
2. Take the next answer. Make a mark. Cross it out.
3. Keep going until every answer is crossed out.

Crossing out is the secret. You always know which answers you have done, so you never count one twice and you never
skip one.

- In some places, especially in South America and parts of Europe, people draw a **square** one side at a time (four
  marks) and then a line from corner to corner for the fifth.
- In China, Japan and Korea, people often write the character **正**, which has exactly five strokes. Each stroke is
  one mark, and a finished character is 5.

All three ideas are the same: one mark for each thing, and a finished picture for every five.

## Worked examples

**Example 1. Reading tallies.** A tally chart shows: red, three gates and two lines; blue, one gate and four lines;
green, two gates. How many of each?

1. Red: gates 5, 10, 15, then lines 16, 17. **17**.
2. Blue: gate 5, then lines 6, 7, 8, 9. **9**.
3. Green: gates 5, 10. **10**.
4. Total: 17 and 9 make 26, and 10 more make **36**.

**Example 2. From a list to a tally chart.** Here are the snacks 15 children chose: apple, crackers, apple, yogurt,
crackers, apple, apple, yogurt, crackers, apple, cheese, apple, crackers, yogurt, apple.

1. Make a chart with four rows: apple, crackers, yogurt, cheese.
2. Go through the list in order, one mark each, crossing out as you go.

| Snack | Tally | Count |
| --- | --- | --- |
| apple | one gate and two lines | 7 |
| crackers | four lines | 4 |
| yogurt | three lines | 3 |
| cheese | one line | 1 |

Check: 7 and 4 make 11, and 3 make 14, and 1 makes **15**. Fifteen children. Good.

**Example 3. The vehicle count.** In ten minutes at the window, the tallies were: cars, four gates and three lines;
trucks, one gate; buses, two lines.

1. Cars: 5, 10, 15, 20, then 21, 22, 23. **23** cars.
2. Trucks: **5**. Buses: **2**.
3. Altogether: 23 and 5 make 28, and 2 make **30** vehicles.
4. How many more cars than trucks? From 5 up to 23 is **18** more.

Notice how the tally let you keep three counts going at once, as fast as the traffic came.

## Try it

1. Draw the tally for 12.
2. What number is shown by four gates and one line?
3. How many gates and how many single lines do you need for 19?
4. A tally chart for favourite fruit shows: apple, two gates and three lines; banana, one gate and one line; grapes,
   three gates. Find each count and the total.
5. Make a tally chart for these answers about pets: dog, cat, dog, fish, dog, cat, dog, dog, fish, cat, dog, cat.
6. Nadia wanted to draw 5. She drew five lines standing up and then a line across them. What went wrong? What
   number did she really draw?
7. Using the square way of tallying, how many full squares (with their corner-to-corner lines) do you need for 20?
   For 22?
8. Why is a tally easier than writing numbers when you count cars going past?

??? note "Answers"
    1. Two gates and two lines: 5, 10, 11, 12.
    2. 5, 10, 15, 20, then 21. **21**.
    3. 19 is 5, 10, 15 and then 16, 17, 18, 19. So **3 gates and 4 single lines**.
    4. Apple: 5, 10, then 11, 12, 13: **13**. Banana: 5, then 6: **6**. Grapes: 5, 10, 15: **15**. Total: 13 and 6
       make 19, and 15 make **34**.
    5. Dog: one gate and one line, **6**. Cat: four lines, **4**. Fish: two lines, **2**. Check: 6 and 4 and 2 make
       12, and there are 12 answers in the list.
    6. The fifth line should go **across** the first four, not stand up next to them. Five lines standing and one
       across is a gate of **6** lines. It looks like a 5 but it is really **6**. To show 5 she needs four standing
       lines and the fifth across them.
    7. 20 is four fives, so **4 squares**. 22 is 4 squares and then 2 more marks (two sides of a new square).
    8. With a tally you only add one line each time, quickly, without stopping to think of the next number or rub
       anything out. And bundles of five make it easy to count up at the end.

## Common mistake

**Drawing five standing lines before the gate.** The gate line is the fifth mark. Four standing lines, then the
fifth goes across. A gate drawn over five standing lines holds 6, not 5.

**Counting every line one by one at the end.** That is slow and easy to get wrong. Count the gates by fives first,
then the single lines by ones.
