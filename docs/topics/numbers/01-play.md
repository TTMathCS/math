# Play: Counting, huge numbers and old ways to write them

*Numbers · step 1 of 5*

## A question to start

Suppose you count out loud, one number every second: "one, two, three, ..." You never stop to eat or sleep. How
long would it take you to reach one million?

Make a guess before you read on. An hour? A day? A week?

The answer is about **11 and a half days**, without a single break. And to reach one *billion* the same way would
take about **32 years**. A billion is not "a bit more" than a million. It is a thousand millions.

Big numbers are hard to imagine. On this page you will find some tricks for counting, feel how big the big numbers
really are, and see how people long ago wrote their numbers down.

## The idea

### Counting in groups

Counting one by one is slow and easy to get wrong. People have always counted in **groups** instead.

**Tally marks.** Draw a short line for each thing. When you reach five, draw the fifth line across the other four.
Now you can count the bundles of five quickly: three bundles and two more is 5, 10, 15, then 16, 17.

**Skip counting.** Count pairs of shoes by twos: 2, 4, 6, 8. Count coins worth five by fives: 5, 10, 15, 20.
Count fingers in a room by tens: 10, 20, 30. Skip counting is the start of the times tables.

**Groups of ten.** We have ten fingers, and that is almost certainly why we count in tens. Ten ones make a ten. Ten
tens make a hundred. Ten hundreds make a thousand. Every time you collect ten of something, you bundle them into
one bigger thing.

### How big is big?

Here are the big number names, each one made of a thousand of the one before it:

- one **thousand** is a thousand ones,
- one **million** is a thousand thousands,
- one **billion** is a thousand millions.

Ways to feel them:

- A thousand steps is a walk of roughly ten minutes.
- A million seconds is about 11 and a half days.
- A billion seconds is about 32 years.
- Mars is, on average, about 228 million kilometres from the Sun. Light, the fastest thing there is, takes about
  13 minutes to make that trip.

A good trick for big numbers: **compare them with something you know**. "A million seconds is less than two weeks,
but a billion seconds is a grown-up's lifetime so far" tells you much more than the long rows of zeros.

### Roman numerals

The ancient Romans wrote numbers with letters. You can still see them on clocks, in book chapters and at the end
of films.

| Letter | I | V | X | L | C | D | M |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Worth | 1 | 5 | 10 | 50 | 100 | 500 | 1000 |

The rules are short:

1. Write the letters from biggest to smallest, and **add them up**. XVI is 10 + 5 + 1 = 16. MDCL is
   1000 + 500 + 100 + 50 = 1650.
2. Do not write the same letter more than three times in a row. Instead, put a **smaller letter in front** of a
   bigger one, and it means "take this away". IV is 5 take away 1, which is 4. IX is 9. XL is 40. XC is 90.
   CM is 900.

So 2026 is MM (two thousands) and XX (two tens) and VI (six): **MMXXVI**.

Roman numerals have no symbol for **zero**, and they are clumsy for doing sums. Try adding XLVIII and LXXIV without
turning them into our numbers first. It is hard work.

### Mayan numerals

The Maya of Central America used just three symbols: a **dot** for one, a **bar** for five, and a **shell** for
zero. They were among the very few peoples of the ancient world to have a symbol for zero.

To write a number up to 19, use as many bars as you can, then dots for the rest. The dots sit on top of the bars.

Seven is one bar (5) and two dots (2). Thirteen is two bars (10) and three dots (3). Nineteen is three bars and
four dots, the biggest number you can make in one level. For bigger numbers the Maya stacked levels on top of each
other, with each level worth twenty times the one below. They counted in twenties, perhaps because they counted
fingers *and* toes.

## Worked example

**Read the Roman numeral MCMXCIV.**

1. Split it into pieces, watching for a small letter in front of a bigger one: M, CM, XC, IV.
2. Work out each piece. M is 1000. CM is 1000 take away 100, which is 900. XC is 100 take away 10, which is 90.
   IV is 5 take away 1, which is 4.
3. Add the pieces: 1000 + 900 + 90 + 4 = **1994**.

**Now go the other way: write 48 in Roman numerals.**

1. Split 48 into tens and ones: 40 and 8.
2. 40 is "ten before fifty": XL.
3. 8 is five and three ones: VIII.
4. Put them together: **XLVIII**.

Notice that you never write IL for 49. The Romans only put I in front of V and X, X in front of L and C, and C in
front of D and M. So 49 is 40 and 9: XLIX.

## Try it

1. Draw tally marks for 23. How many full bundles of five are there?
2. What number is XXVII? What number is CDXL?
3. Write 2026 and 99 in Roman numerals.
4. A Mayan number has two bars and four dots in one level. What number is it?
5. Which is longer: one million seconds or two weeks?
6. Skip count by 25s from 0 to 200. How many jumps did you make?

??? note "Answers"
    1. Four full bundles of five (that is 20) and three more marks. 5, 10, 15, 20, then 21, 22, 23.
    2. XXVII is 10 + 10 + 5 + 1 + 1 = 27. CDXL is CD (500 take away 100, which is 400) and XL (50 take away 10,
       which is 40), so 440.
    3. 2026 is MMXXVI. 99 is 90 and 9: XC and IX, so XCIX. (Not IC: I only goes in front of V and X.)
    4. 14. Two bars are 5 + 5 = 10, and four dots are 4 more.
    5. Two weeks. Two weeks is 14 days, and a million seconds is only about 11 and a half days.
    6. 25, 50, 75, 100, 125, 150, 175, 200: eight jumps. That is the same as saying there are 8 quarters in 2.

## Common mistake

**Thinking a billion is "a little more" than a million.** The names sound alike, but a billion is a thousand
millions. If a million seconds is 11 days, a billion seconds is 1000 times as long: about 32 years. Whenever two
big numbers have similar names, ask "how many of the small one fit into the big one?"
