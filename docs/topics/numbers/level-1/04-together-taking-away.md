# Putting together and taking away

*Numbers · level 1, lesson 4 of 6*

## A question to start

Jack has 3 marbles. Then he wins 9 more. How many does he have now?

Sara works it out by counting every marble from the start: "one, two, three, four, five, six, seven, eight, nine,
ten, eleven, twelve". That is twelve counting words.

Jack does it differently. He says "**nine**... ten, eleven, twelve". Just three words after the nine. He gets twelve
too.

But wait. Jack had the **3** marbles first, and won the 9 after. Is he allowed to start at 9? Is that cheating? By
the end of this lesson you will know why Jack's way is allowed, and why it is so much faster.

## The idea

### Adding means putting together

When we **add**, we put two groups together and find how many there are all together.

We write this as **3 + 4 = 7**, and we read it "three **plus** four **equals** seven". The two new signs are:

- **+** means "put together with" (we say "plus" or "add").
- **=** means "is the same amount as" (we say "equals" or "makes").

The answer to an adding question has a name: the **total** (or the **sum**).

The sign = is like a balance. On one side is 3 + 4, on the other side is 7. They are the same amount, so the balance
stays level. That is why we can also write it the other way round: **7 = 3 + 4**. It is still true.

### Adding in either order gives the same total

Look at the picture of 3 + 4 again, and walk around to the other side of the table. Now you see the 4 blue dots
first and the 3 orange dots second. That is **4 + 3**. But it is the same dots! Nothing was added or taken away.

So **3 + 4 = 4 + 3**. This is true for every pair of numbers. When you put two groups together, it does not matter
which group you pour in first; the total is the same.

### Counting on

To find 6 + 3, you could count all the things from one. But you already know the first group has 6. So start at 6
and **count on** 3 more:

"**six**... seven, eight, nine." Hold up a finger for each number you count on: one finger for seven, one for eight,
one for nine. When 3 fingers are up, stop. The answer is **9**.

On the number line, counting on is **hopping to the right**, one hop for each one you add. Here is 9 + 3:

### Count on from the bigger number

Now Jack's puzzle. 3 + 9 = 9 + 3, because the order does not change the total. So Jack is allowed to start with the
**9** and count on **3**: "nine... ten, eleven, twelve". That is only 3 counting words instead of 12.

Starting with the bigger number is always quicker, because then you count on the **smaller** number. Try 2 + 8 both
ways. Starting at 2 you have to count on 8: "three, four, five, six, seven, eight, nine, ten". Starting at 8 you only
count on 2: "nine, ten". Same answer, much less work. That is not cheating. It is being clever.

### Subtracting means taking away

When we **subtract**, we start with a group and **take some away**. Then we find how many are left.

There were 8 apples. 3 were eaten (crossed out). 5 are left. We write **8 − 3 = 5** and read it "eight **minus**
three equals five" or "eight **take away** three is five". The sign **−** means "take away".

On the number line, taking away is **hopping to the left**. Here is 12 − 5: start at 12, hop 5 to the left, land
on 7. This is called **counting back**: "twelve... eleven, ten, nine, eight, seven".

Order **does** matter for taking away. 8 − 3 is 5, but 3 − 8 does not make sense yet: you cannot take 8 apples from
a bowl of 3. (Much later, in [level 4](../level-4/06-negative-numbers.md), you will meet numbers below zero, and then it will make sense.)

### Subtracting also means "how many more?"

Here is a different kind of question. Tom has 9 stickers. Ana has 6 stickers. How many more does Tom have?

Nobody takes anything away here. But match them up: Ana's 6 stickers pair with 6 of Tom's, and Tom has 3 left over.
So Tom has **3 more**. And this is the same as **9 − 6 = 3**.

**Why is it the same?** Think of Tom's 9 stickers. Cover up the 6 that have partners. What is left uncovered is the
extra. Covering up 6 out of 9 is just like taking 6 away from 9. So "how many more?" is also a subtraction.

You can also find it by **counting up**: start at the smaller number, 6, and count up to 9: "seven, eight, nine".
That is 3 numbers, so the difference is **3**. Counting up is often easier than counting back, especially when the
two numbers are close together, like 15 − 13 (count up "fourteen, fifteen": 2).

### Adding and taking away undo each other

If you have 5 apples, get 3 more, and then 3 are eaten, you are back to 5. Adding 3 and then taking 3 away gets you
back where you started. That is why every adding fact gives you taking-away facts for free: if 5 + 3 = 8, then
8 − 3 = 5. You will use this a lot in level 2.

### Zero

Adding **0** changes nothing: 7 + 0 = 7 (you put together 7 things with no things). Taking away **0** changes
nothing: 7 − 0 = 7. Taking away **everything** leaves nothing: 7 − 7 = 0.

## Worked examples

**1. Counting on.** 8 birds sit on a fence. 4 more land. How many birds now?

Start at the bigger number, 8, and count on 4: "nine, ten, eleven, twelve". **8 + 4 = 12** birds.

**2. Turning it round.** Find 2 + 9.

2 + 9 = 9 + 2. Start at 9 and count on 2: "ten, eleven". **2 + 9 = 11**.

**3. Taking away.** There are 10 balloons. 4 pop. How many are left?

Count back 4 from 10: "nine, eight, seven, six". **10 − 4 = 6** balloons are left. Check with fingers: hold up 10,
put 4 down, 6 are still up.

**4. How many more?** A rover on Mars drives 7 metres on Monday and 11 metres on Tuesday. How many more metres on
Tuesday?

This is a "how many more" question, so it is **11 − 7**. Count up from 7 to 11: "eight, nine, ten, eleven". That is 4.
The rover drove **4 more metres** on Tuesday.

**5. Undoing.** You know 6 + 7 = 13. What is 13 − 7?

Taking away the 7 undoes adding the 7, so you are back at 6. **13 − 7 = 6**.

## Try it

1. Find 5 + 2 and 2 + 5. What do you notice?
2. Count on from the bigger number: 3 + 8.
3. Count back: 11 − 3.
4. There are 9 cookies. You eat 2. How many are left?
5. Mia has 12 crayons and Leo has 8. How many more does Mia have? (Try counting up.)
6. Fill in the gap: 7 + ___ = 10.
7. You know 4 + 9 = 13. Write two taking-away facts that you now know for free.
8. A frog sits on 5 on the number line. It hops 4 to the right and then 6 to the left. Where does it land?

??? note "Answers"
    1. Both are **7**. Adding in either order gives the same total.
    2. Start at 8, count on 3: "nine, ten, eleven". **11**.
    3. Start at 11, count back 3: "ten, nine, eight". **8**.
    4. 9 − 2: count back 2 from 9, "eight, seven". **7** cookies are left.
    5. 12 − 8. Count up from 8 to 12: "nine, ten, eleven, twelve" is 4 numbers. Mia has **4 more**.
    6. Count up from 7 to 10: "eight, nine, ten" is 3 numbers. **7 + 3 = 10**.
    7. **13 − 9 = 4** and **13 − 4 = 9**. Taking away undoes adding.
    8. 4 to the right: 5 → 9. Then 6 to the left: 9 → 3. The frog lands on **3**. (In one go: it went 2 further left
       than right, so it ends 2 to the left of 5.)

## Common mistake

**Counting the starting number again.** For 8 + 4, some people say "eight, nine, ten, eleven" and stop at eleven,
because they counted 4 words. But "eight" is where you **start**, not one of the 4 you add. Say "eight" with no
finger up, then put up one finger for each number after it: "nine (1), ten (2), eleven (3), twelve (4)". The answer
is 12.

**Taking away the wrong way round.** 9 − 6 and 6 − 9 are not the same. Always start with the group you **have**, and
take away from it.
