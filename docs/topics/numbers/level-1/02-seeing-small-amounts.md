# Seeing small amounts at once

*Numbers · level 1, lesson 2 of 6*

## A question to start

Roll a dice and look at the top. Do you count the dots, "one, two, three, four, five"? Or do you just **know** it
is a five?

Most people just know. They see the shape of the dots, like a face they recognise, and the number pops into their
head. Grown-ups who play board games never count the dots on a dice.

Now try this. Someone flashes a pile of 13 beans at you for one second and hides them. Can you say how many? Almost
nobody can. So what is the difference? Why can you "just see" five dots, but not thirteen beans? This lesson is
about that difference, and about a trick that lets you "just see" bigger numbers too.

## The idea

### Seeing without counting

Look quickly at each group and say how many. Do not count.

Two, three, one, four. You probably knew each one in a flash. Mathematicians have a long word for this:
**subitizing** (say "SOO-bi-tie-zing"). It comes from an old word that means "suddenly". Subitizing means seeing how
many things there are, all at once, without counting.

Most people can do this for up to about **four** things scattered anyhow. After that, the eyes get muddled and we
start counting. But there is a way around this: **patterns** and **groups**.

### Dice patterns

The dots on a dice are not scattered anyhow. They sit in a neat pattern, the same on every dice in the world.

Look closely, and you can see that the bigger patterns are made of smaller ones:

- **Four** is a square: a dot in each corner.
- **Five** is the square of four, **and one more** in the middle.
- **Six** is two rows of three, or **three and three**.

So when you "just see" five, your eyes are really seeing "four and one". This is the secret. You cannot see 13 beans
at once, but you **can** see small groups, and then put the groups together.

### Dominoes

A **domino** is a flat tile split into two halves, with a dice pattern on each half. To find how many dots a domino
has, you see each half at once and put them together.

The domino has 4 on one side and 3 on the other. Four and three make seven. You know it is 7 without touching
every dot.

### Fingers: five on each hand

You carry a counting tool everywhere: your hands. Each hand has **five** fingers. Two hands have **ten**.

Show seven on your fingers. Most people hold up one whole hand (that is five, you do not need to count it) and two
more fingers. **Five and two more is seven.** Show nine: one whole hand and four more, or two whole hands with one
finger down. Your hands teach you two very important groups: **five** and **ten**.

### The five frame and the ten frame

A **five frame** is a row of 5 boxes. We put one counter in each box, starting from the left.

The empty boxes help as much as the full ones. You can see 3 counters, and you can also see that 2 more would fill
the frame. So 3 and 2 make 5.

A **ten frame** is two five frames, one on top of the other: 2 rows of 5 boxes. We fill the top row first, from the
left, then the bottom row.

Look how fast you can read it. The top row is full, so that is **five**. The bottom row has **two**. Five and two
more is **seven**. And there are 3 empty boxes, so seven needs **three more to make ten**.

**Why does the frame make it so easy?** Because it turns a big number into small groups that your eyes can see at
once: a full row (always 5), a few more (small enough to subitize), and some empty boxes (small enough too). You never
have to count one by one.

### Two ways to see the same number

There is often more than one way to see a number. Here are ways to see **eight**:

- On a ten frame: a full row of 5 and 3 more. **Five and three.**
- On a ten frame, looking at the gaps: 2 empty boxes. **Two less than ten.**
- On a dice and a domino: two squares of four. **Four and four.**

All three are right. Being able to see a number in many ways is what makes adding easy later. When you meet
8 + 2 or 10 − 2 or 4 + 4, you will already know the answer is 8, because you have **seen** it.

## Worked examples

**1. A dice and one more.** You roll a five on one dice and a one on the other. How many dots?

See the five pattern (no counting). Then **one more** is six. So there are **6** dots. A grown-up would say
"five, six", starting from the five, not from one.

**2. A ten frame showing 9.** A ten frame has a full top row and four counters in the bottom row, with one empty box.
How many counters?

Way 1: a full row is 5, and 4 more. Five and four is **9**. Way 2: only one box is empty, so it is one less than ten.
One less than ten is **9**. Both ways agree.

**3. A domino with six and six.** Each half has two rows of three. Three and three is six on each half. Six and six
is **12**. (This is the "double six", the biggest domino in a normal set.)

**4. Fingers for eight.** Show eight on your fingers without counting. Put up one whole hand: that is five. Then put
up three fingers on the other hand. Five and three is **8**. Check: two fingers are still down, and ten take away two
is eight.

??? note "Answers"
    1. A full top row (5) and 1 more: five and one is **6**.
    2. A full top row (5) and 4 more: **9**. Only 1 box is empty, so **1** more would make ten.
    3. **4** counters, all in the top row. There are **6** empty boxes: 1 in the top row and 5 in the bottom row.
       Check: 4 and 6 make 10, the whole frame.
    4. **Five.** The four pattern is a dot in each corner; five adds one dot in the middle.
    5. Five and two is **7**. See the five pattern, then say "six, seven" for the two.
    6. For example: one whole hand (5) and one finger on the other hand; or three fingers on each hand (three and
       three). There are other ways too, like four and two.
    7. Every box has a counter, so there are **0** empty boxes. Ten is special because it is "one full frame", or
       "two full hands". In the last lesson of this level you will see that we write all big numbers using groups of
       ten.

## Common mistake

**Starting from one every time.** If you see a full row of five on a ten frame and then count "one, two, three, four,
five, six, seven", you have thrown away the pattern. Trust the row: say "**five**", then count on, "six, seven". This
is faster, and it makes fewer mistakes.

**Filling the frame anyhow.** Counters on a ten frame go in the top row first, from the left. If they are scattered,
nobody can read the frame at a glance, and it is just a box of beans again.
