Lily counts them one by one: "one, two, three, ..., twenty-four". Her brother just looks at the rows and says "6,
12, 18, 24". He is much faster.

Then they turn the tray around, so it has 6 rows of 4. "Now there are more rows," says Lily, "so there must be more
muffins." Is she right?

In this lesson you will learn a new way to count things that come in **equal groups**. It is called
**multiplication**, and it answers both of these puzzles.

## The idea

How many apples? You could count all 12 one by one. But each bag has the **same** number, so you can count by threes:
"3, 6, 9, 12". There are **12** apples.

Groups that all have the same number in them are called **equal groups**. Counting equal groups is so useful that it
has its own sign and its own name. We write

**4 × 3 = 12**

and we say "**four times three** is twelve", or "**four groups of three** is twelve". The sign **×** is called the
**times** sign, and this kind of sum is called **multiplying**. The answer, 12, is called the **product**.

In this lesson, 4 × 3 means "4 groups of 3": the first number says **how many groups**, and the second says **how
many in each group**. (Some books use the other order. It does not change the answer, as you will soon see.)

### Three ways to find a product

**1. Repeated adding.** 4 groups of 3 is 3 + 3 + 3 + 3 = 12. Multiplying is a short way of writing an adding sum
where every number is the same. 4 × 3 is "add 3, four times".

**2. Skip counting.** Count by the size of the group, once for each group: "3, 6, 9, 12". You said four numbers, one
for each bag. On a number line, it is 4 equal jumps of 3, starting at 0.

**3. An array.** Put the things in neat **rows** and **columns**. A row goes across, like people in a line. A
column goes up and down, like the floors of a building. A picture like this is called an **array**. The muffin tray is
an array with 4 rows and 6 columns: 4 × 6 = 24 muffins.

### Groups must be equal

Multiplying only works when the groups are **equal**. If one bag has 3 apples, another has 2 and another has 5, you
cannot say "3 bags of 3" or "3 bags of 5". There is no single size for the groups. You have to add: 3 + 2 + 5 = 10. Before you
multiply, always check: are the groups the same size?

Now it has 4 rows of 3. But it is the **same dots**! Nothing was added or taken away. So

**3 × 4 = 4 × 3 = 12.**

This works for any two numbers, because every array can be turned. So 6 × 4 = 4 × 6, and 2 × 9 = 9 × 2. You can
multiply two numbers in **either order** and get the same product.

That answers Lily: 4 rows of 6 and 6 rows of 4 are the same tray turned around, so both have **24** muffins. More rows,
but fewer muffins in each row.

This is very handy. 2 × 9 means "2 groups of 9", which is easy: 9 + 9 = 18. But 9 × 2 means "9 groups of 2", which
takes longer to add: 2 + 2 + 2 + 2 + 2 + 2 + 2 + 2 + 2. Since they are equal, you can always pick the easier one.

### Special numbers: 1, 0, 2, 5 and 10

- **Times 1.** 7 × 1 is 7 groups of 1, which is 7. And 1 × 7 is one group of 7, which is also 7. Multiplying by 1
  changes nothing.
- **Times 0.** 5 × 0 is 5 empty bags: 0 apples. And 0 × 5 is no bags at all: 0 apples. Multiplying by 0 always gives
  0.
- **Times 2.** 2 groups of something is a **double**. 2 × 8 = 8 + 8 = 16.
- **Times 5.** Count by fives: 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, ... The answers always end in 5 or 0. A clock face helps: the
  minute hand counts by fives as it goes from number to number.
- **Times 10.** Count by tens: 10, 20, 30, ... 7 × 10 is 7 tens, which is 70. The 7 just moves into the tens place.

### Building a new fact from one you know

Suppose you know 5 × 4 = 20, but not 6 × 4. Six groups of 4 is five groups of 4 **and one more group** of 4. So
6 × 4 = 20 + 4 = **24**.

Or you know 2 × 7 = 14. Then 4 × 7 is **double** that, because 4 groups is two lots of 2 groups: 14 + 14 = **28**.

## Worked examples

**1. Equal groups.** There are 5 spiders. Each spider has 8 legs. How many legs?

5 groups of 8: 5 × 8. Skip count by 8: 8, 16, 24, 32, 40. So there are **40** legs. (Or turn it around: 8 × 5 is
counting by fives eight times: 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, 30, 35, 40. Same answer.)

**2. Reading an array.** A window has 3 rows of 5 panes. How many panes?

3 × 5. Count by fives, three times: 5, 10, 15. **15** panes.

**3. Write it as multiplying.** Write 6 + 6 + 6 + 6 + 6 as a multiplication.

There are five 6s, so it is 5 groups of 6: **5 × 6 = 30**.

**4. Choosing the easy order.** Find 9 × 2.

Turn it around: 2 × 9 = 9 + 9 = **18**.

**5. Building a fact.** Find 6 × 5 if you know 5 × 5 = 25.

6 groups of 5 is 5 groups of 5 and one more 5: 25 + 5 = **30**.

**6. Mars wheels.** A Mars rover has 6 wheels. How many wheels do 3 rovers have?

3 groups of 6: 6, 12, 18. **18** wheels.

## Try it

1. Write as a multiplication, then find the answer: 4 + 4 + 4.
2. There are 6 cars with 4 wheels each. How many wheels?
3. Draw an array with 2 rows of 7. How many dots? Then turn it: how many rows, and how many in each row?
4. Find 7 × 10 and 10 × 7.
5. Find 0 × 9 and 9 × 1.
6. A box holds 3 bags of 5 marbles and 1 bag of 4 marbles. Can you find the total as one multiplication? Why or why
   not? Find the total.
7. You know 5 × 7 = 35. Use it to find 6 × 7.
8. Which is bigger: 4 × 8 or 8 × 4? Explain without working out either one.

??? note "Answers"
    1. Three 4s: **3 × 4 = 12**.
    2. 6 × 4: skip count by 4 six times, 4, 8, 12, 16, 20, 24. **24** wheels.
    3. 2 × 7 = 7 + 7 = **14** dots. Turned, it has **7 rows of 2**: 7 × 2 = 14, the same dots.
    4. Both are **70**. 7 groups of 10 is 7 tens; 10 groups of 7 is the same array turned.
    5. 0 × 9 = **0** (no groups, so nothing). 9 × 1 = **9** (nine groups of one).
    6. **Not as one** multiplication, because the groups are not equal (three 5s and a 4). Do it in two parts:
       3 × 5 = 15, then 15 + 4 = **19** marbles.
    7. 6 groups of 7 is 5 groups of 7 and one more 7: 35 + 7 = **42**.
    8. **Neither**; they are equal. An array of 4 rows of 8 turned on its side is 8 rows of 4, with the same dots.
       (Both are 32.)

## Common mistake

**Adding instead of multiplying.** "3 bags of 4" is not 3 + 4 = 7. It is 4 + 4 + 4 = 12. Ask yourself: "Do I have
3 things and 4 things, or 3 **groups** of 4 things?" Drawing the groups for a moment usually makes it clear.

**Multiplying unequal groups.** If the groups are different sizes, multiplying gives the wrong answer. Add them
instead.
