# Equal and comparing fractions

*Numbers · level 3, lesson 6 of 7*

## A question to start

Two friends order the same size of pizza. Ana's is cut into 3 slices and she eats 2. Ben's is cut into 6 slices and
he eats 4. Who ate more?

Ben ate more slices. But his slices were smaller. In fact, they ate **exactly the same amount**: $\tfrac23$ and
$\tfrac46$ are two names for the same number. This lesson is about why that happens, how to find all the names of a
fraction, and how to tell which of two fractions is bigger even when they look nothing alike.

Look down the dashed lines. The orange $\tfrac23$ ends at exactly the same place as the orange $\tfrac46$. The blue
$\tfrac12$, $\tfrac24$ and $\tfrac48$ all end at the same place too. Fractions that are the same amount, the same
point on the number line, are called **equivalent fractions** ("equivalent" means "equal in value").

### Why cutting pieces in two keeps the amount the same

Take $\tfrac23$. Now cut **every** third in half. What happens?

- The whole now has twice as many pieces: $3 \times 2 = 6$. So the pieces are sixths.
- The part you had also has twice as many pieces: $2 \times 2 = 4$.
- But you did not add or remove any pizza. You only cut. The amount is the same.

So $\tfrac23 = \tfrac{2 \times 2}{3 \times 2} = \tfrac46$. Cut every piece into 3 instead, and you get
$\tfrac{6}{9}$. Into 4, and you get $\tfrac{8}{12}$. In general:

**Multiplying the numerator and the denominator by the same number gives an equivalent fraction.**

$$\frac23 = \frac46 = \frac69 = \frac{8}{12} = \frac{10}{15} = \cdots$$

A fraction has infinitely many names, one for every way of cutting the pieces smaller.

Be careful: it must be the **same** number on top and bottom, and it must be **multiplying** (or dividing). Adding
does not work: $\tfrac{2+1}{3+1} = \tfrac34$, and $\tfrac34$ is bigger than $\tfrac23$ (look at the strips).

### Going the other way: simplifying

We can also **glue pieces together**. In $\tfrac{6}{8}$, glue the eighths together in pairs. There are now 4
pieces in the whole (quarters), and you have 3 of them:

$$\frac68 = \frac{6 \div 2}{8 \div 2} = \frac34$$

Dividing the top and bottom by the same number is called **simplifying**. A fraction is in **simplest form** (or
**lowest terms**) when no whole number bigger than 1 divides both the top and the bottom. $\tfrac34$ is in simplest
form; $\tfrac68$ is not, because 2 divides both 6 and 8.

To simplify quickly, divide by the biggest number that goes into both. $\tfrac{12}{18}$: 6 goes into both, so
$\tfrac{12}{18} = \tfrac23$. If you only spot 2 at first, that is fine: $\tfrac{12}{18} = \tfrac69$, then 3 goes into
both again: $\tfrac69 = \tfrac23$. You get there in two steps.

### Comparing fractions

Which is bigger? There are four good ways, and the trick is to pick the easiest.

**1. Same denominator: compare numerators.** $\tfrac58$ and $\tfrac38$ are both eighths, so they are the same size
of piece. 5 pieces beat 3 pieces: $\tfrac58 > \tfrac38$.

**2. Same numerator: compare the size of the pieces.** $\tfrac35$ and $\tfrac37$ both have 3 pieces. Fifths are
bigger than sevenths (fewer people sharing). So 3 fifths beat 3 sevenths: $\tfrac35 > \tfrac37$. With the same
numerator, the **smaller** denominator wins.

**3. Make a common denominator.** For $\tfrac23$ and $\tfrac34$, find a size of piece that both can be cut into.
Twelfths work, because 12 is in the 3 times table and the 4 times table:

$$\frac23 = \frac{8}{12} \qquad \frac34 = \frac{9}{12}$$

Now they are both twelfths, and 9 beats 8. So $\tfrac34 > \tfrac23$, by just $\tfrac{1}{12}$.

A quick way to find a common denominator is to multiply the two denominators ($3 \times 4 = 12$). It always works,
though sometimes a smaller one works too (for halves and quarters, quarters are enough).

**4. Use a benchmark.** A **benchmark** is a fraction you know well, like $\tfrac12$ or 1, used to compare others.
Which is bigger, $\tfrac37$ or $\tfrac59$?

- $\tfrac37$: half of 7 is $3\tfrac12$, and 3 is less than that. So $\tfrac37$ is **less than** $\tfrac12$.
- $\tfrac59$: half of 9 is $4\tfrac12$, and 5 is more. So $\tfrac59$ is **more than** $\tfrac12$.

So $\tfrac37 < \tfrac12 < \tfrac59$, and $\tfrac59$ is bigger. No common denominator needed!

Another benchmark: **how far from 1?** Which is bigger, $\tfrac78$ or $\tfrac{9}{10}$? $\tfrac78$ is $\tfrac18$ short
of a whole; $\tfrac{9}{10}$ is $\tfrac1{10}$ short. A tenth is a smaller gap than an eighth, so $\tfrac{9}{10}$ is
closer to 1, and bigger.

## Worked examples

**1. Find the missing number.** $\tfrac34 = \tfrac{\square}{20}$.

The denominator went from 4 to 20, which is times 5. Do the same to the top: $3 \times 5 = 15$. So
$\tfrac34 = \tfrac{15}{20}$.

**2. Simplify.** Write $\tfrac{18}{24}$ in simplest form.

6 divides both 18 and 24: $\tfrac{18 \div 6}{24 \div 6} = \tfrac34$. 3 and 4 have no common factor bigger than 1, so
we are done.

**3. Compare with a common denominator.** Which is bigger, $\tfrac56$ or $\tfrac79$?

$6 \times 3 = 18$ and $9 \times 2 = 18$, so eighteenths work. $\tfrac56 = \tfrac{15}{18}$ and
$\tfrac79 = \tfrac{14}{18}$. So $\tfrac56$ is bigger, by $\tfrac{1}{18}$.

**4. Put in order.** Order from smallest: $\tfrac12$, $\tfrac25$, $\tfrac{7}{10}$.

Use tenths: $\tfrac12 = \tfrac{5}{10}$ and $\tfrac25 = \tfrac{4}{10}$. Order: $\tfrac25$, $\tfrac12$, $\tfrac{7}{10}$.

**5. Benchmark.** Which is bigger, $\tfrac{4}{9}$ or $\tfrac{6}{11}$?

$\tfrac49$ is less than a half (half of 9 is $4\tfrac12$). $\tfrac{6}{11}$ is more than a half (half of 11 is
$5\tfrac12$). So $\tfrac{6}{11}$ is bigger.

## Try it

1. Write three fractions equivalent to $\tfrac35$.
2. Fill in: (a) $\tfrac23 = \tfrac{\square}{12}$ (b) $\tfrac{5}{\square} = \tfrac{20}{24}$
   (c) $\tfrac{\square}{7} = \tfrac{12}{28}$
3. Simplify: (a) $\tfrac{10}{15}$ (b) $\tfrac{16}{20}$ (c) $\tfrac{21}{28}$ (d) $\tfrac{24}{36}$.
4. Which is bigger? (a) $\tfrac47$ or $\tfrac67$ (b) $\tfrac25$ or $\tfrac28$ (c) $\tfrac34$ or $\tfrac58$
5. Use $\tfrac12$ to decide: which is bigger, $\tfrac{5}{12}$ or $\tfrac{4}{7}$?
6. Order from smallest to biggest: $\tfrac23$, $\tfrac12$, $\tfrac56$, $\tfrac14$.
7. Leo says $\tfrac23 = \tfrac34$ "because I added 1 to the top and bottom". Explain why Leo is wrong.
8. Harder: find a fraction that lies between $\tfrac13$ and $\tfrac12$.

??? note "Answers"
    1. For example $\tfrac{6}{10}$ (times 2), $\tfrac{9}{15}$ (times 3), $\tfrac{12}{20}$ (times 4).
    2. (a) $3 \times 4 = 12$, so $2 \times 4 = 8$: $\tfrac{8}{12}$. (b) $5 \times 4 = 20$, so $24 \div 4 = 6$:
       $\tfrac56$. (c) $28 \div 4 = 7$, so $12 \div 4 = 3$: $\tfrac37$.
    3. (a) Divide by 5: $\tfrac23$. (b) Divide by 4: $\tfrac45$. (c) Divide by 7: $\tfrac34$. (d) Divide by 12:
       $\tfrac23$.
    4. (a) Same denominator: $\tfrac67$. (b) Same numerator, fifths are bigger pieces: $\tfrac25$.
       (c) $\tfrac34 = \tfrac68$, which is more than $\tfrac58$: $\tfrac34$.
    5. Half of 12 is 6, and 5 is less, so $\tfrac{5}{12} < \tfrac12$. Half of 7 is $3\tfrac12$, and 4 is more, so
       $\tfrac47 > \tfrac12$. So $\tfrac47$ is bigger.
    6. In twelfths: $\tfrac{8}{12}$, $\tfrac{6}{12}$, $\tfrac{10}{12}$, $\tfrac{3}{12}$. Order: $\tfrac14$,
       $\tfrac12$, $\tfrac23$, $\tfrac56$.
    7. Adding the same number to top and bottom does not keep a fraction the same. In twelfths,
       $\tfrac23 = \tfrac{8}{12}$ and $\tfrac34 = \tfrac{9}{12}$, which are different. Only multiplying (or dividing)
       top and bottom by the same number keeps the amount, because that is cutting (or gluing) every piece the same
       way.
    8. Use a common denominator: $\tfrac13 = \tfrac26$ and $\tfrac12 = \tfrac36$. There is no whole number between 2
       and 3, so cut smaller: $\tfrac13 = \tfrac{4}{12}$ and $\tfrac12 = \tfrac{6}{12}$. So $\tfrac{5}{12}$ is between
       them. (Many others work too, such as $\tfrac25$: $\tfrac13 = \tfrac{5}{15}$, $\tfrac25 = \tfrac{6}{15}$, and
       $\tfrac25 < \tfrac12$ since 2 is less than half of 5.)

## Common mistake

**Adding to the top and bottom.** $\tfrac12$ and $\tfrac23$ are not equivalent, even though you can get one from the
other by adding 1 to the top and the bottom. Only **multiplying or dividing** both by the same number keeps a
fraction the same.

**Thinking bigger numbers mean a bigger fraction.** $\tfrac{5}{12}$ has bigger numbers than $\tfrac12$, but it is
smaller. Compare the amounts, not the size of the numbers written down.
