# Fractions are numbers

*Numbers · level 3, lesson 5 of 7*

## A question to start

Where is $\tfrac34$ on a number line? Is it between 3 and 4? Between 0 and 1? Somewhere else?

And here is a stranger one: can a fraction be **bigger than 1**? Is $\tfrac74$ a real thing, or a mistake? After
all, how can you have 7 quarters of a pizza when a pizza only has 4 quarters?

Until now you have met fractions as **pieces of something**: half a cake, a third of a class. In this lesson you will
see fractions as **numbers** in their own right, with their own places on the number line, in between the whole
numbers. That small change of view makes everything about fractions easier.

## The idea

### The two numbers in a fraction

A fraction like $\tfrac34$ (say "three quarters", or "three fourths") has two parts:

- The bottom number, the **denominator**, tells you **how many equal parts the whole is cut into**. Here, 4. It
  names the size of the pieces: quarters. ("Denominator" comes from the Latin word for "namer".)
- The top number, the **numerator**, tells you **how many of those parts** you have. Here, 3. ("Numerator" means
  "counter".)

A fraction with numerator 1, like $\tfrac14$, is a **unit fraction**: one single piece. Every other fraction is
made of copies of a unit fraction: $\tfrac34$ is three copies of $\tfrac14$, just as 3 apples is three copies of
one apple.

**The parts must be equal.** If a cake is cut into 4 pieces of different sizes, one piece is not a quarter. A
quarter is a piece such that exactly 4 of them make the whole.

### Fractions live on the number line

Draw a number line with 0 and 1. Cut the gap from 0 to 1 into 4 equal steps. Each step is $\tfrac14$ long. Starting
at 0 and taking 3 of those steps, you land on **$\tfrac34$**.

So $\tfrac34$ is a point **between 0 and 1**, closer to 1. It is a number, just like 3 is a number. It is a
distance from 0: three quarters of the way to 1.

This view answers a lot of questions at once:

- $\tfrac44$ lands exactly on 1. Four quarters make one whole.
- $\tfrac04$ is no steps at all: it is 0.
- Keep stepping past 1 and you reach $\tfrac54$, $\tfrac64$, $\tfrac74$, $\tfrac84 = 2$, and so on. There is no
  reason to stop at 1!

### Fractions bigger than one

So yes, $\tfrac74$ is a real number. It means 7 quarters. You cannot cut 7 quarters from **one** pizza, but you can
from **two**. On the number line, $\tfrac74$ is 7 quarter-steps from 0: past 1, and $\tfrac14$ short of 2.

A fraction whose numerator is as big as its denominator, or bigger, is called an **improper fraction**. ("Improper"
is an old word; there is nothing wrong with these fractions at all. In later years you will use them more than any
other kind.)

The same number can also be written as a whole number and a fraction together. $\tfrac74$ is 1 whole and 3 quarters
more, written $1\tfrac34$ ("one and three quarters"). This is called a **mixed number**.

### Changing between the two ways of writing

**Improper to mixed: divide.** How many wholes are in $\tfrac{17}{5}$? Every 5 fifths make one whole, so ask how
many 5s fit in 17: $17 \div 5 = 3$ remainder 2. So 3 wholes and 2 fifths left over:

$$\frac{17}{5} = 3\tfrac25$$

**Mixed to improper: multiply and add.** How many fifths are in $3\tfrac25$? Each whole is 5 fifths, so 3 wholes
are $3 \times 5 = 15$ fifths. Add the 2 extra fifths: 17 fifths.

$$3\tfrac25 = \frac{3 \times 5 + 2}{5} = \frac{17}{5}$$

These two moves undo each other, just as division and multiplication do.

### A fraction is also a division

Here is the most surprising fact in this lesson. Share 3 pizzas among 4 people. How much does each person get?

Cut every pizza into quarters. That makes 12 quarters. Shared among 4 people, each gets 3 quarters. So

$$3 \div 4 = \frac34$$

This always works: **$\tfrac{a}{b}$ is the same as $a \div b$**. The fraction bar is a division sign. That is why
$\tfrac{12}{4} = 3$ (12 shared by 4) and why $\tfrac{7}{3}$, which is $7 \div 3 = 2$ r 1, is $2\tfrac13$: the
remainder from the last lesson has become the fraction part.

So the leftover 1 pizza in "7 pizzas shared by 3" is not a problem anymore. It is cut into 3 parts, and each person
gets $\tfrac13$ of it.

### Wholes can be anything

A fraction is always a fraction **of** something. Half of a small pizza is less pizza than half of a large one. On
the number line, the whole is the length from 0 to 1. In a story, ask: "a third of **what**?" A third of a class of
24 is 8 children; a third of a sol (a Mars day, about 24 hours and 40 minutes) is a bit more than 8 hours.

## Worked examples

**1. Place a fraction.** Where is $\tfrac25$ on the number line?

Cut the gap from 0 to 1 into 5 equal steps (fifths). Take 2 steps from 0. It is a little less than halfway to 1.

**2. Improper to mixed.** Write $\tfrac{23}{4}$ as a mixed number.

$23 \div 4 = 5$ r 3 (because $4 \times 5 = 20$). So $\tfrac{23}{4} = 5\tfrac34$. Check: $5 \times 4 + 3 = 23$. ✓

**3. Mixed to improper.** Write $4\tfrac23$ as an improper fraction.

4 wholes are $4 \times 3 = 12$ thirds. Plus 2 thirds is 14 thirds: $4\tfrac23 = \tfrac{14}{3}$.

**4. Between which whole numbers?** Between which two whole numbers is $\tfrac{30}{7}$?

$7 \times 4 = 28$ and $7 \times 5 = 35$. So 30 sevenths is more than 4 wholes but less than 5: it is between **4
and 5**. Exactly, $\tfrac{30}{7} = 4\tfrac27$.

**5. Fraction as division.** 5 friends share 2 big chocolate bars equally. How much does each get?

$2 \div 5 = \tfrac25$ of a bar. Check: cut each bar into 5 pieces, making 10 pieces. 10 pieces among 5 friends is 2
pieces each, and each piece is a fifth of a bar. ✓

## Common mistake

**Thinking the bigger denominator means the bigger fraction.** $\tfrac18$ is **smaller** than $\tfrac14$, not
bigger. Cutting a whole into more pieces makes each piece smaller. 8 people sharing a pizza get less each than 4
people do.

**Unequal parts.** A shape cut into 4 pieces of different sizes does not show quarters. Before you name a fraction,
check that the parts are equal.
