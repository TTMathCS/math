# 9. Diophantine equations

*Which whole numbers $a, b, c$ satisfy $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$? Are there any with $a^4 + b^4 = c^4$? Which $x, y$ solve
$x^2 - 61y^2 = 1$? An equation whose solutions must be whole numbers (or fractions) is called a Diophantine equation,
after Diophantus of Alexandria, whose* Arithmetica *(probably third century CE) collected problems of this kind. This
chapter solves the first equation completely, proves the second impossible with Fermat's method of infinite descent,
and shows that the third always has infinitely many solutions forming a group. It then tours some famous equations,
solved and unsolved, and ends with a theorem that puts a limit on the whole subject: no algorithm can decide whether
an arbitrary Diophantine equation has a solution.*

!!! abstract "In this chapter"
    **You will learn:**

    - every Pythagorean triple, by algebra and by drawing lines through a point on a circle
    - Fermat's method of infinite descent, and a full proof that $x^4 + y^4 = z^2$ has no solutions in positive integers
    - Pell's equation $x^2 - dy^2 = 1$: why its solutions form a group, generated by one fundamental solution
    - how congruences show that an equation has no solutions, and where that test stops working
    - Markov's equation, Catalan's equation, sums of three cubes and other famous cases
    - Hilbert's tenth problem and Matiyasevich's theorem: there is no general method

    **You need:** [Chapter 2](02-Primes-and-Unique-Factorization.md) (unique factorization),
    [Chapter 3](03-GCD-and-Euclids-Algorithm.md) (gcd; linear Diophantine equations), [Chapter 4](04-Congruences.md)
    (congruences) and [Chapter 8](08-Quadratic-Residues-and-Reciprocity.md) for some arguments mod $p$. The gentler
    versions are [Pythagorean triples](../../topics/number-theory/level-7/05-pythagorean-triples.md) and

    9.5 is expert (the ideas, not the full proof).

## 9.1 Pythagorean triples

The triangle with sides 3, 4, 5 has a right angle, because $3^2 + 4^2 = 9 + 16 = 25 = 5^2$. A Babylonian clay tablet
known as Plimpton 322 (about 1800 BCE) lists fifteen rows of numbers that are read today as sides of such triangles,
some as large as $12\,709$, $13\,500$, $18\,541$; so the question of finding them is very old.

!!! note "Definition"
    A **Pythagorean triple** is a triple $(a, b, c)$ of positive integers with $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$. It is **primitive**
    if $\gcd(a, b, c) = 1$.

Multiplying a triple by any $k$ gives another ($6, 8, 10$ from $3, 4, 5$). Conversely, if $d = \gcd(a, b, c)$, dividing
by $d$ gives a primitive triple. So it is enough to find the primitive ones. Note that in a primitive triple any two of
$a, b, c$ are already coprime: a prime dividing $a$ and $b$ divides $c^2$, hence $c$; a prime dividing $a$ and $c$
divides $b^2$, hence $b$.

!!! note "Theorem 9.1 (Pythagorean triples)"
    The primitive Pythagorean triples with $b$ even are exactly

    $$
    a = m^2 - n^2, \qquad b = 2mn, \qquad c = m^2 + n^2,
    $$

    where $m > n > 0$ are coprime integers of opposite parity (one even, one odd). Each primitive triple arises from
    exactly one such pair.

*Proof.* **These are triples:** $(m^2 - n^2)^2 + (2mn)^2 = m^4 - 2m^2n^2 + n^4 + 4m^2n^2 = (m^2 + n^2)^2$.
**They are primitive:** a prime $p$ dividing $a$ and $c$ divides their sum $2m^2$ and difference $2n^2$; $p \ne 2$ since
$c$ is odd ($m, n$ have opposite parity); so $p \mid m$ and $p \mid n$, contradicting $\gcd(m, n) = 1$.

**Every primitive triple has this form.** Let $(a, b, c)$ be primitive. Not both $a, b$ are even (they are coprime).
Not both are odd: odd squares are $\equiv 1 \pmod 4$, so $c^2 \equiv 2 \pmod 4$, which no square is. So exactly one is
even; call it $b$. Then $a$ and $c$ are odd, and

$$
b^2 = c^2 - a^2 = (c - a)(c + a), \qquad \left(\frac b2\right)^2 = \frac{c + a}{2} \cdot \frac{c - a}{2} ,
$$

where both fractions are whole numbers, since $c \pm a$ are even. Let $u = (c+a)/2$ and $v = (c-a)/2$. Any common
divisor of $u$ and $v$ divides $u + v = c$ and $u - v = a$, so $\gcd(u, v) = 1$. Two coprime positive integers whose
product is a square must each be squares (look at each prime's exponent, by unique factorization). So $u = m^2$ and
$v = n^2$ with $m > n > 0$, and

$$
c = u + v = m^2 + n^2, \qquad a = u - v = m^2 - n^2, \qquad b = 2\sqrt{uv} = 2mn .
$$

Here $\gcd(m, n) = 1$ because $\gcd(u, v) = 1$, and $m, n$ have opposite parity because $c$ is odd. Finally $m, n$ are
determined by the triple ($m^2 = (c+a)/2$, $n^2 = (c-a)/2$), which gives uniqueness. $\square$

**Worked example.** The pairs $(m, n)$ with $m \le 4$: $(2, 1) \to (3, 4, 5)$; $(3, 2) \to (5, 12, 13)$;
$(4, 1) \to (15, 8, 17)$; $(4, 3) \to (7, 24, 25)$. ($(3, 1)$ is excluded since both are odd: it gives $(8, 6, 10)$, not
primitive.) There are exactly 16 primitive triples with $c < 100$; the largest is $(65, 72, 97)$ from $(m, n) = (9, 4)$.

### The same answer by geometry

Divide $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$ by $c^2$: $(a/c)^2 + (b/c)^2 = 1$. So primitive triples correspond to **rational points** on
the unit circle $x^2 + y^2 = 1$, points whose coordinates are both fractions. There is a beautiful way to find them all.

Start from the obvious rational point $A = (-1, 0)$. Draw the line through $A$ with slope $t$. It meets the circle in
one more point $P$. If $t$ is rational, so is $P$; and conversely, if $P$ is rational, the slope of $AP$ is rational.

To find $P$, substitute $y = t(x + 1)$ into $x^2 + y^2 = 1$:

$$
x^2 + t^2(x+1)^2 = 1 \iff (x + 1)\big((1 + t^2)x - (1 - t^2)\big) = 0 .
$$

The root $x = -1$ is $A$; the other root gives

$$
P = \left( \frac{1 - t^2}{1 + t^2},\ \frac{2t}{1 + t^2} \right) .
$$

With $t = n/m$ this is $\big((m^2 - n^2)/(m^2 + n^2),\ 2mn/(m^2 + n^2)\big)$: the formulas of Theorem 9.1 again. In the
picture $t = 1/2$ gives $P = (3/5, 4/5)$, the 3-4-5 triangle.

This "chord method" is the first example of a principle that runs through the rest of the book: *a curve's rational
points are governed by its geometry.* A conic (degree 2) with one rational point has infinitely many, all given by
one parameter $t$. A cubic is different: a line through one rational point usually meets it in two more, which need not
be rational, and through two rational points in a third, which is. That leads to the group of points on an elliptic
curve ([Chapter 19](19-Elliptic-Curves.md)).

And not every conic has a rational point. The circle $x^2 + y^2 = 3$ has none: if $(a/c)^2 + (b/c)^2 = 3$ with
$\gcd(a, b, c) = 1$ then $a^2 + b^2 = 3c^2$. Mod 3, squares are 0 or 1, so $a^2 + b^2 \equiv 0$ forces $3 \mid a$ and
$3 \mid b$; then $9 \mid 3c^2$, so $3 \mid c$, contradicting $\gcd = 1$.

## 9.2 Fermat's descent

*Level: advanced.*

In the margin of his copy of Diophantus, next to the problem on Pythagorean triples, Pierre de Fermat wrote (around
1637) that it is impossible to split a cube into two cubes, or a fourth power into two fourth powers, or in general
any power higher than the second into two like powers, and that he had a truly marvellous proof which the margin was
too narrow to contain. The note was published by his son in 1670, after Fermat's death. The general claim became
**Fermat's last theorem**, proved by Andrew Wiles in 1994 ([Chapter 20](20-Modular-Forms-and-Fermats-Last-Theorem.md)).
For the fourth power, though, Fermat did leave an argument, and it introduced a method.

**Infinite descent** is a form of proof by contradiction. Show that from any positive integer solution you can build
another with a strictly smaller positive size. Then there would be an infinite strictly decreasing sequence of positive
integers, which is impossible. Equivalently: take a solution with the smallest size, and produce a smaller one.

!!! note "Theorem 9.2 (Fermat)"
    The equation $x^4 + y^4 = z^2$ has no solutions in positive integers.

*Proof.* Suppose there are solutions, and choose one with $z$ as small as possible.

**Step 1: $\gcd(x, y) = 1$.** If a prime $p$ divides both $x$ and $y$, then $p^4 \mid z^2$, so $p^2 \mid z$, and
$(x/p, y/p, z/p^2)$ is a solution with smaller $z$.

**Step 2: a primitive triple.** $(x^2)^2 + (y^2)^2 = z^2$, and $\gcd(x^2, y^2) = 1$, so $(x^2, y^2, z)$ is a primitive
Pythagorean triple. Exactly one of $x^2, y^2$ is even; say $y$ is even. By Theorem 9.1 there are coprime $m > n > 0$ of
opposite parity with

$$
x^2 = m^2 - n^2, \qquad y^2 = 2mn, \qquad z = m^2 + n^2 .
$$

**Step 3: a second triple.** The first equation says $x^2 + n^2 = m^2$, with $\gcd(m, n) = 1$: another primitive
triple. Here $x$ is odd, so $n$ must be the even member: if $n$ were odd and $m$ even, then $x^2 = m^2 - n^2 \equiv 0 -
1 \equiv 3 \pmod 4$, impossible. Applying Theorem 9.1 again, there are coprime $r > s > 0$ with

$$
x = r^2 - s^2, \qquad n = 2rs, \qquad m = r^2 + s^2 .
$$

**Step 4: three squares.** Now $y^2 = 2mn = 4rs(r^2 + s^2)$, so $(y/2)^2 = r \cdot s \cdot (r^2 + s^2)$. The three
factors are pairwise coprime: $\gcd(r, s) = 1$, and a prime dividing $r$ and $r^2 + s^2$ would divide $s^2$. A product
of pairwise coprime positive integers that is a square has each factor a square. So $r = u^2$, $s = v^2$,
$r^2 + s^2 = w^2$ for positive integers $u, v, w$.

**Step 5: descent.** Then $u^4 + v^4 = r^2 + s^2 = w^2$: a new solution. And $w \le w^2 = m \le m^2 < m^2 + n^2 = z$
(the last step uses $n > 0$). This contradicts the minimality of $z$. $\square$

**Corollary 9.3.** $x^4 + y^4 = z^4$ has no solutions in positive integers, since $(x, y, z^2)$ would solve Theorem 9.2.
This is Fermat's last theorem for exponent 4. It has a useful consequence: if $n$ is divisible by 4, then
$x^n + y^n = z^n$ has no solutions either ($x^n = (x^{n/4})^4$). Every $n \ge 3$ is divisible by 4 or by an odd prime,
so Fermat's last theorem reduces to the case of odd prime exponents. Euler handled $n = 3$ (1770, with a gap later
filled), Dirichlet and Legendre $n = 5$ (around 1825), Lamé $n = 7$ (1839), and Kummer a large class of primes in the
1840s ([Algebra, Chapter 18](../algebra/18-Factorization-in-Rings.md)).

**Fermat's own application.** The same descent (applied to $x^4 - y^4 = z^2$, a close cousin) proves that the area
$ab/2$ of a right triangle with whole-number sides is never a perfect square. Equivalently, 1 is not a **congruent
number**: no right triangle with rational sides has area 1. The congruent number problem, which asks which whole
numbers are areas of rational right triangles, leads straight to elliptic curves and the Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer
conjecture ([Chapter 19](19-Elliptic-Curves.md)).

**Why descent works so often.** In Step 2 to Step 4, the equation was taken apart using unique factorization ("coprime
factors of a square are squares"). That only works in rings where unique factorization holds. In the 1840s attempts to
prove Fermat's last theorem by factoring $x^p + y^p$ over the complex numbers ran into exactly this obstacle, and the
repair (ideals, the class number) became algebraic number theory ([Chapter 17](17-Algebraic-Number-Theory.md)).

## 9.3 Pell's equation

*Level: advanced.*

Look for whole numbers with $x^2 - 2y^2 = 1$. A small search finds $(3, 2)$: $9 - 8 = 1$. The next is $(17, 12)$:
$289 - 288 = 1$. Then $(99, 70)$, $(577, 408)$. The fractions $3/2, 17/12, 99/70, 577/408$ are very good approximations
of $\sqrt 2 \approx 1.4142136$ (indeed $577/408 \approx 1.4142157$), and that is no coincidence: $x^2 - 2y^2 = 1$
means $x/y = \sqrt{2 + 1/y^2}$.

!!! note "Definition"
    **Pell's equation** is $x^2 - dy^2 = 1$, where $d$ is a positive integer that is not a perfect square, to be solved
    in integers $x, y$.

The solutions $(\pm 1, 0)$ are **trivial**. (If $d$ were a square $k^2$, then $(x - ky)(x + ky) = 1$ would force only
the trivial solutions; that is why squares are excluded.) The name is a historical accident: Euler attributed the
equation to John Pell, who had little to do with it. It is far older, as we will see.

### Multiplying solutions

The key is to factor the equation using $\sqrt d$:

$$
x^2 - dy^2 = (x + y\sqrt d)(x - y\sqrt d) .
$$

Consider numbers of the form $\alpha = x + y\sqrt d$ with $x, y$ integers. They form a ring, written
$\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt d]$: sums and products stay of that form, since $(x + y\sqrt d)(u + v\sqrt d) = (xu + dyv) + (xv +
yu)\sqrt d$. Its **conjugate** is $\bar\alpha = x - y\sqrt d$, and its **norm** is $N(\alpha) = \alpha\bar\alpha = x^2 -
dy^2$. Since $\sqrt d$ is irrational, $x$ and $y$ are determined by $\alpha$ (see [Chapter 2](02-Primes-and-Unique-Factorization.md)
for irrationality of square roots), and a direct computation shows conjugation respects products:
$\overline{\alpha\beta} = \bar\alpha\bar\beta$. So

$$
N(\alpha\beta) = \alpha\beta\bar\alpha\bar\beta = N(\alpha)N(\beta) .
$$

Pell's equation asks for the $\alpha$ with $N(\alpha) = 1$. By the norm rule, **the product of two solutions is a
solution**. This identity, in the form

$$
(x^2 - dy^2)(u^2 - dv^2) = (xu + dyv)^2 - d(xv + yu)^2 ,
$$

was found by Brahmagupta in 628, in his *Brāhmasphuṭasiddhānta*; he called the combining of solutions *bhāvanā*.

**Worked example.** $(3 + 2\sqrt 2)^2 = 9 + 12\sqrt 2 + 8 = 17 + 12\sqrt 2$ and $(3 + 2\sqrt 2)^3 = (17 + 12\sqrt
2)(3 + 2\sqrt 2) = 51 + 48 + (34 + 36)\sqrt 2 = 99 + 70\sqrt 2$. The powers of the first solution produce all the
others. In general, from $(x, y)$ the next solution of $x^2 - 2y^2 = 1$ is $(3x + 4y, 2x + 3y)$.

Also, if $N(\alpha) = 1$ then $\alpha^{-1} = \bar\alpha$, again of the same form. So the solutions of Pell's equation,
viewed as numbers $x + y\sqrt d$, form a **group** under multiplication: they are the units of norm 1 in
$\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt d]$.

### Every solution is a power of one

!!! note "Theorem 9.4"
    Suppose Pell's equation $x^2 - dy^2 = 1$ has a nontrivial solution. Let $\varepsilon = x_1 + y_1\sqrt d$ be the
    smallest solution with $\varepsilon > 1$ (the **fundamental solution**). Then the solutions are exactly
    $\pm\varepsilon^k$ for $k \in \mathbb{Z}$. In particular, the positive solutions $(x_k, y_k)$ are given by
    $x_k + y_k\sqrt d = \varepsilon^k$ for $k = 1, 2, 3, \dots$

*Proof.* First, **which $\alpha = x + y\sqrt d$ of norm 1 are greater than 1?** If $\alpha > 1$ then $\bar\alpha =
1/\alpha$ lies in $(0, 1)$, so $x = (\alpha + \bar\alpha)/2 > 0$ and $y\sqrt d = (\alpha - \bar\alpha)/2 > 0$. So
$\alpha > 1$ exactly when $x, y > 0$. Among solutions with $x, y \ge 1$, the size of $\alpha$ grows with $x$ and $y$,
so there is a smallest one, $\varepsilon$, as soon as there is any.

Now let $\eta$ be any solution with $\eta > 1$. Since $\varepsilon > 1$, its powers grow without bound, so there is a
$k \ge 1$ with $\varepsilon^k \le \eta < \varepsilon^{k+1}$. Divide by $\varepsilon^k$: the number $\theta = \eta
\varepsilon^{-k} = \eta\,\bar\varepsilon^{\,k}$ is in $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt d]$, has norm $1 \cdot 1 = 1$, and satisfies
$1 \le \theta < \varepsilon$. If $\theta > 1$ it would be a solution between 1 and $\varepsilon$, contradicting the
choice of $\varepsilon$. So $\theta = 1$ and $\eta = \varepsilon^k$.

Every solution $\eta$ is $\pm 1$, or $\eta > 1$, or $0 < \eta < 1$ (then $1/\eta = \bar\eta > 1$), or negative (then
$-\eta$ is positive). Applying the previous paragraph covers all cases: $\eta = \pm\varepsilon^k$. $\square$

In group language, the solutions form the group $\{\pm 1\} \times \mathbb{Z}$: a sign, and a free exponent. This is the
simplest case of Dirichlet's unit theorem ([Chapter 17](17-Algebraic-Number-Theory.md)).

### Existence

Theorem 9.4 assumed a nontrivial solution exists. It always does:

!!! note "Theorem 9.5 (Lagrange, 1768)"
    For every positive integer $d$ that is not a square, $x^2 - dy^2 = 1$ has a solution with $y \ne 0$, and hence
    infinitely many.

We state it here and prove it in [Chapter 10](10-Continued-Fractions.md), where continued fractions both prove the
theorem and compute the fundamental solution. The idea: Dirichlet's approximation theorem gives infinitely many
fractions $x/y$ with $|x - y\sqrt d| < 1/y$; for these, $|x^2 - dy^2| = |x - y\sqrt d| \cdot |x + y\sqrt d| < 2\sqrt
d + 1$, so some value $N$ of $x^2 - dy^2$ occurs infinitely often; two such pairs that are congruent mod $|N|$ can be
divided to produce a solution of norm 1.

**The fundamental solution can be enormous.** A few examples:

| $d$ | fundamental solution $(x_1, y_1)$ |
|---|---|
| 2 | $(3, 2)$ |
| 3 | $(2, 1)$ |
| 7 | $(8, 3)$ |
| 13 | $(649, 180)$ |
| 61 | $(1\,766\,319\,049,\ 226\,153\,980)$ |
| 109 | $(158\,070\,671\,986\,249,\ 15\,140\,424\,455\,100)$ |

**Check for $d = 13$:** $649^2 = 421\,201$ and $13 \cdot 180^2 = 421\,200$.

The size jumps erratically with $d$: $d = 60$ has $(31, 4)$, while its neighbour 61 needs ten digits. No simple pattern
holds; the fundamental solution can have on the order of $\sqrt d$ digits.

### History

Brahmagupta (628) solved several cases, including $x^2 - 92y^2 = 1$ with $(1151, 120)$, by combining solutions of
$x^2 - dy^2 = k$ for small $k$. Bhāskara II (1150) gave a general method, the *chakravāla* ("cyclic") method, and used
it to solve $d = 61$. In Europe, Fermat in 1657 challenged English mathematicians with the equation, naming $d = 61$
and $d = 109$ as examples; Brouncker and Wallis found a method. Euler studied it with continued fractions; Lagrange gave
the first complete proof of existence in 1768.

A famous instance is the **cattle problem** attributed to Archimedes, a poem that asks for the sizes of herds of the
sun god's cattle under arithmetic conditions. It reduces to a Pell equation with $d = 410\,286\,423\,278\,424$. A. Amthor
showed in 1880 that the smallest answer has 206 545 digits; it was first printed out in full with computer help in
1965 (by Williams, German and Zarnke).

### The negative Pell equation

What about $x^2 - dy^2 = -1$? For $d = 2$: $(1, 1)$, $(7, 5)$, $(41, 29)$, which are the odd powers of $1 + \sqrt 2$
(note $(1 + \sqrt 2)^2 = 3 + 2\sqrt 2$, the fundamental solution of the $+1$ equation). But for $d = 3$ there is no
solution: mod 3 it says $x^2 \equiv -1 \equiv 2$, and 2 is not a square mod 3. More generally, if any prime
$p \equiv 3 \pmod 4$ divides $d$, then $x^2 \equiv -1 \pmod p$ is impossible ([Chapter 8](08-Quadratic-Residues-and-Reciprocity.md)).
Exactly which $d$ allow a solution is subtle; the proportion among suitable $d$ was a long-standing conjecture of
Peter Stevenhagen, on which there has been major recent progress (Koymans and Pagano, 2022).

!!! tip "Try it in Python"
    ```python
    from math import isqrt

    def pell_bruteforce(d, ymax=10**6):
        """Smallest y >= 1 with d*y^2 + 1 a square. Only for small fundamental solutions."""
        for y in range(1, ymax):
            x2 = d * y * y + 1
            x = isqrt(x2)
            if x * x == x2:
                return x, y

    def powers(x1, y1, d, k):
        x, y, out = x1, y1, []
        for _ in range(k):
            out.append((x, y))
            x, y = x1 * x + d * y1 * y, x1 * y + y1 * x
        return out

    print(pell_bruteforce(13))        # (649, 180)
    print(powers(3, 2, 2, 5))         # (3,2) (17,12) (99,70) (577,408) (3363,2378)
    ```
    Brute force fails badly for $d = 61$; [Chapter 10](10-Continued-Fractions.md) gives a method that finds it at once.

## 9.4 Markov's equation and other famous equations

*Level: advanced, with expert statements.*

### Congruence obstructions

The quickest way to show an equation has no solutions is to find a modulus where it has none. Some examples:

- $x^2 + y^2 = 4k + 3$: squares are 0 or 1 mod 4, so a sum of two squares is 0, 1 or 2 mod 4, never 3.
- $x^2 - 3y^2 = 2$: mod 3, $x^2 \equiv 2$, impossible.
- $x^3 + y^3 + z^3 = n$ with $n \equiv 4$ or $5 \pmod 9$: cubes mod 9 are only $0, 1, 8$ (that is $0, \pm 1$), since
  $(3k)^3 \equiv 0$ and $(3k \pm 1)^3 = 27k^3 \pm 27k^2 + 9k \pm 1 \equiv \pm 1$. Three of them add to at most $\pm 3$,
  never $\pm 4$ mod 9.

But the method has limits. An equation can have solutions mod every $n$ and still have no integer solutions. For
quadratic forms, the Hasse-Minkowski theorem says that "solvable mod every $n$ and over the reals" does imply "solvable
in rationals"; for cubic equations it fails, as Selmer's example $3x^3 + 4y^3 + 5z^3 = 0$ shows
([Chapter 18](18-p-adic-Numbers.md)).

### Markov's equation

$$
x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 3xyz
$$

Small solutions: $(1, 1, 1)$, $(1, 1, 2)$, $(1, 2, 5)$, $(1, 5, 13)$, $(2, 5, 29)$. Andrey Markov met this equation in
1879-1880 while studying how well real numbers can be approximated by fractions ([Chapter 10](10-Continued-Fractions.md)):
the numbers appearing in its solutions, the **Markov numbers** $1, 2, 5, 13, 29, 34, 89, 169, 194, 233, 433, 610, 985,
\dots$, describe the worst-approximable numbers and binary quadratic forms with extreme minima.

**Vieta jumping.** Fix $y$ and $z$ and view the equation as a quadratic in $x$:

$$
x^2 - 3yz\,x + (y^2 + z^2) = 0 .
$$

If $x$ is one root, the other is $x' = 3yz - x$ (the roots sum to $3yz$), which is also a positive integer, since
$xx' = y^2 + z^2 > 0$. So from any solution we can jump to a new one by replacing one coordinate: $(x, y, z) \mapsto
(3yz - x, y, z)$. From $(1, 2, 5)$ the three jumps give $(29, 2, 5)$, $(1, 13, 5)$ and $(1, 2, 1)$.

!!! note "Theorem 9.6 (Markov)"
    Every positive solution of $x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 3xyz$ can be reached from $(1, 1, 1)$ by a sequence of such jumps
    (and permutations). The solutions form an infinite tree in which every vertex other than the first two has three
    neighbours.

*Proof (descent).* Take a solution with $x \le y \le z$. Let $f(t) = t^2 - 3xy\,t + (x^2 + y^2)$; its two roots are
$z$ and $z' = 3xy - z$, with $zz' = x^2 + y^2 > 0$, so $z' > 0$. Evaluate $f$ at $y$:

$$
f(y) = y^2 - 3xy^2 + x^2 + y^2 = x^2 - (3x - 2)y^2 \le x^2 - y^2 \le 0 ,
$$

using $3x - 2 \ge 1$ and $y \ge x$. A monic quadratic is $\le 0$ only between its roots, so $z' \le y \le z$.
Equality $f(y) = 0$ needs both inequalities to be equalities: $3x - 2 = 1$ and $y = x$, so $x = y = 1$, and then
$2 + z^2 = 3z$ gives $z = 1$ or $z = 2$. So for every solution other than $(1, 1, 1)$ and $(1, 1, 2)$ we have $f(y) <
0$, hence $z' < y \le z$: jumping in the largest coordinate gives a solution with a strictly smaller sum. Sums of
positive integers cannot decrease forever, so repeated jumps reach $(1, 1, 1)$ or $(1, 1, 2)$, and $(1, 1, 2)$ jumps to
$(1, 1, 1)$. Each jump is its own inverse (jumping twice in the same place returns), so retracing the path leads from
$(1, 1, 1)$ to the solution we started with. $\square$

**The uniqueness conjecture.** In every solution found so far, the largest number determines the other two: there is
only one Markov triple with largest entry 5, one with 13, one with 29, and so on. Frobenius asked in 1913 whether this
always holds. The **Markov uniqueness conjecture** is still open; it has been checked for very large Markov numbers and
proved for some special ones (for example prime powers).

### Catalan's equation

The numbers 8 and 9 are consecutive and both perfect powers: $3^2 - 2^3 = 1$. Eugène Catalan conjectured in 1844 that
this is the only case: $x^a - y^b = 1$ with $x, y, a, b > 1$ has only the solution $3^2 - 2^3$. Preda Mihăilescu proved
it in 2002, with a proof based on cyclotomic fields that is famous for using only classical (if deep) tools.

### Ramanujan-Nagell

For which $n$ is $2^n - 7$ a perfect square? $2^3 - 7 = 1$, $2^4 - 7 = 9$, $2^5 - 7 = 25$, $2^7 - 7 = 121$ and
$2^{15} - 7 = 32\,761 = 181^2$. Ramanujan conjectured in 1913 that these are all; Trygve Nagell proved it in 1948,
working in the ring $\mathbb{Z}[(1 + \sqrt{-7})/2]$, which has unique factorization.

### Sums of three cubes

Which integers $n$ are $x^3 + y^3 + z^3$ with $x, y, z$ integers (negative allowed)? The congruence argument rules out
$n \equiv \pm 4 \pmod 9$, and it is conjectured that every other $n$ is possible, in infinitely many ways. The
solutions can be huge. For $n = 33$, the first solution was found by Andrew Booker in 2019:

$$
33 = 8\,866\,128\,975\,287\,528^3 + (-8\,778\,405\,442\,862\,239)^3 + (-2\,736\,111\,468\,807\,040)^3 ,
$$

and the last case below 100, $n = 42$, was solved by Booker and Andrew Sutherland later in 2019 with a large
distributed computation:

$$
42 = (-80\,538\,738\,812\,075\,974)^3 + 80\,435\,758\,145\,817\,515^3 + 12\,602\,123\,297\,335\,631^3 .
$$

**Check:** Python's integers are exact, so typing either right side gives 33 and 42. Whether every
$n \not\equiv \pm 4 \pmod 9$ is a sum of three cubes is unknown; there is not even an algorithm known to decide it for
a given $n$.

### Fermat's last theorem and beyond

$x^n + y^n = z^n$ has no positive solutions for $n \ge 3$ (Wiles, with Taylor, 1995). Its generalisation
$x^a + y^b = z^c$ with $1/a + 1/b + 1/c < 1$ (the Fermat-Catalan or Beal-type equations) is open in general
([Chapter 20](20-Modular-Forms-and-Fermats-Last-Theorem.md)), and the abc conjecture would control it
([Chapter 21](21-Where-Number-Theory-Goes.md)).

## 9.5 Hilbert's tenth problem

*Level: expert (the ideas; the full proof fills a short book).*

At the International Congress of Mathematicians in Paris in 1900, David Hilbert presented a list of problems for the
new century. The tenth asked for a process which, given any Diophantine equation (a polynomial equation with integer
coefficients in any number of unknowns), decides in a finite number of steps whether it has a solution in integers.

Notice what is and is not asked. Not a formula for the solutions, just a yes-or-no procedure. For a single unknown
there is one (an integer root divides the constant term). For two-variable quadratics there is one (via Pell's equation
and congruences). Hilbert assumed the general answer would also be yes.

It is no. The answer came in stages:

- In the 1930s Gödel, Church and Turing made "algorithm" precise and showed that some yes-or-no problems, such as
  whether a computer program ever stops, have no algorithm.
- Martin Davis (1950s) conjectured that the sets of integers definable by Diophantine equations are exactly the sets a
  computer can list, the **listable** (recursively enumerable) sets.
- Davis, Hilary Putnam and Julia Robinson proved in 1961 that this holds if one also allows the unknowns to appear in
  exponents, and that the exponential case reduces to one fact: a Diophantine set whose growth is exponential.
- Yuri Matiyasevich, in 1970, supplied that fact, using the Fibonacci numbers. He showed that the relation
  $y = F_{2x}$ (Fibonacci numbers) is Diophantine. (Later proofs use the solutions of Pell's equation $x^2 - (a^2 -
  1)y^2 = 1$, which grow exponentially in the same way.)

!!! note "Theorem 9.7 (Matiyasevich-Robinson-Davis-Putnam, 1970)"
    A set of positive integers is listable by a computer program if and only if it is **Diophantine**: there is a
    polynomial $P(a, x_1, \dots, x_m)$ with integer coefficients such that $a$ is in the set exactly when
    $P(a, x_1, \dots, x_m) = 0$ has a solution in non-negative integers $x_1, \dots, x_m$. Consequently, there is no
    algorithm that decides, for every Diophantine equation, whether it has an integer solution.

The second sentence follows from the first: if there were such an algorithm, it would decide membership in every
listable set, including the set of programs that halt, which Turing showed is impossible.

**Strange consequences.**

- **A polynomial for the primes.** The primes are listable, so they are Diophantine. Rearranging gives a polynomial
  whose positive values, as its variables run over the non-negative integers, are exactly the primes. J. P. Jones,
  D. Sato, H. Wada and D. Wiens wrote one down explicitly in 1976, with 26 variables and degree 25. It is useless for
  computing primes, but it exists.
- **Few variables suffice.** Matiyasevich showed that 9 unknowns are enough for a "universal" equation, one able to
  encode every listable set (the proof was written up by Jones in 1982).
- **Undecidable statements.** For any consistent formal system strong enough for arithmetic (such as the usual axioms
  of set theory), there is a specific Diophantine equation that has no solutions, but whose lack of solutions cannot be
  proved in that system. Gödel's incompleteness, translated into a polynomial.
- **Famous problems become equations.** Goldbach's conjecture and the Riemann hypothesis can each be rewritten as "this
  particular Diophantine equation has no solutions".

**What is still open.** The same question **over the rationals**, deciding whether a polynomial equation has a solution
in fractions, is unsolved. It contains, for example, deciding whether an elliptic curve has infinitely many rational
points, which is linked to the Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer conjecture ([Chapter 19](19-Elliptic-Curves.md)). Many
experts suspect that the answer may even be positive for curves, and the analogous questions over rings of integers of
number fields are an active research area.

So the subject of this chapter has no master key. Each family of equations needs its own idea: factorization and descent
for Pythagoras and Fermat, groups for Pell and for elliptic curves, congruences for impossibility, and deep machinery
(modular forms, cyclotomic fields, linear forms in logarithms) for the hardest cases.

## Check yourself

??? question "1. List the primitive Pythagorean triples with $c < 30$."

    From $(m, n) = (2,1), (3,2), (4,1), (4,3), (5,2)$: $(3, 4, 5)$, $(5, 12, 13)$, $(15, 8, 17)$, $(7, 24, 25)$,
    $(21, 20, 29)$. The next pairs, $(5, 4)$ and $(6, 1)$, give $c = 41$ and $37$.

??? question "2. Prove that in every Pythagorean triple, $60$ divides $abc$."

    It suffices to do primitive triples. **4:** $b = 2mn$ and one of $m, n$ is even, so $4 \mid b$. **3:** if $3 \nmid
    a, b$ then $a^2 + b^2 \equiv 2 \pmod 3$, not a square. **5:** squares mod 5 are $0, \pm 1$; if none of $a, b$ is
    divisible by 5, then $a^2 + b^2 \in \{0, \pm 2\}$ mod 5, and it must be a square, so it is 0 and $5 \mid c$.

??? question "3. Which rational point on $x^2 + y^2 = 1$ does the line through $(-1, 0)$ of slope $2/3$ hit? Which triple is it?"

    $x = (1 - 4/9)/(1 + 4/9) = 5/13$ and $y = (4/3)/(13/9) = 12/13$: the triple $(5, 12, 13)$.

??? question "4. Show that $x^2 + y^2 = 3$ has no rational solutions, but $x^2 + y^2 = 2$ has infinitely many."

    The first was shown at the end of 9.1 (descent mod 3). For the second, $(1, 1)$ is a rational point, and lines of
    rational slope through it give infinitely many: with slope $t$, the second point is $\big((t^2 - 2t - 1)/(t^2 + 1),
    (-t^2 - 2t + 1)/(t^2 + 1)\big)$; $t = 2$ gives $(-1/5, -7/5)$, and $1/25 + 49/25 = 2$.

??? question "5. Find the first three positive solutions of $x^2 - 3y^2 = 1$."

    Fundamental: $(2, 1)$. Then $(2 + \sqrt 3)^2 = 7 + 4\sqrt 3$ and $(2 + \sqrt 3)^3 = (7 + 4\sqrt 3)(2 + \sqrt 3) = 26
    + 15\sqrt 3$. Check: $676 - 675 = 1$.

??? question "6. Verify Brahmagupta's solution of $x^2 - 92y^2 = 1$ and find the next one."

    $1151^2 = 1\,324\,801$ and $92 \cdot 120^2 = 1\,324\,800$. The next is $(1151 + 120\sqrt{92})^2 = 1151^2 + 92 \cdot
    120^2 + 2 \cdot 1151 \cdot 120\sqrt{92} = 2\,649\,601 + 276\,240\sqrt{92}$.

??? question "7. Show that $x^2 - 3y^2 = -1$ and $x^2 - 7y^2 = -1$ have no integer solutions."

    Mod 3: $x^2 \equiv -1 \equiv 2$, impossible. Mod 7: $x^2 \equiv -1$, impossible since $7 \equiv 3 \pmod 4$. (Or mod 4
    for both: $x^2 - 3y^2 \equiv x^2 + y^2 \in \{0, 1, 2\}$, never $3 \equiv -1$.)

??? question "8. Starting from the Markov triple $(1, 5, 13)$, find its three neighbours."

    Replace each entry by $3 \times$ (product of the other two) minus itself: $3 \cdot 65 - 1 = 194$, giving
    $(194, 5, 13)$; $3 \cdot 13 - 5 = 34$, giving $(1, 34, 13)$; $3 \cdot 5 - 13 = 2$, giving $(1, 5, 2)$. Check:
    $1 + 1156 + 169 = 1326 = 3 \cdot 1 \cdot 34 \cdot 13$.

??? question "9. Prove that $x^3 + y^3 + z^3 = 2021$ has no integer solutions. Does the same argument work for 2023?"

    $2021 = 224 \cdot 9 + 5$, so $2021 \equiv 5 \equiv -4 \pmod 9$. Each cube is $0$ or $\pm 1$ mod 9, so three cubes sum
    to something in $\{-3, \dots, 3\}$ mod 9, never $\pm 4$. No solutions. For 2023: $2023 = 224 \cdot 9 + 7 \equiv -2
    \pmod 9$, which is $(-1) + (-1) + 0$, so the mod 9 test does not rule it out. Always compute the residue before
    claiming an obstruction.

??? question "10. Explain why Theorem 9.2 implies that $x^n + y^n = z^n$ has no positive solutions when $4 \mid n$, and why that reduces Fermat's last theorem to odd prime exponents."

    If $n = 4k$, a solution gives $(x^k)^4 + (y^k)^4 = (z^{2k})^2$, contradicting Theorem 9.2. Any $n \ge 3$ not
    divisible by 4 has an odd prime factor $p$, and a solution for $n = pk$ gives the solution $(x^k, y^k, z^k)$ for
    exponent $p$. So it suffices to treat $n = 4$ and odd primes.

??? question "11. Show that $2^{15} - 7$ is a square, and explain why $2^n - 7$ cannot be a square for even $n \ge 4$ other than $n = 4$."

    $2^{15} - 7 = 32\,761 = 181^2$. For $n = 2k$: $2^{2k} - x^2 = 7$ gives $(2^k - x)(2^k + x) = 7$, so $2^k - x = 1$
    and $2^k + x = 7$, hence $2^{k+1} = 8$, $k = 2$, $n = 4$ ($16 - 7 = 9$).

??? question "12. The solutions of $x^2 - 2y^2 = 1$ satisfy $(x, y) \mapsto (3x + 4y, 2x + 3y)$. Prove that this map preserves the equation, and compute the solution after $(577, 408)$."

    It is multiplication by $3 + 2\sqrt 2$, which has norm 1. Directly: $(3x + 4y)^2 - 2(2x + 3y)^2 = 9x^2 + 24xy +
    16y^2 - 8x^2 - 24xy - 18y^2 = x^2 - 2y^2$. From $(577, 408)$: $(3 \cdot 577 + 4 \cdot 408, 2 \cdot 577 + 3 \cdot
    408) = (3363, 2378)$.

## What you learned

- Primitive Pythagorean triples are exactly $(m^2 - n^2, 2mn, m^2 + n^2)$ with $m > n$ coprime of opposite parity;
  geometrically, lines of rational slope through $(-1, 0)$ find every rational point on the circle.
- Infinite descent proves impossibility by turning any solution into a smaller one; it shows $x^4 + y^4 = z^2$ has no
  positive solutions, so Fermat's last theorem holds for $n = 4$.
- The solutions of Pell's equation $x^2 - dy^2 = 1$ are the units of norm 1 in $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt d]$, a group
  $\pm\varepsilon^k$ generated by the fundamental solution; Lagrange proved one always exists.
- Congruences often prove that an equation has no solutions, but passing every congruence test is not enough.
- Markov's equation has a tree of solutions found by Vieta jumping; Catalan's equation was settled in 2002; sums of three
  cubes still produce surprises.
- Hilbert's tenth problem has a negative answer: Diophantine sets are exactly the listable sets, so no algorithm
  decides all Diophantine equations. Over the rationals the question is open.
