# Glossary

*Short definitions of the terms this book uses, from A to Z, each with a link to the chapter that teaches it.*

## A

**Abel-Ruffini theorem**
There is no formula in radicals for the roots of the general polynomial of degree 5 or more. Ruffini gave an incomplete proof in 1799 and Abel a complete one in 1824; Galois theory explains why.
See [Chapter 20](20-Galois-Theory.md#201-the-question).

**Abelian group**
A group in which $ab = ba$ for all elements $a$ and $b$, named after Niels Henrik Abel. Abelian groups are also called commutative, and are often written with $+$.
See [Chapter 13](13-Groups.md#132-the-axioms).

**Abelianization**
The quotient $G/[G, G]$ of a group by the subgroup generated by its commutators: the largest abelian quotient of $G$. It is an example of a functor from groups to abelian groups.
See [Chapter 22](22-Where-Algebra-Goes.md#221-categories-and-functors).

**Absolute value**
The size of a number without its sign: $|a| = a$ if $a \ge 0$ and $|a| = -a$ if $a < 0$. The number $|a - b|$ is the distance between $a$ and $b$ on the number line.
See [Chapter 2](02-Equations-and-Inequalities.md#26-absolute-value).

**Action (of a group)**
A rule that gives, for each $g$ in a group $G$ and each $x$ in a set $X$, a point $g \cdot x$ of $X$, such that the identity moves nothing and $(gh) \cdot x = g \cdot (h \cdot x)$. The set $X$ is then called a $G$-set.
See [Chapter 16](16-Group-Actions-and-Sylow.md#161-actions-orbits-stabilizers).

**Affine variety**
The set of common zeros in $k^n$ of a set of polynomials in $k[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$. Circles, parabolas and the twisted cubic are examples.
See [Chapter 22](22-Where-Algebra-Goes.md#222-algebraic-geometry-equations-as-shapes).

**Algebraic closure**
An algebraic extension of a field that is algebraically closed. Every field has one, unique up to isomorphism; the algebraic closure of $\mathbb{R}$ is $\mathbb{C}$, and that of $\mathbb{Q}$ is the field of algebraic numbers.
See [Chapter 19](19-Fields-and-Extensions.md#194-splitting-fields).

**Algebraic element**
An element $\alpha$ of an extension $K$ of $F$ is algebraic over $F$ if $f(\alpha) = 0$ for some nonzero polynomial $f$ with coefficients in $F$. An extension is algebraic if all its elements are.
See [Chapter 19](19-Fields-and-Extensions.md#192-algebraic-elements-and-minimal-polynomials).

**Algebraic integer**
A complex number that is a root of a monic polynomial with integer coefficients, such as $\sqrt2$ or $i$. The algebraic integers in a number field form its ring of integers.
See [Chapter 22](22-Where-Algebra-Goes.md#223-algebraic-number-theory-and-class-groups).

**Algebraic number**
A complex number that is a root of a nonzero polynomial with rational coefficients. The algebraic numbers form an algebraically closed field, $\overline{\mathbb{Q}}$.
See [Chapter 12](12-The-Fundamental-Theorem-of-Algebra.md#121-the-statement).

**Algebraically closed**
A field in which every nonconstant polynomial has a root, and so splits into linear factors. The fundamental theorem of algebra says that $\mathbb{C}$ is algebraically closed.
See [Chapter 12](12-The-Fundamental-Theorem-of-Algebra.md#121-the-statement).

**Alternating group**
The group $A_n$ of even permutations of $\{1, \ldots, n\}$. It has $n!/2$ elements for $n \ge 2$, and it is simple for $n \ge 5$.
See [Chapter 13](13-Groups.md#136-dihedral-and-symmetric-groups).

**Analytic geometry**
The study of shapes through equations in coordinates, so that geometric questions become algebra and equations can be seen as pictures. It was found independently by Descartes and Fermat.
See [Chapter 3](03-Lines-and-Linear-Systems.md#31-coordinates).

**Argument (of a complex number)**
An angle $\theta$ with $z = r(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta)$, written $\arg z$. It is fixed only up to multiples of $2\pi$; the principal argument is the one in $(-\pi, \pi]$.
See [Chapter 9](09-Complex-Numbers.md#94-polar-form-multiplication-is-rotation).

**Argument principle**
A theorem of complex analysis: if no root of $p$ lies on a circle, the number of times $p$ winds the circle around 0 equals the number of roots inside it.
See [Chapter 12](12-The-Fundamental-Theorem-of-Algebra.md#124-the-winding-number-picture).

**Arithmetic mean**
The average $\frac{x + y}{2}$ of two numbers. For positive numbers it is never less than the geometric mean $\sqrt{xy}$ (the AM-GM inequality).
See [Chapter 5](05-Quadratics.md#55-vertex-form-and-maximum-and-minimum-problems).

**Arithmetic sequence**
A sequence that adds the same number $d$, the common difference, at each step: $a_n = a_1 + (n - 1)d$. Its sum is the number of terms times the average of the first and last.
See [Chapter 8](08-Sequences-Series-and-Induction.md#81-arithmetic-and-geometric-sequences).

**Associates**
Two elements $a$ and $b$ of an integral domain with $a = ub$ for some unit $u$, such as $3$ and $-3$ in $\mathbb{Z}$. Associates divide each other and count as the same factor.
See [Chapter 18](18-Factorization-in-Rings.md#181-euclidean-domains).

**Associative law**
The law $(ab)c = a(bc)$ (and the same for addition): the grouping of a product does not matter. It is one of the group axioms.
See [Chapter 1](01-The-Language-of-Algebra.md#13-the-laws-of-arithmetic).

**Augmented matrix**
The table of numbers of a linear system, one row per equation, with the coefficients followed by the right-hand side. Gaussian elimination works on its rows.
See [Chapter 3](03-Lines-and-Linear-Systems.md#36-three-unknowns).

**Automorphism**
An isomorphism from a structure to itself. An automorphism of a field $K$ is a bijective ring homomorphism $K \to K$; an $F$-automorphism also fixes every element of $F$.
See [Chapter 15](15-Homomorphisms-and-Quotients.md#153-isomorphism) and [Chapter 20](20-Galois-Theory.md#202-automorphisms-and-the-galois-group).

**Axis of symmetry**
The vertical line through the vertex of a parabola $y = ax^2 + bx + c$, namely $x = -\frac{b}{2a}$. The parabola is its own mirror image in it.
See [Chapter 5](05-Quadratics.md#51-the-parabola).

## B

**Base (of a power)**
In $x^n$, the number $x$ that is multiplied by itself; $n$ is the exponent. For exponential functions $a^x$ and logarithms $\log_a$, the base $a$ must be positive and not 1.
See [Chapter 1](01-The-Language-of-Algebra.md#12-expressions-terms-and-coefficients).

**Base case**
The first step of a proof by induction: showing that the statement $P(n)$ holds for the starting value of $n$.
See [Chapter 8](08-Sequences-Series-and-Induction.md#83-proof-by-induction).

**Basis (of a module)**
A list of elements of a module that generates it and is independent. A module with a basis is free, and then $M \cong R^n$.
See [Chapter 21](21-Modules-and-Representations.md#211-modules-over-a-ring).

**Bézout's theorem**
Two plane curves of degrees $d$ and $e$ with no common component meet in at most $de$ points, and in exactly $de$ when counted correctly over $\mathbb{C}$ with points at infinity.
See [Chapter 22](22-Where-Algebra-Goes.md#222-algebraic-geometry-equations-as-shapes).

**Bijection**
A function that is both one-to-one and onto. A bijection pairs off the elements of two sets exactly, and it is precisely a function that has an inverse.
See [Chapter 6](06-Functions.md#65-one-to-one-onto-and-inverse-functions).

**Binary operation**
A rule that takes two elements $a$ and $b$ of a set $G$, in order, and gives an element of $G$. Addition and multiplication of numbers, and composition of symmetries, are examples.
See [Chapter 13](13-Groups.md#132-the-axioms).

**Binet's formula**
The closed formula $F_n = \frac{\varphi^n - \psi^n}{\sqrt5}$ for the Fibonacci numbers, where $\varphi$ and $\psi$ are the roots of $r^2 = r + 1$.
See [Chapter 8](08-Sequences-Series-and-Induction.md#85-recurrences-and-binets-formula).

**Binomial**
A polynomial with two terms, such as $x + 1$ or $3x^2 - 5$.
See [Chapter 4](04-Polynomials-and-Factoring.md#41-polynomials).

**Binomial coefficient**
The number $\binom{n}{k} = \frac{n!}{k!\,(n - k)!}$, read "$n$ choose $k$": the number of ways to choose $k$ things from $n$, and the coefficient of $a^{n-k}b^k$ in $(a + b)^n$.
See [Chapter 8](08-Sequences-Series-and-Induction.md#84-the-binomial-theorem-and-pascals-triangle).

**Binomial theorem**
The expansion $(a + b)^n = \sum_{k=0}^{n} \binom{n}{k} a^{n-k} b^k$ for every whole number $n$, proved by induction using Pascal's rule.
See [Chapter 8](08-Sequences-Series-and-Induction.md#84-the-binomial-theorem-and-pascals-triangle).

**Buchberger's algorithm**
The algorithm that computes a Gröbner basis: form S-polynomials, reduce them by the current basis, add any nonzero remainders, and repeat until every S-polynomial reduces to 0 (Buchberger's criterion).
See [Chapter 22](22-Where-Algebra-Goes.md#226-computer-algebra-and-grobner-bases).

**Burnside's lemma**
If a finite group $G$ acts on a finite set $X$, the number of orbits is the average number of points fixed by an element of $G$. It counts objects "up to symmetry", such as necklaces and coloured cubes.
See [Chapter 16](16-Group-Actions-and-Sylow.md#163-burnside-counting).

## C

**Cancellation law**
In a group, $ab = ac$ implies $b = c$, and $ba = ca$ implies $b = c$. In an integral domain the same holds for multiplication by a nonzero element.
See [Chapter 13](13-Groups.md#132-the-axioms).

**Cardano's formula**
The formula that solves the depressed cubic $t^3 + pt + q = 0$ as a sum of two cube roots, $t = \sqrt[3]{-\frac q2 + \sqrt{D}} + \sqrt[3]{-\frac q2 - \sqrt{D}}$ with $D = \frac{q^2}{4} + \frac{p^3}{27}$. It was published by Cardano in 1545.
See [Chapter 11](11-Cubics-and-Quartics.md#113-cardanos-formula).

**Cartesian product**
The set $A \times B$ of all ordered pairs $(a, b)$ with $a \in A$ and $b \in B$. A function from $A$ to $B$ can be defined as a subset of it.
See [Chapter 6](06-Functions.md#67-functions-as-sets-of-pairs-the-formal-definition).

**Category**
A collection of objects and, for each pair of objects, a set of morphisms (arrows) between them, with an associative composition and identity morphisms. Sets, groups, rings and vector spaces each form a category.
See [Chapter 22](22-Where-Algebra-Goes.md#221-categories-and-functors).

**Cauchy's theorem**
If a prime $p$ divides the order of a finite group $G$, then $G$ has an element of order $p$.
See [Chapter 16](16-Group-Actions-and-Sylow.md#165-cauchys-theorem).

**Cayley table**
The full multiplication table of a small finite group: the entry in row $a$, column $b$ is $ab$. Each element appears exactly once in every row and every column.
See [Chapter 13](13-Groups.md#134-group-tables-and-order).

**Cayley's theorem**
Every group is isomorphic to a group of permutations: $G$ acts faithfully on itself by left multiplication, so it sits inside the symmetric group on its own elements.
See [Chapter 16](16-Group-Actions-and-Sylow.md#161-actions-orbits-stabilizers).

**Centralizer**
The subgroup $C_G(x) = \{g : gx = xg\}$ of elements that commute with $x$. It is the stabilizer of $x$ under conjugation.
See [Chapter 16](16-Group-Actions-and-Sylow.md#161-actions-orbits-stabilizers).

**Centre (of a group)**
The subgroup $Z(G)$ of elements that commute with every element of $G$. It is always normal, and it is all of $G$ exactly when $G$ is abelian.
See [Chapter 15](15-Homomorphisms-and-Quotients.md#154-normal-subgroups).

**Character**
The function $\chi_\rho(g) = \operatorname{tr} \rho(g)$ that sends each group element to the trace of its matrix in a representation $\rho$. Characters are class functions, and they determine a complex representation up to isomorphism.
See [Chapter 21](21-Modules-and-Representations.md#215-characters-and-orthogonality).

**Characteristic (of a ring)**
The smallest positive integer $n$ with $n \cdot 1 = 0$, or 0 if there is none. The characteristic of an integral domain is 0 or a prime.
See [Chapter 17](17-Rings-and-Ideals.md#176-characteristic).

**Characteristic equation**
The polynomial equation obtained by trying $a_n = r^n$ in a linear recurrence with constant coefficients; for Fibonacci it is $r^2 = r + 1$. Its roots give the general solution.
See [Chapter 8](08-Sequences-Series-and-Induction.md#85-recurrences-and-binets-formula).

**Characteristic polynomial**
For a linear map $T$, the polynomial $\det(xI - T)$. In the module view it is the product of the invariant factors of $V$ as an $F[x]$-module.
See [Chapter 21](21-Modules-and-Representations.md#212-modules-over-a-pid-the-structure-theorem).

**Chinese remainder theorem**
If $\gcd(m, n) = 1$, each pair of congruences $x \equiv a \pmod m$, $x \equiv b \pmod n$ has exactly one solution mod $mn$; equivalently $\mathbb{Z}_{mn} \cong \mathbb{Z}_m \times \mathbb{Z}_n$. For rings, coprime ideals $I$ and $J$ give $R/IJ \cong R/I \times R/J$.
See [Chapter 15](15-Homomorphisms-and-Quotients.md#157-direct-products-and-the-chinese-remainder-theorem) and [Chapter 17](17-Rings-and-Ideals.md#174-ring-homomorphisms-and-the-isomorphism-theorem).

**Class equation**
For a finite group, $|G| = |Z(G)| + \sum [G : C_G(x_i)]$, the sum running over one element from each conjugacy class of size greater than 1. It shows that every group of prime-power order has a nontrivial centre.
See [Chapter 16](16-Group-Actions-and-Sylow.md#164-the-class-equation-and-p-groups-have-a-centre).

**Class field theory**
The theory, built by Takagi and Artin in the 1920s, that describes how primes factor in a number field with abelian Galois group, through congruence conditions. It generalizes quadratic reciprocity.
See [Chapter 22](22-Where-Algebra-Goes.md#223-algebraic-number-theory-and-class-groups).

**Class function**
A function on a group that is constant on each conjugacy class, so $\chi(hgh^{-1}) = \chi(g)$. Characters are class functions.
See [Chapter 21](21-Modules-and-Representations.md#215-characters-and-orthogonality).

**Class number**
The size of the ideal class group of a ring of integers. It measures how far unique factorization of elements fails, and it is 1 exactly when the ring is a PID.
See [Chapter 18](18-Factorization-in-Rings.md#187-kummer-ideals-and-fermats-last-theorem).

**Classification of finite simple groups**
The theorem that every finite simple group is cyclic of prime order, an alternating group $A_n$ with $n \ge 5$, a group of Lie type, or one of 26 sporadic groups. Its proof fills tens of thousands of pages.
See [Chapter 16](16-Group-Actions-and-Sylow.md#168-simple-groups-and-the-classification).

**Codomain**
A set named as the place where a function's outputs live, as in $f : A \to B$. The range, the outputs actually produced, may be smaller.
See [Chapter 6](06-Functions.md#61-what-a-function-is).

**Coefficient**
The number factor of a term: in $3x^2$ it is $3$, in $-5xy$ it is $-5$, and in $x$ alone it is $1$.
See [Chapter 1](01-The-Language-of-Algebra.md#12-expressions-terms-and-coefficients).

**Commutative law**
The law $ab = ba$ (and $a + b = b + a$): the order of the two numbers does not matter. Matrix multiplication and the symmetries of a square do not obey it.
See [Chapter 1](01-The-Language-of-Algebra.md#13-the-laws-of-arithmetic).

**Commutative ring**
A ring in which $ab = ba$ for all $a$ and $b$, such as $\mathbb{Z}$, $\mathbb{Z}_n$ and $F[x]$. Square matrices form a ring that is not commutative.
See [Chapter 17](17-Rings-and-Ideals.md#171-rings-examples).

**Commutator**
The element $hkh^{-1}k^{-1}$ of a group; it equals $e$ exactly when $h$ and $k$ commute. The subgroup the commutators generate, $[G, G]$, measures how far $G$ is from abelian.
See [Chapter 15](15-Homomorphisms-and-Quotients.md#157-direct-products-and-the-chinese-remainder-theorem).

**Companion matrix**
A matrix whose characteristic polynomial is a given monic polynomial, so that its eigenvalues are exactly the polynomial's roots. Numerical software finds polynomial roots this way, and companion matrices are the blocks of the rational canonical form.
See [Chapter 12](12-The-Fundamental-Theorem-of-Algebra.md#126-numerical-root-finding-and-what-solving-means).

**Completing the square**
Rewriting $x^2 + bx$ as $\left(x + \frac b2\right)^2 - \frac{b^2}{4}$, turning a quadratic into a perfect square plus a constant. It proves the quadratic formula and gives vertex form.
See [Chapter 5](05-Quadratics.md#52-completing-the-square).

**Complex number**
An expression $a + bi$ with $a$ and $b$ real and $i^2 = -1$. The set of complex numbers, $\mathbb{C}$, is a field that contains the real numbers.
See [Chapter 9](09-Complex-Numbers.md#92-arithmetic-with-complex-numbers).

**Complex plane**
The plane in which the complex number $a + bi$ is the point $(a, b)$, also called the Argand diagram. The horizontal axis holds the real numbers and the vertical axis the purely imaginary ones.
See [Chapter 9](09-Complex-Numbers.md#93-the-complex-plane-the-conjugate-and-the-modulus).

**Composition (of functions)**
Doing one function and then feeding its output into another: $(g \circ f)(x) = g(f(x))$, "first $f$, then $g$". Composition is associative but usually not commutative.
See [Chapter 6](06-Functions.md#64-composition-one-function-after-another).

**Composition series**
A chain of subgroups $\{e\} = G_0 \trianglelefteq G_1 \trianglelefteq \cdots \trianglelefteq G_n = G$ in which each quotient $G_i/G_{i-1}$ is simple. These quotients are the composition factors.
See [Chapter 16](16-Group-Actions-and-Sylow.md#168-simple-groups-and-the-classification).

**Conjugacy class**
The set $\{gxg^{-1} : g \in G\}$ of all conjugates of $x$: the orbit of $x$ when $G$ acts on itself by conjugation.
See [Chapter 16](16-Group-Actions-and-Sylow.md#161-actions-orbits-stabilizers).

**Conjugate (complex)**
The conjugate of $z = a + bi$ is $\bar z = a - bi$, its mirror image in the real axis. The product $z\bar z = a^2 + b^2$ is real, which is how complex division works.
See [Chapter 9](09-Complex-Numbers.md#93-the-complex-plane-the-conjugate-and-the-modulus).

**Conjugate (in a group)**
The element $gkg^{-1}$ is the conjugate of $k$ by $g$; it is $k$ seen from another point of view. Conjugate elements of $S_n$ are exactly those with the same cycle shape.
See [Chapter 15](15-Homomorphisms-and-Quotients.md#154-normal-subgroups).

**Constant term**
A term with no letters, such as $7$ in $3x^2 - 5xy + 7$. In a polynomial $a_nx^n + \cdots + a_0$ it is $a_0$.
See [Chapter 1](01-The-Language-of-Algebra.md#12-expressions-terms-and-coefficients).

**Constructible number**
A real number that is a coordinate of a point reachable from $(0, 0)$ and $(1, 0)$ with straightedge and compass. Every constructible number has degree a power of 2 over $\mathbb{Q}$.
See [Chapter 19](19-Fields-and-Extensions.md#193-constructible-numbers-and-three-impossible-problems).

**Content (of a polynomial)**
The greatest common divisor of the coefficients of a nonzero polynomial in $\mathbb{Z}[x]$. A polynomial with content 1 is primitive.
See [Chapter 18](18-Factorization-in-Rings.md#185-gausss-lemma-and-eisensteins-criterion).

**Converge**
A series converges to $S$ if its partial sums approach $S$; a sequence converges if it has a limit. A series that does not converge diverges.
See [Chapter 8](08-Sequences-Series-and-Induction.md#86-infinite-geometric-series-and-a-first-look-at-convergence).

**Coprime ideals**
Two ideals $I$ and $J$ of a ring with $I + J = R$, that is, $a + b = 1$ for some $a \in I$ and $b \in J$. They are the setting of the Chinese remainder theorem for rings.
See [Chapter 17](17-Rings-and-Ideals.md#174-ring-homomorphisms-and-the-isomorphism-theorem).

**Correspondence theorem**
The subgroups of $G/N$ are exactly the $H/N$ for subgroups $H$ with $N \le H \le G$, matched in an order-preserving way; the same holds for ideals of a quotient ring.
See [Chapter 15](15-Homomorphisms-and-Quotients.md#156-the-isomorphism-theorems).

**Coset**
For a subgroup $H$ of $G$ and $g \in G$, the left coset $gH = \{gh : h \in H\}$ or the right coset $Hg$. The left cosets cut $G$ into disjoint pieces, all the same size as $H$.
See [Chapter 14](14-Subgroups-Cosets-and-Lagrange.md#143-cosets).

**Cramer's rule**
The formula for the solution of a linear system as a ratio of determinants: for two unknowns, $x = D_x / D$ and $y = D_y / D$ when $D \neq 0$.
See [Chapter 3](03-Lines-and-Linear-Systems.md#34-two-equations-two-unknowns).

**Cubic**
A polynomial of degree 3. Every real cubic has at least one real root, and its roots can be found by Cardano's formula.
See [Chapter 11](11-Cubics-and-Quartics.md#112-depressing-the-cubic).

**Cycle (permutation)**
A permutation that moves $a_1 \to a_2 \to \cdots \to a_k \to a_1$ and fixes everything else, written $(a_1\,a_2\,\ldots\,a_k)$: a $k$-cycle. Every permutation is a product of disjoint cycles.
See [Chapter 13](13-Groups.md#136-dihedral-and-symmetric-groups).

**Cyclic group**
A group generated by one element $g$: every element is a power $g^k$. Up to isomorphism the cyclic groups are $\mathbb{Z}$ and the $\mathbb{Z}_n$.
See [Chapter 13](13-Groups.md#135-cyclic-groups).

**Cyclic subgroup**
The subgroup $\langle g \rangle = \{g^k : k \in \mathbb{Z}\}$ of all powers of one element. Its order equals the order of $g$.
See [Chapter 13](13-Groups.md#135-cyclic-groups).

**Cyclotomic polynomial**
For a prime $p$, the polynomial $\Phi_p(x) = x^{p-1} + x^{p-2} + \cdots + x + 1$, whose roots are the primitive $p$-th roots of unity. It is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$, by Eisenstein after the shift $x \mapsto x + 1$.
See [Chapter 18](18-Factorization-in-Rings.md#185-gausss-lemma-and-eisensteins-criterion).

## D

**De Moivre's theorem**
The formula $(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta)^n = \cos n\theta + i\sin n\theta$: to raise a complex number to a power, raise its modulus to the power and multiply its argument by $n$.
See [Chapter 9](09-Complex-Numbers.md#95-de-moivres-theorem).

**Dedekind domain**
An integral domain in which every nonzero ideal factors uniquely as a product of prime ideals. The ring of integers of every number field is one, which rescues unique factorization at the level of ideals.
See [Chapter 22](22-Where-Algebra-Goes.md#223-algebraic-number-theory-and-class-groups).

**Degree (of a field extension)**
The dimension $[K : F]$ of $K$ as a vector space over $F$. For example $[\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2) : \mathbb{Q}] = 2$ and $[\mathbb{C} : \mathbb{R}] = 2$.
See [Chapter 19](19-Fields-and-Extensions.md#191-field-extensions-degree-and-the-tower-law).

**Degree (of a polynomial)**
The highest power $n$ with a nonzero coefficient $a_n$. The zero polynomial has no degree, and over an integral domain the degree of a product is the sum of the degrees.
See [Chapter 4](04-Polynomials-and-Factoring.md#41-polynomials).

**Degree (of a term)**
The total number of letter factors in a term: $3x^2$ and $-5xy$ both have degree 2.
See [Chapter 1](01-The-Language-of-Algebra.md#12-expressions-terms-and-coefficients).

**Dependent system**
A linear system with infinitely many solutions, because one equation adds nothing to the others; for two unknowns the two lines are the same line.
See [Chapter 3](03-Lines-and-Linear-Systems.md#35-when-there-is-no-solution-or-infinitely-many).

**Depressed cubic**
A cubic with no square term, $t^3 + pt + q$. Every cubic $x^3 + bx^2 + cx + d$ becomes one by the substitution $x = t - \frac b3$.
See [Chapter 11](11-Cubics-and-Quartics.md#112-depressing-the-cubic).

**Descartes' rule of signs**
The number of positive roots of a real polynomial equals the number of sign changes in its coefficients, or is less than it by an even number.
See [Chapter 10](10-Roots-and-Coefficients.md#107-where-the-roots-are-bounds-and-signs).

**Determinant (of a 2 by 2 system)**
The number $D = ad - bc$ for the system $ax + by = e$, $cx + dy = f$. The system has exactly one solution when $D \neq 0$.
See [Chapter 3](03-Lines-and-Linear-Systems.md#34-two-equations-two-unknowns).

**Difference of two squares**
The identity $a^2 - b^2 = (a - b)(a + b)$, one of the most used factorizations.
See [Chapter 4](04-Polynomials-and-Factoring.md#43-special-products-and-identities).

**Dihedral group**
The group $D_n$ of the $2n$ symmetries of a regular $n$-gon: $n$ rotations and $n$ reflections. $D_4$, the symmetries of a square, is the book's first group.
See [Chapter 13](13-Groups.md#136-dihedral-and-symmetric-groups).

**Direct product**
The group $G \times H$ of pairs $(g, h)$, multiplied one coordinate at a time. The order of $(g, h)$ is the least common multiple of the orders of $g$ and $h$.
See [Chapter 15](15-Homomorphisms-and-Quotients.md#157-direct-products-and-the-chinese-remainder-theorem).

**Directrix**
The fixed line $L$ in the definition of a parabola as the set of points equally far from a point $F$ (the focus) and from $L$.
See [Chapter 5](05-Quadratics.md#51-the-parabola).

**Discriminant (of a polynomial)**
For a monic polynomial with roots $r_1, \ldots, r_n$, the product $\prod_{i<j} (r_i - r_j)^2$. It is zero exactly when there is a repeated root, and it is a polynomial in the coefficients.
See [Chapter 11](11-Cubics-and-Quartics.md#117-the-discriminant).

**Discriminant (of a quadratic)**
The number $\Delta = b^2 - 4ac$. The quadratic $ax^2 + bx + c$ has two real roots if $\Delta > 0$, one double root if $\Delta = 0$, and none if $\Delta < 0$.
See [Chapter 5](05-Quadratics.md#54-the-discriminant).

**Distributive law**
The law $a(b + c) = ab + ac$, the one law that links addition and multiplication. It is behind expanding brackets and factoring.
See [Chapter 1](01-The-Language-of-Algebra.md#13-the-laws-of-arithmetic).

**Diverge**
A series diverges if its partial sums do not approach a number. The harmonic series diverges even though its terms go to 0.
See [Chapter 8](08-Sequences-Series-and-Induction.md#86-infinite-geometric-series-and-a-first-look-at-convergence).

**Division algorithm (for polynomials)**
For polynomials $f$ and $g \neq 0$ over a field there are unique $q$ and $r$ with $f = qg + r$ and $r = 0$ or $\deg r < \deg g$.
See [Chapter 4](04-Polynomials-and-Factoring.md#45-long-division-and-synthetic-division).

**Division ring**
A ring in which every nonzero element has a multiplicative inverse, but multiplication need not commute. Hamilton's quaternions are the classic example.
See [Chapter 17](17-Rings-and-Ideals.md#171-rings-examples).

**Domain (of a function)**
The set of inputs a function accepts. The natural domain of a formula is the set of all inputs for which it makes sense.
See [Chapter 6](06-Functions.md#61-what-a-function-is).

**Double root**
A root that occurs twice, as $x = -\frac{b}{2a}$ does when the discriminant of a quadratic is 0: the factor appears squared, and the graph touches the axis there.
See [Chapter 5](05-Quadratics.md#54-the-discriminant).

## E

**$e$ (the number)**
The number $e \approx 2.71828$, the limit of $(1 + 1/m)^m$ as $m$ grows: the result of continuous compounding at 100% for one unit of time. It is the base of the natural logarithm.
See [Chapter 7](07-Exponents-and-Logarithms.md#77-the-number-e).

**Eisenstein's criterion**
If a prime $p$ divides every coefficient of $f \in \mathbb{Z}[x]$ except the leading one, and $p^2$ does not divide the constant term, then $f$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$.
See [Chapter 18](18-Factorization-in-Rings.md#185-gausss-lemma-and-eisensteins-criterion).

**Elementary divisors**
The prime powers in the decomposition of a finite abelian group (or a module over a PID) into cyclic pieces of prime-power order. The list is unique up to order.
See [Chapter 15](15-Homomorphisms-and-Quotients.md#158-finite-abelian-groups-classified).

**Elementary row operations**
The three moves of elimination: swap two equations, multiply one by a nonzero number, and add a multiple of one to another. Each gives an equivalent system.
See [Chapter 3](03-Lines-and-Linear-Systems.md#34-two-equations-two-unknowns).

**Elementary symmetric polynomials**
The polynomials $e_1 = \sum x_i$, $e_2 = \sum_{i<j} x_ix_j$, ..., $e_n = x_1 \cdots x_n$. By Vieta's formulas they are, up to sign, the coefficients of the monic polynomial with roots $x_1, \ldots, x_n$.
See [Chapter 10](10-Roots-and-Coefficients.md#104-vietas-formulas).

**Equation**
A statement that two expressions are equal, meant to be true for particular values of its unknowns. To solve it is to find its solution set.
See [Chapter 1](01-The-Language-of-Algebra.md#12-expressions-terms-and-coefficients).

**Equivalence relation**
A relation that is reflexive, symmetric and transitive. Its classes form a partition; the left cosets of a subgroup are the classes of $a \sim b \iff a^{-1}b \in H$.
See [Chapter 14](14-Subgroups-Cosets-and-Lagrange.md#143-cosets).

**Equivalent equations**
Equations with exactly the same solution set. The safe moves of algebra turn an equation into an equivalent one.
See [Chapter 2](02-Equations-and-Inequalities.md#21-the-balance-idea).

**Euclidean domain**
An integral domain with a size function $N$ that allows division with a smaller remainder: for $a$ and $b \ne 0$ there are $q, r$ with $a = qb + r$ and $r = 0$ or $N(r) < N(b)$. $\mathbb{Z}$, $F[x]$ and the Gaussian integers are examples.
See [Chapter 18](18-Factorization-in-Rings.md#181-euclidean-domains).

**Euclid's lemma**
If a prime $p$ divides $ab$, then $p$ divides $a$ or $p$ divides $b$. It is the key step in proving unique factorization.
See [Chapter 18](18-Factorization-in-Rings.md#181-euclidean-domains).

**Euler's formula**
The identity $e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta$. With $\theta = \pi$ it gives $e^{i\pi} + 1 = 0$.
See [Chapter 9](09-Complex-Numbers.md#97-eulers-formula).

**Euler's phi function**
The number $\varphi(n)$ of integers from 1 to $n$ that are coprime to $n$; it is the order of the group $U(n)$. Euler's theorem says $a^{\varphi(n)} \equiv 1 \pmod n$ when $\gcd(a, n) = 1$.
See [Chapter 14](14-Subgroups-Cosets-and-Lagrange.md#144-lagranges-theorem).

**Evaluate**
To work out which number an expression names once the values of its letters are known.
See [Chapter 1](01-The-Language-of-Algebra.md#12-expressions-terms-and-coefficients).

**Evaluation homomorphism**
The ring homomorphism $F[x] \to K$, $f \mapsto f(\alpha)$. Its kernel is generated by the minimal polynomial of $\alpha$ when $\alpha$ is algebraic.
See [Chapter 19](19-Fields-and-Extensions.md#192-algebraic-elements-and-minimal-polynomials).

**Even and odd functions**
A function is even if $f(-x) = f(x)$ (graph symmetric in the $y$-axis) and odd if $f(-x) = -f(x)$ (graph symmetric under a half-turn about the origin).
See [Chapter 6](06-Functions.md#66-even-odd-periodic-and-piecewise-functions).

**Even and odd permutations**
A permutation is even if it is a product of an even number of transpositions and odd otherwise. The parity does not depend on how the permutation is written.
See [Chapter 13](13-Groups.md#136-dihedral-and-symmetric-groups).

**Exponent**
In $x^n$, the number $n$ that says how many copies of the base are multiplied. Exponents extend to zero, negative, fractional and finally all real values.
See [Chapter 7](07-Exponents-and-Logarithms.md#71-exponents-whole-negative-fractional-real).

**Exponential function**
A function $f(x) = a^x$ with base $a > 0$, $a \neq 1$, or more generally $f(t) = Ca^t$. Equal steps in $t$ multiply $f$ by equal factors.
See [Chapter 7](07-Exponents-and-Logarithms.md#72-exponential-growth-and-decay).

**Expression**
A meaningful string of numbers, letters and operation signs, such as $3x^2 - 5xy + 7$. It names a number once its letters are given values.
See [Chapter 1](01-The-Language-of-Algebra.md#12-expressions-terms-and-coefficients).

**Extension (of fields)**
If $F \subseteq K$ are fields with the same operations, $K$ is an extension of $F$, written $K/F$. $K$ is then a vector space over $F$.
See [Chapter 19](19-Fields-and-Extensions.md#191-field-extensions-degree-and-the-tower-law).

**Extraneous solution**
A number produced by a solving step that is not a solution of the original equation. Multiplying by an expression that could be zero, or squaring both sides, can create one.
See [Chapter 2](02-Equations-and-Inequalities.md#27-a-first-proof-why-the-methods-are-safe).

## F

**Factor**
A piece that is multiplied: the term $-5xy$ has factors $-5$, $x$ and $y$. To factor a polynomial is to write it as a product of polynomials of lower degree.
See [Chapter 4](04-Polynomials-and-Factoring.md#44-factoring).

**Factor theorem**
A number $c$ is a root of a polynomial $f$ exactly when $x - c$ is a factor of $f$.
See [Chapter 10](10-Roots-and-Coefficients.md#101-the-remainder-and-factor-theorems).

**Faithful action**
An action in which only the identity moves nothing: no other element fixes every point.
See [Chapter 16](16-Group-Actions-and-Sylow.md#161-actions-orbits-stabilizers).

**Feasible region**
The set of points that satisfy all the constraints of a linear program. It is a convex polygon, and the best value of the objective is found at a corner.
See [Chapter 3](03-Lines-and-Linear-Systems.md#37-linear-inequalities-and-a-first-linear-program).

**Feit-Thompson theorem**
Every finite group of odd order is solvable (Feit and Thompson, 1963). Its proof took 255 pages.
See [Chapter 16](16-Group-Actions-and-Sylow.md#168-simple-groups-and-the-classification).

**Fermat's little theorem**
If $p$ is prime and $p$ does not divide $a$, then $a^{p-1} \equiv 1 \pmod p$. The book proves it from Lagrange's theorem.
See [Chapter 14](14-Subgroups-Cosets-and-Lagrange.md#144-lagranges-theorem).

**Ferrari's method**
The method, found by Lodovico Ferrari, that solves the quartic by adding a well-chosen square to both sides, which leads to a cubic (the resolvent cubic).
See [Chapter 11](11-Cubics-and-Quartics.md#116-ferraris-quartic-method).

**Field**
A commutative ring with $1 \neq 0$ in which every nonzero element has a multiplicative inverse. $\mathbb{Q}$, $\mathbb{R}$, $\mathbb{C}$ and $\mathbb{Z}_p$ for prime $p$ are fields.
See [Chapter 1](01-The-Language-of-Algebra.md#13-the-laws-of-arithmetic) and [Chapter 17](17-Rings-and-Ideals.md#172-integral-domains-and-fields).

**Finite extension**
A field extension $K/F$ of finite degree $[K : F]$. Every finite extension is algebraic.
See [Chapter 19](19-Fields-and-Extensions.md#191-field-extensions-degree-and-the-tower-law).

**Finite field**
A field with finitely many elements. There is one, $\mathbb{F}_{p^n}$, of each prime-power size and no others, and its group of nonzero elements is cyclic.
See [Chapter 19](19-Fields-and-Extensions.md#195-finite-fields).

**Finitely generated**
A module (or group) is finitely generated if some finite list of its elements generates it: every element is a combination $r_1m_1 + \cdots + r_nm_n$.
See [Chapter 21](21-Modules-and-Representations.md#211-modules-over-a-ring).

**First isomorphism theorem**
If $\varphi : G \to H$ is a homomorphism, then $G/\ker\varphi \cong \operatorname{im}\varphi$. The same holds for rings with ideals in place of normal subgroups.
See [Chapter 15](15-Homomorphisms-and-Quotients.md#156-the-isomorphism-theorems).

**Fixed field**
For a group $G$ of automorphisms of a field $K$, the subfield $K^G$ of elements fixed by every element of $G$.
See [Chapter 20](20-Galois-Theory.md#203-normal-and-separable-extensions).

**Focus**
The fixed point $F$ in the definition of a parabola as the set of points equally far from $F$ and a line (the directrix). Rays parallel to the axis reflect through it.
See [Chapter 5](05-Quadratics.md#51-the-parabola).

**Formula**
An equation that says how to compute one quantity from others, such as $F = \frac95 C + 32$.
See [Chapter 1](01-The-Language-of-Algebra.md#16-substitution-and-formulas).

**Free module**
A module that has a basis; it is then isomorphic to $R^n$. Vector spaces are always free, but $\mathbb{Z}_2$ as a $\mathbb{Z}$-module is not.
See [Chapter 21](21-Modules-and-Representations.md#211-modules-over-a-ring).

**Frobenius map**
In a ring of prime characteristic $p$, the map $a \mapsto a^p$. It is a ring homomorphism, because $(a + b)^p = a^p + b^p$ (the "freshman's dream"), and on a finite field it is an automorphism.
See [Chapter 17](17-Rings-and-Ideals.md#176-characteristic).

**Function**
A rule that takes an input and gives back exactly one output. Formally, a function from $A$ to $B$ is a subset of $A \times B$ containing exactly one pair $(a, b)$ for each $a \in A$.
See [Chapter 6](06-Functions.md#61-what-a-function-is).

**Functor**
A map between categories that sends objects to objects and morphisms to morphisms, preserving composition and identities. A contravariant functor reverses the arrows.
See [Chapter 22](22-Where-Algebra-Goes.md#221-categories-and-functors).

**Fundamental theorem of algebra**
Every nonconstant polynomial with complex coefficients has a complex root; so a polynomial of degree $n$ splits into $n$ linear factors over $\mathbb{C}$.
See [Chapter 12](12-The-Fundamental-Theorem-of-Algebra.md#121-the-statement).

**Fundamental theorem of finite abelian groups**
Every finite abelian group is a direct product of cyclic groups of prime-power order, in a way that is unique up to order.
See [Chapter 15](15-Homomorphisms-and-Quotients.md#158-finite-abelian-groups-classified).

**Fundamental theorem of Galois theory**
For a finite Galois extension $K/F$ with group $G$, subgroups of $G$ and fields between $F$ and $K$ match one to one, reversing inclusions, with $H \mapsto K^H$; degrees become indices, and normal subgroups match normal extensions.
See [Chapter 20](20-Galois-Theory.md#204-the-fundamental-theorem).

**Fundamental unit**
In the ring of integers of a real quadratic field, a unit from which all units are $\pm$ its powers, such as $1 + \sqrt2$ in $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt2]$. Finding it means solving Pell's equation.
See [Chapter 22](22-Where-Algebra-Goes.md#223-algebraic-number-theory-and-class-groups).

## G

**Galois extension**
A finite extension $K/F$ that is normal and separable; equivalently, the splitting field of a separable polynomial, or one whose automorphism group has order $[K : F]$, or one whose fixed field is exactly $F$.
See [Chapter 20](20-Galois-Theory.md#203-normal-and-separable-extensions).

**Galois group**
The group $\operatorname{Gal}(K/F)$ of $F$-automorphisms of a Galois extension $K/F$. The Galois group of a polynomial is that of its splitting field; it permutes the roots.
See [Chapter 20](20-Galois-Theory.md#202-automorphisms-and-the-galois-group).

**Galois representation**
A representation of the Galois group of a number field, usually on a vector space over a field such as the $\ell$-adic numbers. They are central to the proof of Fermat's last theorem and to the Langlands programme.
See [Chapter 21](21-Modules-and-Representations.md#216-uses-in-physics-and-chemistry).

**Gaussian elimination**
The method that solves a linear system by eliminating unknowns one at a time with elementary row operations, reaching triangular form, and then substituting back.
See [Chapter 3](03-Lines-and-Linear-Systems.md#36-three-unknowns).

**Gaussian integers**
The ring $\mathbb{Z}[i] = \{a + bi : a, b \in \mathbb{Z}\}$ with norm $N(a + bi) = a^2 + b^2$. It is a Euclidean domain, and its primes explain which numbers are sums of two squares.
See [Chapter 18](18-Factorization-in-Rings.md#184-gaussian-integers-and-sums-of-two-squares).

**Gauss's lemma**
The product of two primitive polynomials in $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ is primitive. Consequently a polynomial in $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ that factors over $\mathbb{Q}$ already factors over $\mathbb{Z}$.
See [Chapter 18](18-Factorization-in-Rings.md#185-gausss-lemma-and-eisensteins-criterion).

**General polynomial**
The polynomial $(x - t_1)(x - t_2) \cdots (x - t_n)$ with independent variables $t_i$ as roots, over the field of symmetric rational functions in them. Its Galois group is $S_n$, which is why it has no formula in radicals for $n \ge 5$.
See [Chapter 20](20-Galois-Theory.md#206-why-the-general-quintic-has-no-formula).

**Generator**
An element $g$ of a group with $\langle g \rangle$ the whole group. A generator of the cyclic group $U(n)$ is called a primitive root mod $n$; a generator of the multiplicative group of a finite field is called a primitive element.
See [Chapter 13](13-Groups.md#135-cyclic-groups).

**Geometric mean**
The number $\sqrt{xy}$ for positive $x$ and $y$. It is at most the arithmetic mean, with equality exactly when $x = y$.
See [Chapter 5](05-Quadratics.md#55-vertex-form-and-maximum-and-minimum-problems).

**Geometric sequence**
A sequence that multiplies by the same number $r$, the common ratio, at each step: $a_n = a_1r^{n-1}$. Its sum is $a_1\frac{1 - r^n}{1 - r}$ for $r \neq 1$.
See [Chapter 8](08-Sequences-Series-and-Induction.md#81-arithmetic-and-geometric-sequences).

**Geometric series**
The infinite sum $a + ar + ar^2 + \cdots$. It converges to $\frac{a}{1 - r}$ when $|r| < 1$ and diverges otherwise (for $a \neq 0$).
See [Chapter 8](08-Sequences-Series-and-Induction.md#86-infinite-geometric-series-and-a-first-look-at-convergence).

**Golden ratio**
The number $\varphi = \frac{1 + \sqrt5}{2} \approx 1.618$, the positive root of $r^2 = r + 1$. Ratios of consecutive Fibonacci numbers approach it.
See [Chapter 8](08-Sequences-Series-and-Induction.md#85-recurrences-and-binets-formula).

**Graph (of a function)**
The set of all points $(x, f(x))$ in the plane, for $x$ in the domain. A curve is the graph of a function of $x$ exactly when every vertical line meets it at most once.
See [Chapter 6](06-Functions.md#62-graphs-and-how-to-read-them).

**Gröbner basis**
A generating set of a polynomial ideal $I$ such that a polynomial lies in $I$ exactly when its remainder on division by the set is 0. Buchberger's algorithm computes one; it is the engine of computer algebra.
See [Chapter 22](22-Where-Algebra-Goes.md#226-computer-algebra-and-grobner-bases).

**Group**
A set with a binary operation that is associative, has an identity element, and gives every element an inverse. Symmetries, numbers under addition and invertible matrices are examples.
See [Chapter 13](13-Groups.md#132-the-axioms).

**Group algebra**
The ring $\mathbb{C}[G]$ with the elements of a group $G$ as a basis and multiplication coming from the group. Representations of $G$ are the same as $\mathbb{C}[G]$-modules.
See [Chapter 21](21-Modules-and-Representations.md#213-representations-of-finite-groups).

**Group of Lie type**
A member of one of 16 infinite families of finite simple matrix groups over finite fields, such as $PSL_n(q)$. Together with the cyclic, alternating and sporadic groups they make up the finite simple groups.
See [Chapter 16](16-Group-Actions-and-Sylow.md#168-simple-groups-and-the-classification).

## H

**Half-life**
The fixed time $T$ in which a decaying quantity halves; after $t$ units a fraction $\left(\frac12\right)^{t/T}$ is left.
See [Chapter 7](07-Exponents-and-Logarithms.md#72-exponential-growth-and-decay).

**Half-plane**
One of the two sides of a line in the plane, the solution set of a linear inequality such as $2x + 3y \le 12$.
See [Chapter 3](03-Lines-and-Linear-Systems.md#37-linear-inequalities-and-a-first-linear-program).

**Harmonic series**
The series $1 + \frac12 + \frac13 + \cdots$. Its terms go to 0 but it diverges, which shows that terms going to zero is not enough for convergence.
See [Chapter 8](08-Sequences-Series-and-Induction.md#86-infinite-geometric-series-and-a-first-look-at-convergence).

**Hilbert's basis theorem**
If $R$ is Noetherian, so is $R[x]$; in particular every ideal of $F[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$ is generated by finitely many polynomials.
See [Chapter 17](17-Rings-and-Ideals.md#177-polynomial-rings).

**Hilbert's Nullstellensatz**
Over an algebraically closed field $k$: a set of polynomials has no common zero exactly when 1 is in the ideal they generate (weak form), and $I(V(J)) = \sqrt{J}$ for every ideal $J$ (strong form). It ties ideals to varieties.
See [Chapter 22](22-Where-Algebra-Goes.md#222-algebraic-geometry-equations-as-shapes).

**Homomorphism (of groups)**
A function $\varphi : G \to H$ with $\varphi(ab) = \varphi(a)\varphi(b)$ for all $a, b$: a map that respects the operation. It sends the identity to the identity and inverses to inverses.
See [Chapter 15](15-Homomorphisms-and-Quotients.md#151-maps-that-respect-structure).

**Horner's form**
A polynomial rewritten as nested multiplications, such as $((2x - 3)x + 4)x - 5$. Evaluating it this way is the same work as synthetic division.
See [Chapter 4](04-Polynomials-and-Factoring.md#45-long-division-and-synthetic-division).

## I

**Ideal**
A subset $I$ of a commutative ring that is a subgroup under addition and absorbs multiplication: $a \in I$ and $r \in R$ give $ra \in I$. Ideals are exactly the kernels of ring homomorphisms.
See [Chapter 17](17-Rings-and-Ideals.md#173-ideals-and-quotient-rings).

**Ideal class group**
The group of nonzero ideals of a ring of integers modulo the principal ones. Its size, the class number, measures the failure of unique factorization of elements.
See [Chapter 18](18-Factorization-in-Rings.md#187-kummer-ideals-and-fermats-last-theorem).

**Idempotent**
A ring element $a$ with $a^2 = a$, such as 0, 1, and $3$ and $4$ in $\mathbb{Z}_6$.
See [Chapter 17](17-Rings-and-Ideals.md#172-integral-domains-and-fields).

**Identity**
An equation meant to hold for every value of its variables, such as $(a + b)^2 = a^2 + 2ab + b^2$. It is proved, not solved.
See [Chapter 1](01-The-Language-of-Algebra.md#12-expressions-terms-and-coefficients).

**Identity element**
An element $e$ with $ea = ae = a$ for every $a$: 0 for addition, 1 for multiplication, the "do nothing" symmetry for a group. In a group it is unique.
See [Chapter 13](13-Groups.md#132-the-axioms).

**Image**
The set $\operatorname{im}\varphi = \{\varphi(g) : g \in G\}$ of values of a homomorphism. It is a subgroup of the target.
See [Chapter 15](15-Homomorphisms-and-Quotients.md#152-kernel-and-image).

**Imaginary part**
The real number $b$ in $z = a + bi$, written $\operatorname{Im} z$. Note that it is $b$, not $bi$.
See [Chapter 9](09-Complex-Numbers.md#92-arithmetic-with-complex-numbers).

**Inconsistent system**
A linear system with no solution; for two unknowns the lines are parallel and distinct.
See [Chapter 3](03-Lines-and-Linear-Systems.md#35-when-there-is-no-solution-or-infinitely-many).

**Index (of a subgroup)**
The number of left cosets of $H$ in $G$, written $[G : H]$. For finite groups $[G : H] = |G|/|H|$, and index is multiplicative in towers.
See [Chapter 14](14-Subgroups-Cosets-and-Lagrange.md#143-cosets).

**Induction**
A method of proof for statements $P(n)$ about whole numbers: prove the base case, then prove that $P(n)$ implies $P(n + 1)$. It rests on the well-ordering principle.
See [Chapter 8](08-Sequences-Series-and-Induction.md#83-proof-by-induction).

**Inductive hypothesis**
In the inductive step, the assumption that $P(n)$ holds, from which $P(n + 1)$ is to be shown.
See [Chapter 8](08-Sequences-Series-and-Induction.md#83-proof-by-induction).

**Inequality**
A statement that one expression is less than (or greater than, or at most, or at least) another. Multiplying both sides by a negative number reverses its direction.
See [Chapter 2](02-Equations-and-Inequalities.md#25-inequalities-and-the-sign-flip).

**Integral domain**
A commutative ring with $1 \ne 0$ and no zero divisors, so $ab = 0$ forces $a = 0$ or $b = 0$. $\mathbb{Z}$ and every field are integral domains; $\mathbb{Z}_6$ is not.
See [Chapter 17](17-Rings-and-Ideals.md#172-integral-domains-and-fields).

**Intertwiner**
A linear map $\varphi$ between representations with $\varphi\rho_V(g) = \rho_W(g)\varphi$ for all $g$. Schur's lemma says an intertwiner between irreducible representations is 0 or an isomorphism.
See [Chapter 21](21-Modules-and-Representations.md#215-characters-and-orthogonality).

**Interval**
A whole stretch of the number line, such as $[a, b]$ (ends included), $(a, b)$ (ends excluded) or $[a, \infty)$. Solutions of inequalities are usually intervals.
See [Chapter 2](02-Equations-and-Inequalities.md#25-inequalities-and-the-sign-flip).

**Invariant factors**
The numbers (or ring elements) $d_1 \mid d_2 \mid \cdots \mid d_k$ in the decomposition $\mathbb{Z}_{d_1} \times \cdots \times \mathbb{Z}_{d_k}$ of a finite abelian group, or $R/(d_1) \oplus \cdots$ of a module over a PID. They are uniquely determined.
See [Chapter 15](15-Homomorphisms-and-Quotients.md#158-finite-abelian-groups-classified) and [Chapter 21](21-Modules-and-Representations.md#212-modules-over-a-pid-the-structure-theorem).

**Invariant subspace**
A subspace $W$ with $T(W) \subseteq W$ for a linear map $T$, or $\rho(g)W \subseteq W$ for every $g$ in a representation. It is a submodule in the module view.
See [Chapter 21](21-Modules-and-Representations.md#211-modules-over-a-ring).

**Inverse (in a group)**
For an element $a$, the element $a^{-1}$ with $aa^{-1} = a^{-1}a = e$. In a group it is unique, and $(ab)^{-1} = b^{-1}a^{-1}$ (the socks and shoes rule).
See [Chapter 13](13-Groups.md#132-the-axioms).

**Inverse function**
A function $g : B \to A$ that undoes $f : A \to B$: $g(f(a)) = a$ and $f(g(b)) = b$. A function has an inverse exactly when it is a bijection, and its graph is the mirror image of $f$'s in $y = x$.
See [Chapter 6](06-Functions.md#65-one-to-one-onto-and-inverse-functions).

**Irreducible element**
A nonzero non-unit $p$ of an integral domain such that whenever $p = ab$, one of $a$ and $b$ is a unit. In a UFD irreducibles are prime, but in $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ the element 2 is irreducible and not prime.
See [Chapter 18](18-Factorization-in-Rings.md#181-euclidean-domains).

**Irreducible polynomial**
A nonconstant polynomial that is not a product of two polynomials of smaller degree over the given field. $x^2 + 1$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{R}$ but not over $\mathbb{C}$.
See [Chapter 4](04-Polynomials-and-Factoring.md#44-factoring) and [Chapter 17](17-Rings-and-Ideals.md#177-polynomial-rings).

**Irreducible representation**
A nonzero representation whose only invariant subspaces are 0 and the whole space. By Maschke's theorem every complex representation of a finite group is a direct sum of irreducible ones.
See [Chapter 21](21-Modules-and-Representations.md#213-representations-of-finite-groups).

**Isomorphism**
A homomorphism that is a bijection. Two groups (or rings) are isomorphic, $G \cong H$, if an isomorphism exists: they are the same structure with the elements renamed.
See [Chapter 15](15-Homomorphisms-and-Quotients.md#153-isomorphism).

## J

**Jordan block**
The matrix with $\lambda$ on the diagonal, 1 just above it, and 0 elsewhere. The Jordan normal form writes a complex linear map as a block-diagonal matrix of Jordan blocks.
See [Chapter 21](21-Modules-and-Representations.md#212-modules-over-a-pid-the-structure-theorem).

**Jordan-Hölder theorem**
The list of composition factors of a finite group, with multiplicities, does not depend on the composition series chosen. Simple groups are the atoms of finite groups.
See [Chapter 16](16-Group-Actions-and-Sylow.md#168-simple-groups-and-the-classification).

## K

**Kernel**
The set $\ker\varphi$ of elements a homomorphism sends to the identity (or to 0 for rings). It is a normal subgroup (or an ideal), and it is trivial exactly when $\varphi$ is one-to-one.
See [Chapter 15](15-Homomorphisms-and-Quotients.md#152-kernel-and-image).

**Klein four-group**
The group $V_4 = \{e, a, b, c\}$ in which every non-identity element has order 2 and the product of any two of them is the third. It is isomorphic to $\mathbb{Z}_2 \times \mathbb{Z}_2$, and with $\mathbb{Z}_4$ it is one of the two groups of order 4.
See [Chapter 14](14-Subgroups-Cosets-and-Lagrange.md#147-groups-of-small-order).

## L

**Lagrange interpolation**
The formula for the unique polynomial of degree less than $n$ through $n$ given points with different $x$-values.
See [Chapter 10](10-Roots-and-Coefficients.md#108-lagrange-interpolation).

**Lagrange resolvent**
An expression such as $r_1 + \omega r_2 + \omega^2 r_3$ in the roots of a polynomial, with $\omega$ a root of unity. Its powers take few values under permutations of the roots, and that is why cubics and quartics can be solved.
See [Chapter 11](11-Cubics-and-Quartics.md#118-lagranges-resolvents-the-pattern-that-fails-at-degree-5).

**Lagrange's theorem**
In a finite group the order of every subgroup divides the order of the group. It follows that the order of every element divides $|G|$.
See [Chapter 14](14-Subgroups-Cosets-and-Lagrange.md#144-lagranges-theorem).

**Leading coefficient**
In a polynomial of degree $n$, the coefficient $a_n$ of the highest power; $a_nx^n$ is the leading term.
See [Chapter 4](04-Polynomials-and-Factoring.md#41-polynomials).

**Lexicographic order**
The dictionary order on monomials: compare the powers of $x_1$, then of $x_2$ if they are equal, and so on. It is used to prove the theorem on symmetric polynomials and in Gröbner bases.
See [Chapter 10](10-Roots-and-Coefficients.md#105-symmetric-polynomials).

**Lie algebra**
A vector space with a bilinear bracket $[X, Y]$ that is skew-symmetric and satisfies the Jacobi identity. The tangent space at the identity of a Lie group is one.
See [Chapter 22](22-Where-Algebra-Goes.md#224-lie-groups-and-lie-algebras-symmetry-that-moves).

**Lie group**
A group that is also a smooth manifold, with multiplication and inverse smooth maps, such as the rotation group $SO(3)$. Lie groups describe continuous symmetry.
See [Chapter 22](22-Where-Algebra-Goes.md#224-lie-groups-and-lie-algebras-symmetry-that-moves).

**Like terms**
Terms with exactly the same letters raised to exactly the same powers, such as $3x^2$ and $-8x^2$. Only like terms can be collected into one.
See [Chapter 1](01-The-Language-of-Algebra.md#12-expressions-terms-and-coefficients).

**Limit (of a sequence)**
A sequence $s_n$ has limit $S$ if, for every tolerance $\varepsilon > 0$, all terms from some point on are within $\varepsilon$ of $S$.
See [Chapter 8](08-Sequences-Series-and-Induction.md#86-infinite-geometric-series-and-a-first-look-at-convergence).

**Linear equation**
An equation in $x$ that can be rewritten as $ax + b = 0$. If $a \neq 0$ it has exactly one solution, $x = -b/a$.
See [Chapter 2](02-Equations-and-Inequalities.md#22-linear-equations).

**Linear program**
A problem of making a linear objective as large or small as possible subject to linear inequality constraints. The best value is reached at a corner of the feasible region.
See [Chapter 3](03-Lines-and-Linear-Systems.md#37-linear-inequalities-and-a-first-linear-program).

**Linear recurrence with constant coefficients**
A rule such as $a_{n+1} = pa_n + qa_{n-1}$ with fixed $p$ and $q$. It is solved by trying $a_n = r^n$, which leads to the characteristic equation.
See [Chapter 8](08-Sequences-Series-and-Induction.md#85-recurrences-and-binets-formula).

**Logarithm**
The logarithm to base $a$ of $x$, $\log_a x$, is the exponent $y$ with $a^y = x$: it counts how many factors of $a$ make $x$. It turns products into sums.
See [Chapter 7](07-Exponents-and-Logarithms.md#73-logarithms-the-inverse-of-exponentials).

## M

**Maschke's theorem**
Every representation of a finite group over $\mathbb{C}$ (or any field whose characteristic does not divide $|G|$) is a direct sum of irreducible representations: every invariant subspace has an invariant complement.
See [Chapter 21](21-Modules-and-Representations.md#214-maschkes-theorem).

**Maximal ideal**
An ideal $M \ne R$ with no ideal strictly between $M$ and $R$. $R/M$ is a field exactly when $M$ is maximal.
See [Chapter 17](17-Rings-and-Ideals.md#175-prime-and-maximal-ideals).

**Minimal polynomial (of an algebraic element)**
The monic polynomial of least degree over $F$ with $\alpha$ as a root. It is irreducible, it divides every polynomial with root $\alpha$, and its degree is $[F(\alpha) : F]$.
See [Chapter 19](19-Fields-and-Extensions.md#192-algebraic-elements-and-minimal-polynomials).

**Minimal polynomial (of a linear map)**
The monic polynomial of least degree with $p(T) = 0$. It is the largest invariant factor of $V$ as an $F[x]$-module.
See [Chapter 21](21-Modules-and-Representations.md#212-modules-over-a-pid-the-structure-theorem).

**Modular form**
A function on the upper half-plane with an enormous symmetry group, given by a $q$-series. The modularity theorem says every elliptic curve over $\mathbb{Q}$ matches one.
See [Chapter 22](22-Where-Algebra-Goes.md#225-the-langlands-programme).

**Module**
An abelian group $M$ with a multiplication by elements of a ring $R$ satisfying the vector space rules. Over a field it is a vector space; over $\mathbb{Z}$ it is an abelian group.
See [Chapter 21](21-Modules-and-Representations.md#211-modules-over-a-ring).

**Modulus (of a complex number)**
The length $|z| = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}$ of $z = a + bi$, its distance from 0. It satisfies $|zw| = |z|\,|w|$ and $z\bar z = |z|^2$.
See [Chapter 9](09-Complex-Numbers.md#93-the-complex-plane-the-conjugate-and-the-modulus).

**Monic polynomial**
A polynomial whose leading coefficient is 1, such as $x^2 - 3x + 2$.
See [Chapter 4](04-Polynomials-and-Factoring.md#41-polynomials).

**Monomial**
A polynomial with one term, such as $5x^3$, or in several variables a product such as $x_1^{a_1} \cdots x_n^{a_n}$.
See [Chapter 4](04-Polynomials-and-Factoring.md#41-polynomials).

**Multiplicity**
A root $a$ of $f$ has multiplicity $m$ if $(x - a)^m$ divides $f$ but $(x - a)^{m+1}$ does not. Counted with multiplicity, a polynomial of degree $n$ has at most $n$ roots.
See [Chapter 10](10-Roots-and-Coefficients.md#102-at-most-n-roots).

## N

**Natural logarithm**
The logarithm to base $e$, written $\ln$. It is the inverse of the exponential function $e^x$.
See [Chapter 7](07-Exponents-and-Logarithms.md#73-logarithms-the-inverse-of-exponentials).

**Natural transformation**
A way of mapping one functor to another that commutes with every morphism. It makes precise when an isomorphism is "natural", like a vector space and its double dual.
See [Chapter 22](22-Where-Algebra-Goes.md#221-categories-and-functors).

**Newton's identities**
Formulas that relate the power sums $p_k = r_1^k + \cdots + r_n^k$ of the roots to the elementary symmetric polynomials, so power sums can be found from the coefficients without the roots.
See [Chapter 10](10-Roots-and-Coefficients.md#106-newtons-identities).

**Newton's method**
The iteration $x_{k+1} = x_k - \frac{f(x_k)}{f'(x_k)}$ for finding a root numerically. Near a simple root it roughly doubles the number of correct digits at each step.
See [Chapter 12](12-The-Fundamental-Theorem-of-Algebra.md#126-numerical-root-finding-and-what-solving-means).

**Nilpotent**
A ring element $a$ with $a^n = 0$ for some $n$, such as $6$ in $\mathbb{Z}_{12}$. The nilpotent elements of a commutative ring form an ideal, the nilradical.
See [Chapter 17](17-Rings-and-Ideals.md#172-integral-domains-and-fields).

**Noetherian ring**
A ring in which every ideal is finitely generated; equivalently, every increasing chain of ideals stops. Named after Emmy Noether.
See [Chapter 17](17-Rings-and-Ideals.md#177-polynomial-rings).

**Norm**
A multiplicative size function on a ring of numbers, such as $N(a + bi) = a^2 + b^2$ on $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ or $N(a + b\sqrt{-5}) = a^2 + 5b^2$. Units have norm 1, and it turns factoring questions into questions about integers.
See [Chapter 18](18-Factorization-in-Rings.md#181-euclidean-domains).

**Normal extension**
An algebraic extension $K/F$ in which every irreducible polynomial over $F$ that has one root in $K$ splits completely in $K$. Finite normal extensions are splitting fields.
See [Chapter 20](20-Galois-Theory.md#203-normal-and-separable-extensions).

**Normal subgroup**
A subgroup $N$ with $gNg^{-1} = N$ for every $g \in G$, written $N \trianglelefteq G$; equivalently, its left and right cosets agree. Normal subgroups are exactly the kernels of homomorphisms, and the ones you can form quotients by.
See [Chapter 15](15-Homomorphisms-and-Quotients.md#154-normal-subgroups).

**Normalizer**
The subgroup $N_G(H) = \{g : gHg^{-1} = H\}$: the stabilizer of $H$ when $G$ acts on its subgroups by conjugation. The number of conjugates of $H$ is $[G : N_G(H)]$.
See [Chapter 16](16-Group-Actions-and-Sylow.md#161-actions-orbits-stabilizers).

**Number field**
A finite extension of $\mathbb{Q}$, such as $\mathbb{Q}(i)$ or $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]2)$. Its ring of integers plays the part that $\mathbb{Z}$ plays for $\mathbb{Q}$.
See [Chapter 22](22-Where-Algebra-Goes.md#223-algebraic-number-theory-and-class-groups).

## O

**Objective**
The quantity a linear program makes as large or as small as possible, such as profit $P = 30x + 50y$.
See [Chapter 3](03-Lines-and-Linear-Systems.md#37-linear-inequalities-and-a-first-linear-program).

**One-to-one (injective)**
A function is one-to-one if different inputs give different outputs: $f(a_1) = f(a_2)$ implies $a_1 = a_2$. A homomorphism is one-to-one exactly when its kernel is trivial.
See [Chapter 6](06-Functions.md#65-one-to-one-onto-and-inverse-functions).

**Onto (surjective)**
A function $f : A \to B$ is onto if every element of $B$ is an output: the range is the whole codomain.
See [Chapter 6](06-Functions.md#65-one-to-one-onto-and-inverse-functions).

**Orbit**
The set $G \cdot x = \{g \cdot x : g \in G\}$ of all places an action can move $x$ to. The orbits partition the set acted on.
See [Chapter 16](16-Group-Actions-and-Sylow.md#161-actions-orbits-stabilizers).

**Orbit-stabilizer theorem**
For a group $G$ acting on $X$, the orbit of $x$ is in one-to-one correspondence with the cosets of its stabilizer, so $|G| = |G \cdot x| \cdot |G_x|$ for finite $G$.
See [Chapter 16](16-Group-Actions-and-Sylow.md#162-the-orbit-stabilizer-theorem).

**Order (of a group)**
The number of elements of a group, written $|G|$: $|D_4| = 8$, $|S_n| = n!$, $|U(n)| = \varphi(n)$.
See [Chapter 13](13-Groups.md#134-group-tables-and-order).

**Order (of an element)**
The smallest $k \ge 1$ with $g^k = e$, or infinite if there is none. It equals the order of the cyclic subgroup $\langle g \rangle$, and $g^m = e$ exactly when the order divides $m$.
See [Chapter 13](13-Groups.md#134-group-tables-and-order) and [Chapter 14](14-Subgroups-Cosets-and-Lagrange.md#145-the-order-of-an-element).

**Order of operations**
The convention for reading an expression: brackets first, then powers, then multiplication and division from left to right, then addition and subtraction from left to right.
See [Chapter 1](01-The-Language-of-Algebra.md#14-the-order-of-operations).

**Ordered field**
A field with a notion of positive numbers that is closed under addition and multiplication, with every number exactly one of positive, negative or zero. $\mathbb{Q}$ and $\mathbb{R}$ are ordered fields; $\mathbb{C}$ cannot be ordered.
See [Chapter 2](02-Equations-and-Inequalities.md#25-inequalities-and-the-sign-flip).

**Ordered pair**
A pair $(x, y)$ in which order matters, so $(2, 5) \neq (5, 2)$. Points of the plane are ordered pairs of coordinates.
See [Chapter 3](03-Lines-and-Linear-Systems.md#31-coordinates).

**Origin**
The point $O = (0, 0)$ where the axes of a coordinate plane cross.
See [Chapter 3](03-Lines-and-Linear-Systems.md#31-coordinates).

## P

**$p$-group**
A group whose order is a power of a prime $p$. Every nontrivial finite $p$-group has a nontrivial centre.
See [Chapter 16](16-Group-Actions-and-Sylow.md#164-the-class-equation-and-p-groups-have-a-centre).

**Parabola**
The graph of $y = ax^2 + bx + c$ with $a \neq 0$; equivalently, the set of points equally far from a focus and a directrix.
See [Chapter 5](05-Quadratics.md#51-the-parabola).

**Parallel lines**
Lines with the same slope that do not meet. Two distinct non-vertical lines are parallel exactly when their slopes are equal.
See [Chapter 3](03-Lines-and-Linear-Systems.md#33-forms-of-a-line).

**Parameter**
A number that is fixed in a given situation but could change from one situation to another, such as $a$, $b$ and $c$ in $ax^2 + bx + c$.
See [Chapter 1](01-The-Language-of-Algebra.md#11-why-letters).

**Partial fraction decomposition**
Writing a proper rational expression as a sum of simpler fractions with denominators that are powers of linear or irreducible quadratic factors, such as $\frac{1}{x^2 - 1} = \frac{1/2}{x - 1} - \frac{1/2}{x + 1}$.
See [Chapter 4](04-Polynomials-and-Factoring.md#47-partial-fractions).

**Partial sums**
For a series $\sum a_k$, the finite sums $s_n = a_1 + \cdots + a_n$. The series converges when its partial sums approach a limit.
See [Chapter 8](08-Sequences-Series-and-Induction.md#86-infinite-geometric-series-and-a-first-look-at-convergence).

**Partition**
A splitting of a set into disjoint, nonempty pieces that cover it, such as the cosets of a subgroup. A partition of a number is a way of writing it as a sum of positive integers, which counts abelian groups of prime-power order.
See [Chapter 14](14-Subgroups-Cosets-and-Lagrange.md#143-cosets).

**Pascal's triangle**
The triangle of binomial coefficients: each row starts and ends with 1, and every inner number is the sum of the two above it (Pascal's rule, $\binom{n}{k} = \binom{n-1}{k-1} + \binom{n-1}{k}$).
See [Chapter 8](08-Sequences-Series-and-Induction.md#84-the-binomial-theorem-and-pascals-triangle).

**Pell's equation**
The equation $a^2 - db^2 = \pm 1$ in integers. Its solutions give the units of $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt d]$.
See [Chapter 22](22-Where-Algebra-Goes.md#223-algebraic-number-theory-and-class-groups).

**Periodic function**
A function with $f(x + p) = f(x)$ for every $x$ and some fixed $p > 0$, the period. Its graph repeats every $p$.
See [Chapter 6](06-Functions.md#66-even-odd-periodic-and-piecewise-functions).

**Permutation**
A one-to-one, onto function from a set to itself: a rearrangement. The permutations of $\{1, \ldots, n\}$ form the symmetric group $S_n$.
See [Chapter 13](13-Groups.md#133-examples-everywhere).

**Perpendicular lines**
Lines that meet at a right angle. Two non-vertical lines are perpendicular exactly when the product of their slopes is $-1$.
See [Chapter 3](03-Lines-and-Linear-Systems.md#33-forms-of-a-line).

**Pigeonhole principle**
If more than $n$ objects go into $n$ boxes, some box gets two; as a fact about functions, there is no one-to-one function from a larger finite set to a smaller one.
See [Chapter 6](06-Functions.md#67-functions-as-sets-of-pairs-the-formal-definition).

**Polar form**
Writing a complex number as $z = r(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta) = re^{i\theta}$, with $r = |z|$ and $\theta$ an argument. Multiplying complex numbers multiplies their moduli and adds their arguments.
See [Chapter 9](09-Complex-Numbers.md#94-polar-form-multiplication-is-rotation).

**Polynomial**
An expression $a_nx^n + a_{n-1}x^{n-1} + \cdots + a_1x + a_0$ with whole-number powers of $x$ and numbers as coefficients. Formally, an element of a polynomial ring.
See [Chapter 4](04-Polynomials-and-Factoring.md#41-polynomials).

**Polynomial ring**
For a commutative ring $R$, the ring $R[x]$ of polynomials with coefficients in $R$. When $F$ is a field, $F[x]$ has division with remainder and every ideal is principal.
See [Chapter 17](17-Rings-and-Ideals.md#177-polynomial-rings).

**Power sums**
The sums $p_k = r_1^k + r_2^k + \cdots + r_n^k$ of the $k$-th powers of the roots. Newton's identities relate them to the coefficients.
See [Chapter 10](10-Roots-and-Coefficients.md#106-newtons-identities).

**Prime element**
A nonzero non-unit $p$ of an integral domain such that $p \mid ab$ implies $p \mid a$ or $p \mid b$. Every prime is irreducible.
See [Chapter 18](18-Factorization-in-Rings.md#181-euclidean-domains).

**Prime field**
The smallest subfield of a field: $\mathbb{Q}$ in characteristic 0, and $\mathbb{F}_p$ in characteristic $p$.
See [Chapter 17](17-Rings-and-Ideals.md#176-characteristic).

**Prime ideal**
An ideal $P \ne R$ such that $ab \in P$ implies $a \in P$ or $b \in P$. $R/P$ is an integral domain exactly when $P$ is prime, and every maximal ideal is prime.
See [Chapter 17](17-Rings-and-Ideals.md#175-prime-and-maximal-ideals).

**Primitive element**
A generator of the cyclic group of nonzero elements of a finite field. Separately, an element $\alpha$ with $K = F(\alpha)$; the primitive element theorem says one exists for every finite extension in characteristic 0.
See [Chapter 19](19-Fields-and-Extensions.md#195-finite-fields).

**Primitive polynomial**
A polynomial in $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ whose coefficients have greatest common divisor 1.
See [Chapter 18](18-Factorization-in-Rings.md#185-gausss-lemma-and-eisensteins-criterion).

**Primitive root (mod $n$)**
A generator of the group $U(n)$ of units mod $n$. One exists exactly when $n$ is $1$, $2$, $4$, $p^k$ or $2p^k$ for an odd prime $p$.
See [Chapter 13](13-Groups.md#135-cyclic-groups).

**Primitive root of unity**
An $n$-th root of unity $\omega$ whose powers give all $n$ of them, such as $e^{2\pi i/n}$.
See [Chapter 9](09-Complex-Numbers.md#96-roots-of-unity).

**Principal ideal**
The ideal $(a) = \{ra : r \in R\}$ of all multiples of one element. In $\mathbb{Z}$ and in $F[x]$ every ideal is principal.
See [Chapter 17](17-Rings-and-Ideals.md#173-ideals-and-quotient-rings).

**Principal ideal domain (PID)**
An integral domain in which every ideal is principal. Every Euclidean domain is a PID, and every PID is a UFD; in a PID gcds exist and satisfy Bézout's identity.
See [Chapter 18](18-Factorization-in-Rings.md#182-principal-ideal-domains).

**Proper rational expression**
A fraction $\frac{p}{q}$ of polynomials with $\deg p < \deg q$. Partial fractions apply to proper fractions; an improper one is divided first.
See [Chapter 4](04-Polynomials-and-Factoring.md#47-partial-fractions).

**Proper subgroup**
A subgroup other than the trivial subgroup $\{e\}$ and the whole group.
See [Chapter 14](14-Subgroups-Cosets-and-Lagrange.md#141-subgroups-and-the-test).

**Purely imaginary**
A complex number $bi$ with real part 0.
See [Chapter 9](09-Complex-Numbers.md#92-arithmetic-with-complex-numbers).

## Q

**Quadrant**
One of the four regions into which the axes cut the coordinate plane, numbered counterclockwise from the top right.
See [Chapter 3](03-Lines-and-Linear-Systems.md#31-coordinates).

**Quadratic**
A polynomial of degree 2, $ax^2 + bx + c$ with $a \neq 0$. Its graph is a parabola.
See [Chapter 5](05-Quadratics.md#51-the-parabola).

**Quadratic formula**
The roots of $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$ are $x = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}$, proved by completing the square.
See [Chapter 5](05-Quadratics.md#53-the-quadratic-formula-proved).

**Quartic**
A polynomial of degree 4. Ferrari's method reduces it to a cubic, so quartics can be solved by radicals.
See [Chapter 11](11-Cubics-and-Quartics.md#116-ferraris-quartic-method).

**Quaternion group**
The group $Q_8 = \{\pm 1, \pm i, \pm j, \pm k\}$ with $i^2 = j^2 = k^2 = ijk = -1$. It is a nonabelian group of order 8 in which every subgroup is normal.
See [Chapter 14](14-Subgroups-Cosets-and-Lagrange.md#147-groups-of-small-order).

**Quaternions**
Hamilton's numbers $a + bi + cj + dk$ with $i^2 = j^2 = k^2 = ijk = -1$, found in 1843. They form a division ring that is not commutative; unit quaternions describe rotations in space.
See [Chapter 9](09-Complex-Numbers.md#98-the-complex-numbers-as-a-field) and [Chapter 17](17-Rings-and-Ideals.md#171-rings-examples).

**Quintic**
A polynomial of degree 5. The general quintic has no formula in radicals, and particular ones such as $x^5 - 4x + 2$ cannot be solved by radicals.
See [Chapter 20](20-Galois-Theory.md#206-why-the-general-quintic-has-no-formula).

**Quotient group**
For a normal subgroup $N$ of $G$, the group $G/N$ of cosets of $N$, with $(aN)(bN) = abN$. Also called a factor group; $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z} = \mathbb{Z}_n$ is the basic example.
See [Chapter 15](15-Homomorphisms-and-Quotients.md#155-quotient-groups).

**Quotient ring**
For an ideal $I$ of $R$, the ring $R/I$ of cosets $a + I$, with the operations of $R$. $\mathbb{Z}/(n) = \mathbb{Z}_n$ and $\mathbb{R}[x]/(x^2 + 1) \cong \mathbb{C}$ are examples.
See [Chapter 17](17-Rings-and-Ideals.md#173-ideals-and-quotient-rings).

## R

**Radical (of an ideal)**
The ideal $\sqrt{J}$ of polynomials (or ring elements) $f$ with $f^r \in J$ for some $r \ge 1$. The strong Nullstellensatz says $I(V(J)) = \sqrt{J}$.
See [Chapter 22](22-Where-Algebra-Goes.md#222-algebraic-geometry-equations-as-shapes).

**Radical extension**
An extension $L/F$ reached by a chain of fields $F = L_0 \subseteq L_1 \subseteq \cdots \subseteq L_m = L$, each obtained from the one before by adjoining an $n$-th root of one of its elements.
See [Chapter 20](20-Galois-Theory.md#206-why-the-general-quintic-has-no-formula).

**Range**
The set of outputs a function actually produces. It lies inside the codomain and may be smaller.
See [Chapter 6](06-Functions.md#61-what-a-function-is).

**Rank (of a module)**
The number $r$ of copies of $R$ in the decomposition $M \cong R^r \oplus R/(d_1) \oplus \cdots \oplus R/(d_k)$ of a finitely generated module over a PID: the size of its free part.
See [Chapter 21](21-Modules-and-Representations.md#212-modules-over-a-pid-the-structure-theorem).

**Rate of change**
How fast $y$ changes as $x$ changes; for a line it is the slope, with units of $y$ per unit of $x$.
See [Chapter 3](03-Lines-and-Linear-Systems.md#32-slope-as-a-rate).

**Rational canonical form**
A block-diagonal matrix of companion matrices, one for each invariant factor of a linear map. It exists over any field, with no need to find eigenvalues.
See [Chapter 21](21-Modules-and-Representations.md#212-modules-over-a-pid-the-structure-theorem).

**Rational expression**
A fraction $\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}$ of two polynomials, with $q$ not the zero polynomial. You may cancel common factors, never common terms.
See [Chapter 4](04-Polynomials-and-Factoring.md#46-rational-expressions).

**Rational root theorem**
If a polynomial with integer coefficients has a rational root $\frac pq$ in lowest terms, then $p$ divides the constant term and $q$ divides the leading coefficient.
See [Chapter 10](10-Roots-and-Coefficients.md#103-the-rational-root-theorem).

**Real part**
The real number $a$ in $z = a + bi$, written $\operatorname{Re} z$.
See [Chapter 9](09-Complex-Numbers.md#92-arithmetic-with-complex-numbers).

**Reciprocal**
For a number $a \neq 0$, the number $\frac1a$ with $a \cdot \frac1a = 1$: its inverse under multiplication. Zero has no reciprocal.
See [Chapter 1](01-The-Language-of-Algebra.md#13-the-laws-of-arithmetic).

**Recurrence**
A rule that builds each term of a sequence from earlier ones, such as $F_{n+1} = F_n + F_{n-1}$ for the Fibonacci numbers.
See [Chapter 8](08-Sequences-Series-and-Induction.md#81-arithmetic-and-geometric-sequences).

**Regular representation**
The representation of a group $G$ on the group algebra $\mathbb{C}[G]$ by left multiplication, of dimension $|G|$. It contains every irreducible representation, as many times as its dimension.
See [Chapter 21](21-Modules-and-Representations.md#213-representations-of-finite-groups).

**Relation**
A set of ordered pairs in which an element may be paired with several others, or none, such as "is less than". A function is a special kind of relation.
See [Chapter 6](06-Functions.md#67-functions-as-sets-of-pairs-the-formal-definition).

**Remainder theorem**
The remainder when a polynomial $f(x)$ is divided by $x - c$ is $f(c)$.
See [Chapter 10](10-Roots-and-Coefficients.md#101-the-remainder-and-factor-theorems).

**Representation**
A homomorphism $\rho : G \to GL(V)$ from a group to the invertible linear maps of a vector space, so each group element acts as a matrix with $\rho(gh) = \rho(g)\rho(h)$. Its dimension is $\dim V$.
See [Chapter 21](21-Modules-and-Representations.md#213-representations-of-finite-groups).

**Resolvent cubic**
The cubic equation that Ferrari's method needs to solve on the way to the roots of a quartic.
See [Chapter 11](11-Cubics-and-Quartics.md#116-ferraris-quartic-method).

**Ring**
A set with addition and multiplication such that it is an abelian group under addition, multiplication is associative with an identity 1, and the distributive laws hold. $\mathbb{Z}$, $\mathbb{Z}_n$, polynomials and square matrices are rings.
See [Chapter 17](17-Rings-and-Ideals.md#171-rings-examples).

**Ring homomorphism**
A map $\varphi : R \to S$ with $\varphi(a + b) = \varphi(a) + \varphi(b)$, $\varphi(ab) = \varphi(a)\varphi(b)$ and $\varphi(1) = 1$. Its kernel is an ideal.
See [Chapter 17](17-Rings-and-Ideals.md#174-ring-homomorphisms-and-the-isomorphism-theorem).

**Ring of integers**
The ring $\mathcal{O}_K$ of algebraic integers in a number field $K$, such as $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ in $\mathbb{Q}(i)$. It is a Dedekind domain, but it need not have unique factorization of elements.
See [Chapter 22](22-Where-Algebra-Goes.md#223-algebraic-number-theory-and-class-groups).

**Root**
A number that makes an equation true, or at which a polynomial is 0. Also called a solution or, for a polynomial, a zero.
See [Chapter 2](02-Equations-and-Inequalities.md#21-the-balance-idea).

**Roots of unity**
The $n$ complex numbers $z$ with $z^n = 1$, namely $e^{2\pi ik/n}$ for $k = 0, \ldots, n - 1$. They sit at the corners of a regular $n$-gon on the unit circle.
See [Chapter 9](09-Complex-Numbers.md#96-roots-of-unity).

## S

**S-polynomial**
For two polynomials, the combination that multiplies each by the monomial needed to make their leading terms equal and subtracts, cancelling those terms. Buchberger's algorithm is built on it.
See [Chapter 22](22-Where-Algebra-Goes.md#226-computer-algebra-and-grobner-bases).

**Semidirect product**
A twisted version of the direct product, $N \rtimes H$, formed when only one of the two subgroups is normal. $S_3 \cong \mathbb{Z}_3 \rtimes \mathbb{Z}_2$, and the nonabelian group of order 21 is $\mathbb{Z}_7 \rtimes \mathbb{Z}_3$.
See [Chapter 15](15-Homomorphisms-and-Quotients.md#157-direct-products-and-the-chinese-remainder-theorem).

**Separable**
A polynomial is separable if it has no repeated roots in a splitting field; an algebraic extension is separable if every element's minimal polynomial is. In characteristic 0 everything is separable.
See [Chapter 20](20-Galois-Theory.md#203-normal-and-separable-extensions).

**Sequence**
A list of numbers in order, $a_1, a_2, a_3, \ldots$, given by an explicit formula or by a recurrence. The numbers are its terms.
See [Chapter 8](08-Sequences-Series-and-Induction.md#81-arithmetic-and-geometric-sequences).

**Series**
An infinite sum $\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} a_k$, defined as the limit of its partial sums when that limit exists.
See [Chapter 8](08-Sequences-Series-and-Induction.md#86-infinite-geometric-series-and-a-first-look-at-convergence).

**Sigma notation**
The notation $\sum_{k=1}^{n} a_k$ for $a_1 + a_2 + \cdots + a_n$. The letter $k$ is a dummy index with no meaning outside the sum.
See [Chapter 8](08-Sequences-Series-and-Induction.md#82-sigma-notation).

**Simple extension**
A field extension $F(\alpha)$ generated by one element. If $\alpha$ is algebraic, $F(\alpha) \cong F[x]/(m(x))$ with $m$ its minimal polynomial.
See [Chapter 19](19-Fields-and-Extensions.md#191-field-extensions-degree-and-the-tower-law).

**Simple group**
A nontrivial group whose only normal subgroups are $\{e\}$ and itself. The cyclic groups of prime order and $A_n$ for $n \ge 5$ are simple.
See [Chapter 16](16-Group-Actions-and-Sylow.md#167-using-sylow).

**Slope**
For the line through $(x_1, y_1)$ and $(x_2, y_2)$ with $x_1 \neq x_2$, the number $m = \frac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1}$: rise over run. Vertical lines have no slope.
See [Chapter 3](03-Lines-and-Linear-Systems.md#32-slope-as-a-rate).

**Smith normal form**
The diagonal form $\operatorname{diag}(d_1, d_2, \ldots)$ with $d_1 \mid d_2 \mid \cdots$ that row and column operations reach for any matrix over a Euclidean domain. It proves the structure theorem for modules over a PID.
See [Chapter 21](21-Modules-and-Representations.md#212-modules-over-a-pid-the-structure-theorem).

**Solution set**
The collection of all solutions of an equation or inequality. To solve is to find it.
See [Chapter 2](02-Equations-and-Inequalities.md#21-the-balance-idea).

**Solvable by radicals**
A polynomial $f$ over $F$ is solvable by radicals if its splitting field lies in some radical extension of $F$: its roots can be written with $+$, $-$, $\times$, $\div$ and $n$-th roots. In characteristic 0 this happens exactly when its Galois group is solvable.
See [Chapter 20](20-Galois-Theory.md#206-why-the-general-quintic-has-no-formula).

**Solvable group**
A group with a chain of subgroups $\{e\} = G_0 \trianglelefteq G_1 \trianglelefteq \cdots \trianglelefteq G_n = G$ in which every quotient $G_{i}/G_{i-1}$ is abelian. $S_4$ is solvable; $S_5$ is not.
See [Chapter 20](20-Galois-Theory.md#205-solvable-groups).

**Splitting field**
An extension of $F$ in which a polynomial $f$ factors into linear factors, and which is generated over $F$ by the roots of $f$. It exists and is unique up to isomorphism.
See [Chapter 19](19-Fields-and-Extensions.md#194-splitting-fields).

**Sporadic groups**
The 26 finite simple groups that fit into none of the infinite families. The largest is the Monster, of order about $8 \times 10^{53}$.
See [Chapter 16](16-Group-Actions-and-Sylow.md#168-simple-groups-and-the-classification).

**Stabilizer**
The subgroup $G_x = \{g : g \cdot x = x\}$ of elements that leave $x$ where it is.
See [Chapter 16](16-Group-Actions-and-Sylow.md#161-actions-orbits-stabilizers).

**Strong induction**
A form of induction in which the inductive step may assume $P(k)$ for every $k \le n$, not only $P(n)$, to prove $P(n + 1)$.
See [Chapter 8](08-Sequences-Series-and-Induction.md#83-proof-by-induction).

**Structure theorem for modules over a PID**
Every finitely generated module over a PID is $R^r \oplus R/(d_1) \oplus \cdots \oplus R/(d_k)$ with $d_1 \mid \cdots \mid d_k$, uniquely. It gives the classification of finitely generated abelian groups and the Jordan normal form.
See [Chapter 21](21-Modules-and-Representations.md#212-modules-over-a-pid-the-structure-theorem).

**Subgroup**
A subset $H$ of a group that is itself a group with the same operation: it contains the identity and is closed under products and inverses. Written $H \le G$.
See [Chapter 14](14-Subgroups-Cosets-and-Lagrange.md#141-subgroups-and-the-test).

**Subgroup generated by a set**
For $S \subseteq G$, the smallest subgroup $\langle S \rangle$ containing $S$: all products of elements of $S$ and their inverses.
See [Chapter 14](14-Subgroups-Cosets-and-Lagrange.md#142-generated-subgroups).

**Submodule**
A subgroup of a module that is closed under multiplication by ring elements.
See [Chapter 21](21-Modules-and-Representations.md#211-modules-over-a-ring).

**Subring**
A subset of a ring that contains 1 and is closed under subtraction and multiplication, so it is a ring with the same operations.
See [Chapter 17](17-Rings-and-Ideals.md#171-rings-examples).

**Substitute**
To replace each letter of an expression by a given number, putting every substituted number in brackets.
See [Chapter 1](01-The-Language-of-Algebra.md#16-substitution-and-formulas).

**Sylow subgroup**
For $|G| = p^km$ with $p \nmid m$, a subgroup of order $p^k$, the largest power of $p$ possible. Sylow's theorems say such subgroups exist, are all conjugate, and number $n_p \equiv 1 \pmod p$ with $n_p \mid m$.
See [Chapter 16](16-Group-Actions-and-Sylow.md#166-the-sylow-theorems).

**Symmetric group**
The group $S_n$ of all permutations of $\{1, 2, \ldots, n\}$ under composition, with $n!$ elements. Every finite group is isomorphic to a subgroup of some $S_n$.
See [Chapter 13](13-Groups.md#133-examples-everywhere).

**Symmetric polynomial**
A polynomial in several variables that stays the same when the variables are permuted. Every symmetric polynomial is a polynomial in the elementary symmetric polynomials.
See [Chapter 10](10-Roots-and-Coefficients.md#105-symmetric-polynomials).

**Synthetic division**
A shortcut for dividing a polynomial by $x - c$ that works only with the coefficients. Also called Ruffini's rule, it is the same computation as evaluating in Horner's form.
See [Chapter 4](04-Polynomials-and-Factoring.md#45-long-division-and-synthetic-division).

**System of linear equations**
A collection of linear equations to be satisfied at the same time. It has exactly one solution, none (inconsistent) or infinitely many (dependent).
See [Chapter 3](03-Lines-and-Linear-Systems.md#34-two-equations-two-unknowns).

## T

**Term**
A piece of an expression that is added or subtracted: $3x^2 - 5xy + 7$ has the three terms $3x^2$, $-5xy$ and $7$. The numbers in a sequence are also called its terms.
See [Chapter 1](01-The-Language-of-Algebra.md#12-expressions-terms-and-coefficients).

**Torsion**
An element $m$ of a module is torsion if $rm = 0$ for some nonzero $r$ in the ring. A module is torsion-free if 0 is its only torsion element, and torsion if every element is.
See [Chapter 21](21-Modules-and-Representations.md#211-modules-over-a-ring).

**Tower law**
For fields $F \subseteq K \subseteq L$, $[L : F] = [L : K]\,[K : F]$: degrees multiply.
See [Chapter 19](19-Fields-and-Extensions.md#191-field-extensions-degree-and-the-tower-law).

**Transcendental number**
A number that is not algebraic over $\mathbb{Q}$: it is a root of no nonzero polynomial with rational coefficients. Liouville's number, $e$ and $\pi$ are transcendental, and almost every real number is.
See [Chapter 19](19-Fields-and-Extensions.md#196-transcendental-numbers).

**Transitive action**
An action with only one orbit: any point can be moved to any other.
See [Chapter 16](16-Group-Actions-and-Sylow.md#161-actions-orbits-stabilizers).

**Transposition**
A 2-cycle $(a\,b)$, which swaps two elements and fixes the rest. Every permutation is a product of transpositions.
See [Chapter 13](13-Groups.md#136-dihedral-and-symmetric-groups).

**Triangle inequality**
The inequality $|a + b| \le |a| + |b|$, for real numbers and for complex numbers alike: one side of a triangle is never longer than the other two together.
See [Chapter 2](02-Equations-and-Inequalities.md#26-absolute-value) and [Chapter 9](09-Complex-Numbers.md#93-the-complex-plane-the-conjugate-and-the-modulus).

**Triangular number**
The number $1 + 2 + \cdots + n = \frac{n(n + 1)}{2}$, so called because that many dots form a triangle.
See [Chapter 8](08-Sequences-Series-and-Induction.md#81-arithmetic-and-geometric-sequences).

**Trinomial**
A polynomial with three terms, such as $x^2 + 5x + 6$.
See [Chapter 4](04-Polynomials-and-Factoring.md#41-polynomials).

**Trivial subgroup**
The subgroup $\{e\}$ containing only the identity. Every group has it and itself as subgroups.
See [Chapter 14](14-Subgroups-Cosets-and-Lagrange.md#141-subgroups-and-the-test).

## U

**Unique factorization domain (UFD)**
An integral domain in which every nonzero non-unit is a product of irreducibles, uniquely up to order and associates. $\mathbb{Z}$, $F[x]$ and every PID are UFDs; $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ is not, since $6 = 2 \cdot 3 = (1 + \sqrt{-5})(1 - \sqrt{-5})$.
See [Chapter 18](18-Factorization-in-Rings.md#183-unique-factorization-domains-and-where-it-fails).

**Unit**
An element of a ring with a multiplicative inverse. The units of $\mathbb{Z}$ are $\pm 1$, those of $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ are $\pm 1, \pm i$, and in a field every nonzero element is a unit.
See [Chapter 17](17-Rings-and-Ideals.md#171-rings-examples).

**Units mod $n$**
The group $U(n)$ of residues mod $n$ that are coprime to $n$, under multiplication. It has $\varphi(n)$ elements.
See [Chapter 13](13-Groups.md#133-examples-everywhere).

**Universal property**
A description of an object by how it maps to or from every other object, which determines it up to unique isomorphism. Direct products and quotients have universal properties.
See [Chapter 22](22-Where-Algebra-Goes.md#221-categories-and-functors).

**Unknown**
A letter that stands for one particular number (or a few) that we are trying to find, such as $x$ in $3x + 2 = 17$.
See [Chapter 1](01-The-Language-of-Algebra.md#11-why-letters).

## V

**Variable**
A letter that stands for any number from some collection, in a statement meant to hold for each of them, such as $a$ and $b$ in $a + b = b + a$.
See [Chapter 1](01-The-Language-of-Algebra.md#11-why-letters).

**Vertex**
The turning point of a parabola, its highest or lowest point. For $y = a(x - h)^2 + k$ it is $(h, k)$.
See [Chapter 5](05-Quadratics.md#51-the-parabola).

**Vertex form**
The form $y = a(x - h)^2 + k$ of a quadratic, found by completing the square. It shows the vertex $(h, k)$ and so the maximum or minimum value.
See [Chapter 5](05-Quadratics.md#55-vertex-form-and-maximum-and-minimum-problems).

**Vertical line test**
A curve in the plane is the graph of a function of $x$ exactly when every vertical line meets it at most once.
See [Chapter 6](06-Functions.md#62-graphs-and-how-to-read-them).

**Vieta's formulas**
For a monic polynomial with roots $r_1, \ldots, r_n$, the coefficients are, up to sign, the elementary symmetric polynomials of the roots; for a quadratic, the roots add to $-\frac ba$ and multiply to $\frac ca$.
See [Chapter 10](10-Roots-and-Coefficients.md#104-vietas-formulas).

## W

**Well-ordering principle**
Every nonempty set of whole numbers has a smallest element. It is the reason proof by induction is valid.
See [Chapter 8](08-Sequences-Series-and-Induction.md#83-proof-by-induction).

**Wilson's theorem**
For a prime $p$, $(p - 1)! \equiv -1 \pmod p$: in $\mathbb{F}_p$ each nonzero element pairs with its inverse, leaving only $1$ and $-1$.
See [Chapter 18](18-Factorization-in-Rings.md#184-gaussian-integers-and-sums-of-two-squares).

**Winding number**
For a closed path in the plane that misses 0, the number of times it goes around 0 counterclockwise. It drives the winding-number proof of the fundamental theorem of algebra.
See [Chapter 12](12-The-Fundamental-Theorem-of-Algebra.md#124-the-winding-number-picture).

## X

**$x$-intercept**
A point where a graph crosses the $x$-axis, found by putting $y = 0$. For the graph of a polynomial these are its real roots.
See [Chapter 3](03-Lines-and-Linear-Systems.md#33-forms-of-a-line).

## Y

**$y$-intercept**
The point where a graph crosses the $y$-axis, found by putting $x = 0$; for $y = mx + b$ it is $(0, b)$.
See [Chapter 3](03-Lines-and-Linear-Systems.md#33-forms-of-a-line).

## Z

**Zero divisor**
A nonzero element $a$ of a commutative ring with $ab = 0$ for some nonzero $b$, such as $2$ and $3$ in $\mathbb{Z}_6$. Integral domains have none.
See [Chapter 17](17-Rings-and-Ideals.md#172-integral-domains-and-fields).

**Zero polynomial**
The polynomial $0$, with no nonzero coefficients. It has no degree (or degree $-\infty$ by convention).
See [Chapter 4](04-Polynomials-and-Factoring.md#41-polynomials).

**Zero product property**
If $ab = 0$, then $a = 0$ or $b = 0$. It is why factoring solves equations, and it is exactly what an integral domain requires.
See [Chapter 1](01-The-Language-of-Algebra.md#13-the-laws-of-arithmetic).

**$\mathbb{Z}_n$ (integers mod $n$)**
The numbers $0, 1, \ldots, n - 1$ with addition and multiplication mod $n$. Under addition it is the cyclic group of order $n$; as a ring it is a field exactly when $n$ is prime.
See [Chapter 13](13-Groups.md#133-examples-everywhere) and [Chapter 17](17-Rings-and-Ideals.md#171-rings-examples).
